Chalmers and the Hard Problem

Work through David Chalmers' case that explaining what the brain does can't explain what experience is like, and the theory he builds instead.

[ ◄ Noah's Weblog | About ]

Start here

This page teaches the view of consciousness in two books by the philosopher David J. Chalmers: The Conscious Mind (1996) and The Character of Consciousness (2010). It is a study guide written in plain language, not a copy of either book. About the source.

Right now you are seeing these words. Scientists can trace what happens. Light reaches your eyes, neurons fire, and you could read the words aloud. But there is another fact here too. Seeing the words feels like something to you.

Chalmers asks why. Why doesn't all that brain activity happen "in the dark," with no inner feel at all? He named this the hard problem of consciousness. When he first presented it in 1994, the reaction was, in his words, "overwhelming and entirely unexpected" (Character, Introduction). The puzzle also bears on a question you may live to see answered: could a machine ever feel anything?

How to read the references: (Conscious Mind, p. 94) points to a page of the 1996 book. (Character, ch. 1 §3) points to a chapter and section of the 2010 book, because the copy used here has no page numbers.

  • Compare the six main views of consciousness (types A to F) and say what each one costs.

1. What it's like

In this part: tell the felt side of the mind apart from what the mind does.

Words to know

  • Consciousness: in Chalmers' sense, subjective experience. A being is conscious if there is something it is like to be that being.
  • Phenomenal: about how a mental state feels from the inside. The felt qualities themselves are often called qualia.
  • Psychological: about what a mental state does, meaning its role in causing behavior.

The most familiar thing, and the strangest

Chalmers calls conscious experience "at once the most familiar thing in the world and the most mysterious" (Conscious Mind, p. 3). He says it can't be defined in simpler terms. The best anyone can do is point at it and clarify (Conscious Mind, pp. 3–4).

To point at it, he borrows a phrase from the philosopher Thomas Nagel. A being is conscious if there is "something it is like" to be that being. A mental state is conscious if there is something it is like to be in that state (Conscious Mind, p. 4). There is something it is like to taste peppermint, or to hear a phone ring.

He fills several pages with examples (Conscious Mind, pp. 6–10). The deep purple of a book cover. The ring of a telephone. The feel of velvet and the smell of fresh bread. The dull throb of an ache. The "red-hot glow" of anger. Even thinking of a lion has its own faint flavor, different from thinking of the Eiffel Tower. A single patch of color, he adds, raises the whole puzzle as fully as a Bach chorale does (Conscious Mind, p. 11).

Two ways to describe a mind

Chalmers separates two concepts of mind (Conscious Mind, p. 11). On the phenomenal concept, a mind is described by how it feels. On the psychological concept, a mind is described by what it does: the inner causes behind behavior. Learning is a clear psychological idea. Something counts as learning if its behavior adapts in the right way, whatever that feels like (Conscious Mind, pp. 15–16).

Many mental words lead a double life (Conscious Mind, p. 17). "Pain" can mean the hurt you feel. It can also mean the state that detects damage and makes you pull away. In daily life the two come together, so we rarely notice the difference. Chalmers argues that mixing them up is harmless in conversation but fatal when you try to explain the mind (Conscious Mind, p. 23).

The word "consciousness" has the same problem. People use it for being awake, paying attention, being able to report something, or thinking about yourself. Each of those is something a brain does (Conscious Mind, pp. 26–27). Chalmers saves the word awareness for having information available to guide behavior, including speech in creatures with language. He keeps "consciousness" for experience itself. In his view, experience always comes with awareness, but awareness might come without experience (Conscious Mind, p. 28).

Analogy (not from the books)On a cold morning, a weather station and a person both register that it is 2 °C. The station records the number, shows it on a screen, and switches on a heater. You do the same sorts of things: you notice, you say "it's cold," you grab a coat. You also feel the cold. That feeling is what Chalmers is asking about. Where the analogy breaks down: Chalmers does not assume simple machines feel nothing. In Part 9 he even wonders whether a thermostat has a very simple experience.

Why? Why can't Chalmers just define consciousness?

Any definition would either use words that already depend on experience, which is circular, or swap in a job the brain does, which changes the subject. Defining consciousness as "being able to report," for example, explains reporting and leaves the feel out. So he points at examples instead (Conscious Mind, pp. 4, 105).

A common mix-up"Conscious just means awake." Being awake is something a brain does: taking in the world and responding. Chalmers notes that it makes sense to suppose we have experiences while asleep (Conscious Mind, p. 26). And you can experience the edges of your visual field without paying attention to them, so experience isn't attention either (Conscious Mind, p. 27).

2. The easy problems and the hard problem

In this part: explain why explaining every job the brain does in seeing still leaves a question open.

Words to know

  • Function: any causal role a system plays in producing behavior. Chalmers uses the word broadly, not just for what something is designed to do (Character, ch. 1 §3).
  • Easy problems: explaining the mind's abilities, such as telling things apart or reporting them.
  • Hard problem: explaining why those abilities come with experience at all.
  • Explanatory gap: the distance between an account of the functions and the experience itself.

The easy problems

Chalmers splits the problems of consciousness into two kinds. The easy problems are about explaining abilities. His list includes (Character, ch. 1 §2):

  • telling things apart, sorting them, and reacting to them
  • bringing information together
  • reporting your mental states
  • accessing your own inner states
  • focusing attention
  • controlling behavior on purpose
  • the difference between being awake and asleep

"Easy" is relative. Chalmers guesses that getting the details right could take "a century or two" of hard lab work (Character, ch. 1 §2). They count as easy because we know what an answer looks like. To explain an ability, you find a mechanism, in neurons or in a computer model, that performs it.

The hard problem

The hard problem is the problem of experience. When you see, there is a whir of information processing. There is also something it is like to see: the felt quality of redness, of dark and light, of depth (Character, ch. 1 §2). Why does the processing come with an inner life at all? Chalmers asks why it doesn't all go on "in the dark," free of any inner feel (Character, ch. 1 §3).

The heart of his case is a test you can run on any explanation. Take genes. Biologists explained how DNA stores hereditary information and passes it on. Suppose someone replied, "Fine, but you haven't explained how it is a gene." That person is confused, because being a gene just means doing that job (Character, ch. 1 §3).

Now try it with seeing. Suppose a theory explains how the brain tells colors apart, combines them, and reports "red." Someone replies, "Fine, but you haven't explained how it is experienced." Chalmers says this person is not confused. They are asking a real further question (Character, ch. 1 §3). He borrows the philosopher Joseph Levine's name for it: the explanatory gap.

Bait and switch

Chalmers warns about a common pattern in writing on consciousness. A paper opens with the mystery of experience, presents a theory of report or attention, then declares victory. The reader is left feeling like "the victim of a bait-and-switch" (Character, ch. 1 §2).

He then tests his point on two leading theories (Character, ch. 1 §4). Francis Crick and Christof Koch proposed that brain waves of 35 to 75 cycles per second are the basis of consciousness, partly because they might bind information together, such as an object's color and shape. Bernard Baars proposed a "global workspace," a kind of shared blackboard that broadcasts information across the brain. Chalmers thinks both could explain real abilities: binding, memory, access, report. Neither says why those abilities feel like anything. He adds that he doesn't think Crick and Koch claim to solve the hard problem; Koch himself set "subjective feelings" aside as maybe lacking a scientific solution.

New ingredients don't help, he argues. Chaos theory, quantum mechanics, or new brain discoveries could explain more functions. But "from dynamics, one only gets more dynamics" (Character, ch. 1 §5). As he puts it, we won't "suddenly discover a phenomenal glow inside a neuron!"

    Why? Why is "but how is it a gene?" a confusion, while "but how is it experienced?" is not?

    "Gene" is defined by a job: storing and passing on hereditary information. Explain the job and you have explained the gene. Chalmers argues that experience isn't defined by any job. "Phenomenal states, unlike psychological states, are not defined by the causal roles that they play" (Conscious Mind, p. 47). So explaining the jobs leaves the experience open.

    A common mix-up"Chalmers says experience is useless." He doesn't. He writes that experience may turn out to play an important role in the mind. His point is that for any role it plays, explaining the role won't by itself explain the experience (Character, ch. 1 §3).

    3. What comes for free

    In this part: use the idea of supervenience to say exactly how consciousness differs from life, tables, and earthquakes.

    Words to know

    • Supervenience: one set of facts fully fixes another. If the A-facts can't stay the same while the B-facts change, the B-facts supervene on the A-facts.
    • Logical supervenience: the fixing holds in every coherently imaginable world.
    • Natural supervenience: the fixing holds only because of the laws of nature in our world.
    • Reductive explanation: explaining something wholly in terms of simpler, lower-level parts.

    God's to-do list

    Chalmers sharpens the hard problem with a tool called supervenience. It captures the idea that one set of facts can fully determine another (Conscious Mind, p. 32).

    He illustrates it with an image borrowed from the philosopher Saul Kripke (Conscious Mind, p. 38). Imagine God creating a world. First God fixes every physical fact: where each particle is, every field, every physical law. Do the other facts then come along "for free"? Or does God have more work to do?

    For nearly everything, they come for free. Any world physically identical to ours contains an identical kangaroo, and that kangaroo is automatically alive (Conscious Mind, p. 35). Tables, earthquakes, and even economic booms are fixed the same way (Conscious Mind, pp. 42, 71–80). Chalmers calls this logical supervenience. "Logical" here means possible in the broadest sense, roughly what can be coherently imagined (Conscious Mind, p. 35).

    Some links are weaker. They hold because of laws of nature that could, in principle, have been different. A gas's pressure depends on its temperature and volume by a law with a constant number in it. A world with a different constant is imaginable, though not possible in ours (Conscious Mind, p. 36). That is natural supervenience.

    Chalmers' claim is that conscious experience is "almost unique" in failing to supervene logically on the physical (Conscious Mind, p. 71). After fixing the physical facts, God "had more work to do" to give the world experience (Conscious Mind, p. 124). But Chalmers does think experience supervenes naturally. In our world, an exact physical copy of you would very likely be conscious too (Conscious Mind, pp. 96, 124).

    This ties back to explanation. A reductive explanation removes the mystery by showing how the higher facts follow from the lower ones (Conscious Mind, p. 48). Chalmers argues it works exactly when logical supervenience holds (Conscious Mind, pp. 47–48). That is why biochemistry can explain life, and why he thinks physics alone can't explain experience.

    Analogy (not from the books)Fix the position of every piece on a chessboard, and the fact that White is in checkmate comes along for free. You can't change it without moving a piece. Whether the players are enjoying the game is not fixed by the board. Where the analogy breaks down: the players' enjoyment depends on other physical things, their brains, which the board leaves out. Chalmers' claim is stronger. Even fixing every physical fact everywhere leaves experience open.

    Isn't this just vitalism again?

    People once doubted that chemistry could ever explain life, and they posited a "vital spirit." They were wrong. Is Chalmers making the same mistake? He argues the cases differ (Character, ch. 1 §5). The vitalists doubted that matter could perform life's functions. Once science showed how, their doubts "melted away." With experience, nobody doubts the functions. The question remains after they're explained. And the vital spirit was a made-up cause, invented to explain those functions. Experience is the thing that needs explaining in the first place.

    Try it: free, or more work?

    God has just fixed every physical fact in a world exactly like ours. Sort each fact.

    Why? Why does the phrase "the mind arises from the brain" already grant Chalmers something?

    Nobody says learning "arises from" the brain; learning simply is a brain process. The word "arises" signals a further fact that the physical produces. Chalmers imagines someone saying, "Of course I'm a materialist; the mind certainly arises from the brain," and points out that the word gives the game away (Conscious Mind, p. 125).

    A common mix-up"If physics doesn't fix experience logically, then the brain doesn't matter." That isn't Chalmers' view. He holds that experience depends on the brain by natural law, so a physical copy of you in our world would share your experience (Conscious Mind, p. 124). The brain matters a great deal. The link just isn't one you can deduce from physics alone.

    4. Zombies, Mary, and the gap

    In this part: lay out Chalmers' three arguments against materialism and the move they share.

    Words to know

    • Materialism (also called physicalism): the view that the physical facts are all the facts, so everything else is fixed by them.
    • Conceivable: able to be coherently imagined, with no contradiction that shows up even on careful reflection.
    • Zombie: in philosophy, a being physically identical to a conscious person but with no experience at all.
    • Epistemic gap and ontological gap: a gap in what we can know or explain, and a gap in what actually exists.

    The zombie argument

    Chalmers imagines his own zombie twin. The twin gazes out at green trees and munches a chocolate bar, just as Chalmers does, atom for atom. But the twin has no experience: "all is dark inside" (Conscious Mind, pp. 94, 96). This is not a movie zombie. It walks, talks, and even says it is conscious (Conscious Mind, pp. 95, 180).

    Chalmers doesn't think zombies exist. He thinks they probably couldn't exist in our world (Conscious Mind, p. 96). His question is whether the idea is conceivable. Compare a mile-high unicycle. Nobody has built one, but there is no contradiction in the idea (Conscious Mind, p. 96). Chalmers finds no contradiction in the zombie either.

    In its simplest form, the argument runs like this (Character, ch. 5 §3):

    1. It is conceivable that there are zombies.
    2. If it is conceivable, it is metaphysically possible: it could have happened, perhaps in a very different universe.
    3. If zombies are metaphysically possible, consciousness is not physical.
    4. So consciousness is not physical.

    A variant uses an inverted twin. Imagine a physical copy of you who sees blood and fire engines the way you see the sea and the sky, while still calling them "red" (Conscious Mind, p. 100). This inverted spectrum case suggests that physics doesn't fix even which experiences you have.

    Mary's room

    The second argument comes from the philosopher Frank Jackson (Character, ch. 5 §3). Mary is a brilliant scientist who knows every physical fact about color vision. But she has spent her whole life in a black-and-white room and has never seen red. When she finally does, it seems she learns something new: what it is like to see red. If so, the physical facts she knew were not all the facts (Conscious Mind, p. 103).

    Some critics reply that Mary gains only a new ability, such as recognizing red. Chalmers argues she learns a genuine fact, because her new knowledge rules out possibilities she couldn't rule out before (Conscious Mind, pp. 144–145).

    The explanatory argument

    The third argument is Part 2 in three lines (Character, ch. 5 §3):

    1. Physical accounts explain at most structure and function.
    2. Explaining structure and function does not suffice to explain consciousness.
    3. So no physical account can explain consciousness.

    The shared move

    All three arguments start with an epistemic gap: a gap in what we can know, imagine, or explain. Then they infer an ontological gap: a gap in what exists (Character, ch. 5 §3). Type-B materialists, the main opponents of the zombie argument, accept the first step and push back on the second, as Part 5 shows.

    Why? Why doesn't it hurt the argument that zombies probably can't exist in our world?

    Materialism says the physical facts fix every fact, with nothing extra needed. To refute that, you only need a possible world where the physical facts hold and experience doesn't. That world doesn't have to obey our laws. If zombies are impossible only because of our laws of nature, that just means our laws link brains to experience, which Chalmers accepts (Conscious Mind, p. 96; Character, ch. 5 §3).

    A common mix-up"The zombie argument says some people around you might be zombies." Chalmers thinks the people around you are conscious. He calls his zombie twin "only a logical possibility, not an empirical one" (Conscious Mind, p. 180). The argument is about what physics does and doesn't settle, not about your neighbors.

    5. Six answers: types A to F

    In this part: place a view on Chalmers' map and say what it costs.

    Words to know

    • Causal closure: the idea that every physical event has a complete physical cause, leaving no gaps for anything else to fill.
    • Epiphenomenalism: the view that experience is real but has no effects on the physical world.
    • Combination problem: the puzzle of how many tiny bits of experience could add up to one unified mind.

    Three materialist replies

    Chalmers sorts the main views into six types, labeled A to F (Character, ch. 5 §1). Types A, B, and C are materialist. Types D, E, and F are not. He doesn't pretend to be neutral: he argues against the first three.

    Type A denies the gap. Once you explain all the functions, there is nothing left to explain. Daniel Dennett is Chalmers' main example (Character, ch. 1 afterword). Chalmers says the view "appears to deny the manifest." He adds that type-A materialists are a minority even among materialists (Character, ch. 5 §4).

    Type B accepts a gap in knowledge but denies a gap in reality. Experience is a brain state, the way water turned out to be H2O. Chalmers replies that you can work out from the physical facts that water is H2O, because water is whatever plays water's role. Nothing like that works for experience. So type B needs an identity that can't be derived from anything, which looks like a fundamental law under another name. Its opponents, he writes, will see the move as "more akin to theft than to honest toil" (Character, ch. 5 §5).

    Type C says the gap is real for us now but closable in principle, whether by future science or only by minds better equipped than ours. Chalmers argues this view is unstable. Physics describes the world in terms of structure and dynamics, and "from truths about structure and dynamics, one can deduce only further truths about structure and dynamics." So type C collapses into one of the other views (Character, ch. 5 §7).

    Three nonmaterialist options

    If materialism fails, something new and fundamental is in the world. It might be experience itself, or "protophenomenal" properties that make it up (Character, ch. 5 §8). The next question is whether physics is causally closed (Character, ch. 5 §8).

    Type D, interactionist dualism, says no: the mind pushes on matter. Chalmers describes a version built on quantum mechanics, where a conscious observation could cause the quantum state to "collapse." He argues physics doesn't rule this out, though many physicists reject that reading (Character, ch. 5 §9).

    Type E, epiphenomenalism, says physics is closed and experience has no physical effects. Your pain would play no role in pulling your hand from a flame. Chalmers calls the view coherent but "inelegant" and counterintuitive (Character, ch. 5 §10).

    Type F, Russellian monism, starts from a point made by Bertrand Russell. Physics tells us what things do, not what they are in themselves. Mass, for example, is described by its tendency to resist acceleration. Perhaps the inner nature behind physics is experience, or something close to it (Character, ch. 5 §11). Chalmers calls the combination problem "easily the most serious problem" for this view.

    Where does Chalmers land? He rejects A, B, and C. He gives "some credence" to each of D, E, and F. He finds F the most appealing, mostly for aesthetic reasons whose force he calls unclear (Character, ch. 5 §12).

    Why? Why does Chalmers think type C has no space of its own?

    To close the gap, future physics would need to imply facts about experience. But physics describes structure and dynamics, and those only imply more structure and dynamics. Experience isn't a matter of structure and dynamics. So a type-C thinker must eventually either deny there's anything more to explain (type A), accept an underivable identity (type B), or add something beyond structure and dynamics (types D or F) (Character, ch. 5 §7).

    A common mix-up"Rejecting materialism means believing in ghosts or souls." Types D and E can be property dualism: one kind of stuff with two kinds of properties (Character, ch. 5 §9–10). Type F can even be read as a kind of materialism, if "physical" names the inner nature of matter. Chalmers suggests it arguably "fits the letter of materialism" while sharing "the spirit of anti-materialism" (Character, ch. 5 §11).

    6. Chalmers' answer: naturalistic dualism

    In this part: describe Chalmers' own view and the strange puzzle it has to face.

    Words to know

    • Fundamental: basic, not explained by anything simpler. Physics treats mass and charge this way.
    • Psychophysical law: a basic law of nature saying how experience depends on physical processes.
    • Naturalistic dualism: Chalmers' name for his view that experience is a fundamental, non-physical property governed by natural laws.

    Experience as a basic ingredient

    If experience can't be explained by anything simpler, Chalmers says, take it as fundamental (Character, ch. 1 §6). Physics already does this. Mass, charge, and space-time aren't explained in terms of anything simpler. Physics takes them as basic and states laws about how they relate.

    There is a historical model. In the 1800s, electromagnetism couldn't be explained by the mechanical processes physics then used. So James Clerk Maxwell and others added electric charge and electromagnetic forces as new basic ingredients (Character, ch. 1 §6). Chalmers proposes doing the same for experience.

    Where there is a fundamental property, there are fundamental laws. Chalmers calls the new ones psychophysical laws. They would say how experience depends on physical processes. They wouldn't override physics, which already looks like a closed system. They would add to it (Character, ch. 1 §6).

    He named the view naturalistic dualism (Conscious Mind, p. 128). It is dualism because experience is a property beyond the ones physics lists. It is naturalistic because the world still runs on basic properties and laws, with nothing supernatural. It is not Descartes' picture of a separate soul-substance. It is a dualism of properties (Conscious Mind, pp. 124–128).

    Does this fail to explain why experience exists at all? In one sense, yes. But Chalmers points out that physics doesn't explain why there is matter either, and nobody counts that against physics (Character, ch. 1 §6). He hopes the basic laws will be simple, the way physicists hope for laws simple enough to "write them on the front of a T-shirt" (Conscious Mind, p. 214).

    Chalmers says he came to this view "quite reluctantly." He describes himself as temperamentally inclined toward materialist explanation, with no strong spiritual or religious leanings (Conscious Mind, p. xiv).

    The paradox of phenomenal judgment

    The view faces a puzzle Chalmers calls "the greatest tension" a theory like his has to face (Conscious Mind, p. 181). Your zombie twin talks about consciousness all the time. He writes books about its mysteries and argues with materialists, yet experiences nothing (Conscious Mind, p. 180). Whatever explains his words also explains yours, since your brains are identical. So experience seems irrelevant to explaining why you say you're conscious (Conscious Mind, p. 177).

    His reply: you don't know your experience through a chain of causes, the way you know about a table. You know it by having it. "It is having the experiences that justifies the beliefs" (Conscious Mind, p. 196). The zombie makes the same sounds, but its claims lack that support. "From the first-person point of view, my zombie twin and I are very different" (Conscious Mind, p. 199). In 2010 he said his later work on how we think about experience gives a better answer to this paradox than the 1996 book did (Character, Introduction).

    A related worry is type E: maybe experience does nothing at all. In 1996 Chalmers granted that his view was "epiphenomenalistic to a first approximation," while leaving the deeper causal question open (Conscious Mind, pp. 156, 160). By 2010 he wrote that he was "somewhat less sympathetic to epiphenomenalism" than before, and that his earlier criticisms of interactionism (type D) "were much too quick" (Character, Introduction).

    Try it: holds or rejects?

    Sort each claim by whether Chalmers holds it or rejects it.

    Why? Why doesn't "you haven't explained why experience exists" sink his theory?

    Every theory takes something as basic. Physics doesn't say why matter exists, yet it explains a huge amount about matter by showing how facts follow from its basic laws. A theory of experience could do the same with its own basic laws (Character, ch. 1 §6).

    A common mix-up"Dualism is anti-scientific." Chalmers writes that "there is nothing antiscientific or supernatural about this view" (Conscious Mind, p. 126). Physics, neuroscience, and cognitive science carry on as usual. The theory adds laws rather than overriding any (Conscious Mind, p. 170).

    7. A science without a consciousness meter

    In this part: explain how researchers study experience through reports, and what correlations can and can't show.

    Words to know

    • Bridging principle: an assumption linking something we can measure, such as a report, to experience, which we can't measure directly.
    • Structural coherence: Chalmers' principle that the structure of experience mirrors the structure of the brain's information processing.
    • Neural correlate of consciousness (NCC): roughly, a minimal brain system whose activity is enough, in normal conditions, for a given conscious state.

    Two kinds of data

    Chalmers thinks a real science of consciousness is possible even before anyone solves the hard problem (Character, ch. 2). It has to connect two kinds of data. Third-person data are about brains and behavior: what people do and say, and which neurons fire. First-person data are about experience itself: colors, sounds, pains, emotions, the flow of thought.

    Neither kind reduces to the other, he argues. So the science will look for correlations: which brain processes go with which experiences, and by what general principles (Character, ch. 2). "We simply need to distinguish correlation from explanation."

    The missing meter

    Imagine a "consciousness meter" you could point at anything to read off what it experiences. Finding the brain basis of consciousness would then be easy. "Alas, we have no consciousness meter," Chalmers writes, and for principled reasons we probably can't have one (Character, ch. 4).

    So researchers lean on assumptions made before any experiment. Chalmers calls them pre-experimental bridging principles (Character, ch. 4). The simplest: if someone reports seeing something, they consciously saw it. Chalmers calls that one "relatively safe." A broader one: if information is directly available for global control of behavior, it is conscious. Chalmers calls this "at best a first-order approximation" (Character, ch. 4).

    This connects to Part 1. There, awareness was a broad idea that might come without experience. In The Conscious Mind, Chalmers narrows awareness to "direct availability for global control," so that it lines up with experience in both directions. Then he treats the match as a working rule (Conscious Mind, pp. 222–225).

    Structure mirrors structure

    Chalmers proposes a further link, the principle of structural coherence (Character, ch. 1 §7). The structure of experience is mirrored in the structure of processing. Take color. The colors you experience form a three-dimensional space, varying in hue, saturation, and intensity. The visual system also analyzes light along three axes. So which colors look alike to you matches which signals are alike in the brain's coding.

    The principle has a limit. It captures structure, such as which colors look similar, but not what red is like in itself. An inverted spectrum would keep all the structure while swapping the qualities (Character, ch. 1 §7).

    Hunting for the neural correlate

    Much of the science searches for neural correlates of consciousness. A famous line of evidence comes from Nikos Logothetis's experiments with monkeys (Character, ch. 3). Each eye sees a different pattern: horizontal stripes for one, vertical for the other. In humans, this makes experience flip back and forth between the two. Trained monkeys press bars to signal which pattern they see, and their presses flip too.

    In the primary visual cortex, called V1, many cells for both patterns keep firing. They mostly track what reaches the eyes. In a later area, the inferior temporal cortex (IT), almost all of the relevant cells follow the report: cells for the reported pattern fire, the others nearly fall silent, and they switch when the report switches (Character, ch. 3). So IT is a better candidate for an NCC than V1. Chalmers calls this evidence "at least suggestive," not conclusive.

    Why? Why can't even a perfect map of neural correlates solve the hard problem?

    The method assumes the bridge from report or availability to experience, so it can't explain that bridge. It finds correlations, and correlation isn't explanation. In Chalmers' words: "So the hard problem remains" (Character, ch. 4).

    A common mix-up"A brain scan shows your experience." A scan shows brain activity. Experience is inferred from reports, using a bridging principle that the scan can't test (Character, ch. 4). Chalmers adds that a candidate correlate can't serve as a stand-alone meter: 40-hertz brain waves "in a test tube" would almost certainly not be conscious (Character, ch. 4).

    8. Swap your neurons: fading and dancing qualia

    In this part: follow Chalmers' neuron-replacement thought experiments and say what they imply for machines.

    Words to know

    • Functional organization: the abstract pattern of cause and effect among a system's parts, whatever the parts are made of.
    • Organizational invariance: Chalmers' principle that systems with the same fine-grained functional organization have the same experiences.

    Does the material matter?

    Which physical features does experience depend on? Chalmers' answer is the brain's functional organization, described finely enough to fix its behavior (Conscious Mind, pp. 247–248). The stuff it's made of doesn't matter. His principle of organizational invariance says that a system with the same fine-grained organization as a conscious system will have "qualitatively identical experiences" (Conscious Mind, p. 249). The system could be built from silicon chips, from the population of China, or from "beer cans and ping-pong balls."

    The philosopher John Searle disagreed. He held that consciousness is tied to specific biology, so a silicon copy of a brain need not be conscious (Character, ch. 1 §7). Chalmers answers with two thought experiments.

    Fading qualia

    Replace your neurons one by one with silicon chips that do exactly the same job: same inputs, same outputs, same connections (Conscious Mind, p. 254). Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the all-silicon version at the end has no experience. Then somewhere along the way, experience had to go (Conscious Mind, pp. 253–255).

    Could it vanish all at once? Then swapping a single neuron would wipe out an entire field of experience. Chalmers finds this "extremely implausible, if not entirely bizarre." Any cutoff point would be arbitrary (Conscious Mind, p. 255).

    Could it fade gradually? Then picture Joe, a case partway along, at a basketball game. He exclaims about the "glaring bright red-and-yellow uniforms." But he is actually experiencing tepid pink and murky brown (Conscious Mind, p. 256). His brain's organization hasn't changed, so nothing in him can notice. He is "systematically wrong about everything that he is experiencing." Chalmers thinks it far more reasonable that experience doesn't fade at all (Conscious Mind, pp. 256–259).

    Dancing qualia

    Now suppose the silicon version sees blue where you see red. Somewhere in the series, two stages that differ by no more than a tenth of the brain must have noticeably different experiences. Install the silicon version of that small circuit beside your neural one, with a switch (Conscious Mind, pp. 267–268).

    Flip the switch and, by hypothesis, your experience changes from red to blue. Flip it back and red returns. Yet your brain's organization stays exactly the same, so there is no room for the thought "Hmm! Something strange just happened!" (Character, ch. 1 §7). Your experiences would dance before your eyes while you noticed nothing. Chalmers rejects this, because a person paying attention can notice when their experience changes significantly. He finds this argument "even more convincing" than fading qualia (Conscious Mind, p. 270).

    What follows for machines

    Chalmers concludes that a system with your brain's fine-grained organization would have your experiences, whatever it is made of (Conscious Mind, pp. 270, 275). He calls his position nonreductive functionalism. Organization determines experience, but experience isn't reducible to organization (Conscious Mind, p. 275). The same reasoning supports the view that the right computation, run on any hardware, could give rise to a conscious mind (Conscious Mind, pp. 314, 321). "The outlook for machine consciousness is good in principle, if not yet in practice" (Conscious Mind, p. 331).

    He is careful about how strong these claims are. They are plausibility arguments, not proofs (Conscious Mind, p. 250). They aim to show that fading and dancing qualia are impossible given the laws of our world, not impossible in every imaginable world (Conscious Mind, pp. 274–275). And exactly which computations are enough remains an open question (Conscious Mind, p. 332).

    Why? If the hypothesis were true, why would the switch flip go unnoticed?

    Noticing is itself a brain process: a new belief, a startle, a shift of attention. But the two circuits are functionally identical, so every brain process continues exactly as before. Any noticing would have to "float entirely free of the system" and could have no effect on anything Joe does or thinks (Character, ch. 1 §7).

    A common mix-up"Chalmers proved that computers are conscious." His claim is conditional: a system with the same fine-grained organization as a conscious brain would be conscious. He doesn't say which machines, if any, have that organization, and he calls the question of which computations suffice open (Conscious Mind, p. 332). Both books were written before today's AI chatbots existed.

    Check yourself

    9. Information with an inside

    In this part: state Chalmers' most speculative idea and weigh what it would cost.

    Words to know

    • Information: in the engineer Claude Shannon's sense, a state picked out from a space of possible states, whatever it means.
    • Double-aspect principle: Chalmers' guess that information has two sides, one physical and one experiential.
    • Panpsychism: the view that experience is found everywhere in nature, even in very simple systems.

    Looking for the basic law

    Structural coherence and organizational invariance are high-level patterns. Chalmers wants something more basic, a candidate for the fundamental law itself (Conscious Mind, p. 276; Character, ch. 1 §7). His candidate centers on information.

    He means information in Shannon's sense: a state counts as information by being one option out of a space of possibilities, whatever it means (Conscious Mind, pp. 277–280). Physically, information is "a difference that makes a difference," a phrase he borrows from Gregory Bateson (Conscious Mind, p. 281). A light switch can sit in countless slightly different positions, but only up and down make a difference to the light. A scratch on a CD makes no difference to the music. The pits that encode the music do (Conscious Mind, pp. 281–282).

    Two aspects

    Chalmers noticed that the same information space shows up twice. Your color experiences form a three-dimensional space of differences, and the brain's color coding forms a matching three-dimensional space (Conscious Mind, pp. 284–285). His hypothesis is the double-aspect principle: information has two aspects, a physical one and a phenomenal one (Conscious Mind, p. 286; Character, ch. 1 §7). In his words: "Experience is information from the inside; physics is information from the outside" (Conscious Mind, p. 305).

    A thermostat's experience?

    If all information has an experiential side, experience is wherever information is. Take a thermostat with three states. It would have three very simple experiences. "Certainly it will not be very interesting to be a thermostat," Chalmers writes (Conscious Mind, pp. 293–294). It wouldn't think, be intelligent, or be self-conscious (Conscious Mind, p. 295).

    He offers reasons this isn't as strange as it sounds. A fundamental property would be expected to be spread widely, as most fundamental properties are (Character, ch. 1 §7). And if experience switched on only above some level of complexity, it would have to "wink in" somewhere, oddly, between one system and a slightly bigger one (Conscious Mind, p. 297). He grants it is "probably fair to say that the view is a variety of panpsychism" (Conscious Mind, p. 299). Even so, he says he is "much more confident of naturalistic dualism" than of panpsychism.

    How sure is he?

    Not very, by his own account. He calls these ideas the ones "most likely to be entirely wrong" in the book (Conscious Mind, p. 277). He thinks it "more likely than not that the key to a fundamental theory will lie elsewhere" (Conscious Mind, p. 310). By 2010 he wrote that even he was inclined to be skeptical of the double-aspect theory (Character, Introduction).

    The idea also runs into the combination problem from Part 5. If tiny physical systems have tiny experiences, how do they combine into one rich, unified mind? Why not "a disunified, jagged collection of phenomenal spikes" (Character, ch. 5 §11)? Chalmers leaves this open (Conscious Mind, p. 307).

    Where this leaves us

    Chalmers' project is to take experience seriously and still build a theory, with laws, the way physics would. He ends his best-known essay this way: "The hard problem is a hard problem, but there is no reason to believe that it will remain permanently unsolved" (Character, ch. 1 §8).

    Why? Why does Chalmers think widespread experience is less odd than it sounds?

    Fundamental properties like mass and charge are spread throughout nature, not switched on only in special cases. If experience is fundamental, you'd expect the same. And any line where experience suddenly switches on would be arbitrary, like saying 1,000 elements feel nothing but 1,001 do (Conscious Mind, p. 297; Character, ch. 1 §7).

    A common mix-up"Panpsychism means rocks think." Chalmers says a thermostat would not think at all. And a rock as a whole isn't an information processor, so at most it contains simple systems with simple experiences (Conscious Mind, pp. 295–298).

    About the source

    David J. Chalmers, The Conscious Mind: In Search of a Fundamental Theory (New York: Oxford University Press, 1996). The book's page on Chalmers' website.

    David J. Chalmers, The Character of Consciousness (New York: Oxford University Press, 2010). The book's page on Chalmers' website. Two chapters used heavily here began as papers that Chalmers posts free on his site: "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (1995), which became chapter 1, and "Consciousness and Its Place in Nature" (2003), which became chapter 5.

    What this guide covers

    From The Conscious Mind: the Introduction and chapters 1 to 9, with the starred technical sections only summarized. From The Character of Consciousness: the Introduction and chapters 1 to 5.

    What it leaves out

    • The technical machinery behind the arguments: two-dimensional semantics and a posteriori necessity (Conscious Mind ch. 2 §4 and ch. 4 §2; Character ch. 6–7 and the appendix).
    • Most of Conscious Mind ch. 5 (memory, reference, and the content of beliefs about experience), the formal account of computation in ch. 9, and ch. 10 on quantum mechanics.
    • Character chapters 8 to 14: phenomenal concepts, perception and "the fall from Eden," the Matrix, and the unity of consciousness (with Tim Bayne).

    What it simplifies

    • The books distinguish carefully between logical, metaphysical, and natural possibility. This guide often says "could have existed" or "imaginable" instead.
    • Chalmers' definition of a neural correlate of consciousness has more conditions than the rough version here. The rivalry activity is a schematic, not real data.
    • In the explanation machine, the mechanisms for genes and life are simplified textbook biology. The analogies marked "not from the books" are the guide's own.
    • Opponents such as Daniel Dennett, Patricia Churchland, and John Searle appear as Chalmers describes them. They would state their views differently.
    • Chalmers has kept writing since 2010. This guide covers only these two books.
