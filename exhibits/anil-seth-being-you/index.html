This page teaches Being You: A New Science of Consciousness by Anil Seth (2021). It is a study guide written in plain language, not a copy of the original. About the source.

Right now you are having an experience: these words, some sound in the room, the weight of your own body. Anil Seth, a neuroscientist at the University of Sussex, argues that all of it is your brain's best guess about what is out there. He goes further. Your sense of being you, he says, is a guess of the same kind, and it starts with the job of keeping your body alive.

The book has four sections: level (how conscious you are), content (what you are conscious of), self, and other minds. This page starts with content and self, because that is where Seth's main idea lives, and moves his chapters on measuring consciousness near the end.

1. The real problem

In this part: what Seth means by consciousness, and three ways of framing the scientific puzzle about it.

Words to knowConsciousness: any kind of subjective experience at all. Phenomenology: what an experience is like from the inside. Hard problem: why physical processes come with experience at all.

Seth borrows his definition from the philosopher Thomas Nagel. A creature is conscious if there is something it is like to be that creature. By this test you are conscious, and Seth thinks animals such as sheep and dolphins probably are too. A blade of grass or a toy robot almost certainly is not. (ch. 1)

This definition is about experience itself, which philosophers call phenomenology. It leaves aside what consciousness does and how a creature behaves. Seth says that is on purpose. Dreamers and fully paralysed people have experiences without outward behaviour, and babies have them without language. (ch. 1)

In the 1990s the philosopher David Chalmers split the puzzle in two. The easy problems are about what brains do: sorting sensory signals, paying attention, producing speech. They are hard in practice, but there is a clear recipe for solving them. The hard problem asks why any of this processing comes with experience at all. Chalmers argued that explaining every function would leave that question untouched. (ch. 1)

Thinkers have answered with a family of positions, or "isms". Seth's verdicts are in the right-hand column. (ch. 1)

PositionThe claimSeth's verdict
PhysicalismThe universe is physical stuff, and experience depends on how that stuff is arranged.His working position.
DualismMind and matter are two separate kinds of thing.Appealing because experience seems non-physical, but how things seem is a poor guide to how they are.
IdealismMind comes first, and matter somehow comes from mind.The opposite extreme from physicalism.
FunctionalismOnly what a system does matters, never what it is made of.Agnostic and a little suspicious. A computer simulation of a storm never gets anything wet.
PanpsychismConsciousness is a basic property found throughout the universe.Explains nothing and leads to no testable predictions.
MysterianismHuman brains may be too limited ever to understand consciousness.Too pessimistic; science keeps solving puzzles that once seemed hopeless.

One famous argument against physicalism uses a philosophical zombie: a being that looks and acts exactly like you but has no experience. If you can imagine one, the argument goes, consciousness can't be physical. Seth replies that what you can imagine is a fact about you. Anyone can picture a jumbo jet flying backwards, yet the more you know about aerodynamics, the less possible it becomes. A brain of 86 billion neurons is far harder to picture than a jet. (ch. 1)

Seth's own approach is the real problem. Accept that experience exists. Then try to explain, predict, and control its properties in terms of what happens in the brain and body. Take seeing red. Redness seems to belong to surfaces, comes in different strengths, and is clearly unlike blue, or jealousy. The real problem asks which brain mechanisms make redness have those properties. (ch. 1)

For decades the main method has been to hunt for neural correlates of consciousness: the smallest set of brain processes that, together, are enough for a particular experience. In binocular rivalry, one eye sees a face and the other sees a house. Experience flips between the two while the input stays the same, so brain activity that flips too is tied to the experience. Seth sees two limits. That activity might reflect attention or reporting instead of the experience, and a correlation is still short of an explanation. (ch. 1)

An analogy Seth usesA century or two ago, life looked as puzzling as consciousness looks today. Many scientists thought living things needed a special spark, a view called vitalism. Biologists made progress by explaining reproduction, metabolism, growth, and repair one at a time, and the need for a spark faded. Seth hopes consciousness will go the same way. Where the analogy breaks: life's properties can be observed from outside, while experience exists only in the first person, so its data are harder to collect. (ch. 1)

To break the problem into pieces, Seth uses three headings: conscious level (how conscious you are), conscious content (what you are conscious of), and the conscious self (the experience of being you). (ch. 1)

Why? If the real problem sets the hard problem aside, how could it ever help with it?

Seth thinks it goes after the hard problem indirectly. Each explanation that links a mechanism to a property of experience narrows the gap. Once we can explain, predict, and control why red looks like this and not like blue, the sense that experience can never be explained physically may fade, the way vitalism did. (ch. 1)

A common mix-up"To be conscious you have to be smart, or able to talk." Seth defines consciousness by experience alone. A creature that exists for a moment and feels only a flash of pain counts. Complex abstract thinking is one small, possibly mainly human, part of being conscious, and language isn't required at all. (ch. 1)

2. Perceiving from the inside out

In this part: why Seth calls perception a controlled hallucination, and what happens when the control slips.

Words to knowPrediction error: the difference between the signals the brain expects and the signals it gets. Controlled hallucination: Seth's name for perception, a brain-made guess kept in check by the senses.

Imagine being a brain. You are sealed inside a dark, silent skull, and all you receive is a stream of electrical signals. They don't arrive labelled "from a coffee cup", or even "from the eyes". From this stream you have to build a world full of objects, people, and places. (ch. 4)

The common-sense picture runs from the outside in. Light enters the eyes, early brain areas detect edges, later areas detect faces and cups, and a self at the end reads off the result. Seth calls this the "how things seem" view. Brain anatomy and some machine vision systems appear to fit it. (ch. 4)

Seth turns the picture around, and he uses a story about the philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein to explain how. Wittgenstein asked a friend why people once found it natural to think the sun went round the Earth. Because it looked that way, she said. And what would it have looked like if the Earth turned instead? Exactly the same. Seth's point: perception can look just as it always has while the explanation of it flips. (ch. 4)

The flipped view is old. Plato pictured prisoners who took shadows on a cave wall for reality. Around the year 1030 the Arab scholar Ibn al-Haytham argued that seeing involves judgement and inference. In the 1800s Hermann von Helmholtz called it unconscious inference. (ch. 4)

Seth's version has three ingredients. (ch. 4)

  1. The brain keeps predicting the causes of its sensory signals. These predictions flow from the top down.
  2. Sensory signals flow from the bottom up as prediction errors. The brain adjusts its predictions to shrink those errors.
  3. What you experience is the content of the predictions. You never experience raw sensory signals, only the brain's interpretation of them.

Seth uses a phrase he first heard from the psychologist Chris Frith: perception is a controlled hallucination. The control comes from the senses, which keep the guesses tied to the world. A hallucination, in turn, is uncontrolled perception: the same machinery, with predictions that prediction errors no longer properly correct. Normal perception and hallucination differ by degree, the way day shades into night. (ch. 4)

Colour shows the principle at work. A sheet of white paper reflects very different light under a yellowish bulb and in bluish daylight, yet it looks white in both. Your brain "discounts the illuminant". It infers a stable property, the way the paper reflects light, and whiteness is how that inference shows up in experience. So redness is not sitting in a red chair, and there is no red patch inside your head either. Redness depends on both the surface and your brain. (ch. 4)

In 2015 a photo called The Dress split the internet. To some people the dress looked blue and black; to others, white and gold, and each side was certain. Seth's own lab split about half and half. The leading explanation is that brains assume different lighting for an overexposed photo with little context. (ch. 4)

Expectations can change experience in seconds. A two-tone image, a photo reduced to black and white blobs, looks like nonsense until you see the original. Then the blobs snap into a scene, though the light reaching your eyes is exactly the same. (ch. 4)

Two objections come up often. First: "If it's all hallucination, go jump in front of a train." Seth's answer is that the control matters as much as the hallucination, and trains are real. He uses John Locke's distinction: primary qualities such as solidity and motion exist with no observer, while secondary qualities such as colour depend on one. Second: "How could you ever see something new?" A first gorilla is built from predictions you already have at many levels: edges, fur, limbs, the way animals move. (ch. 4)

Why does Seth say perception and hallucination differ only by degree?

Both run on the same machinery: top-down predictions about the causes of sensory signals. In everyday perception, prediction errors keep correcting those predictions, so they stay tied to the world. In hallucination the corrections lose their grip and the predictions run on their own. You saw this on the dial when fog and confidence rose together. (ch. 4)

A common mix-up"The senses are windows, and the brain reads what comes through them." Seth argues that the content of experience comes from the brain's predictions. The senses keep those predictions honest. (ch. 4)

3. Betting on causes: Bayes and prediction error

In this part: how a best guess combines expectations with evidence, and where attention and action come in.

Words to knowPrior: how likely something seemed before the new evidence. Likelihood: how well a possible cause would produce the evidence. Posterior: the updated belief. Precision: how reliable a belief or a signal is taken to be.

Thomas Bayes, an English minister who died in 1761, left behind a rule for reasoning under uncertainty. It formalises abduction, reasoning backward from effects to their most likely causes. Deduction is guaranteed: if Jim is older than Jane and Jane is older than Joe, Jim is older than Joe. Induction extrapolates from past cases. Abduction picks the best explanation, and it can be wrong. (ch. 5)

Seth's example: you look out one morning and your lawn is wet. Did it rain, or did you leave the sprinkler on? Your prior is how likely each cause seemed before you looked. The likelihood is how well each cause would produce a wet lawn. Bayes' rule multiplies the two to get the posterior, your updated belief. Suppose you live in Las Vegas, where overnight rain is rare: the sprinkler wins. Then you notice your neighbour's lawn is wet too. Rain would wet both lawns, your sprinkler only yours, so you change your mind. (ch. 5)

A worked example. Seth gives no numbers; these are invented to show the arithmetic.

Rain0.20.90.180.18 ÷ 0.22 = 0.82

The last step divides by the total (0.18 + 0.04 = 0.22) so the posteriors add up to one. Notice that rain started as the underdog and ended as the favourite. (ch. 5)

Reliability matters too. If your window is filthy and you have lost your glasses, the glimpse of the neighbour's lawn should barely count, and the sprinkler may stay in the lead. And the process rolls on: today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior. (ch. 5)

Seth argues the brain works with beliefs shaped like curves. The peak of a curve is what the brain is guessing. The width shows how sure it is: a narrow curve has high precision. Combining a prior curve with an evidence curve gives a posterior curve, and the posterior lands closer to whichever of the two is more precise. (ch. 5)

Brains could carry out something close to Bayes' rule by minimising prediction error. Seth names three parts. A generative model produces the signals a possible cause would create. Predictions run down a hierarchy, from "gorilla" to limbs and fur to edges and patches of light. And precision weighting sets how much each prediction error counts. (ch. 5)

Turning up the precision of some signals is what we call paying attention. That explains a famous video by the psychologist Daniel Simons (1999). Viewers count passes between players in white. Most never notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the middle of the game. Their attention gave the white players' signals high weight, so the gorilla's signals barely changed the guess. Seth once cited this inattentional blindness in a California traffic court, arguing he had not seen a new "no left turn" sign. He lost. (ch. 5)

Action fits into the same picture. A prediction error can shrink in two ways: change the prediction, or act to change the incoming signals. Karl Friston calls the second active inference. Searching your desk for your car keys is hunting for the sensory data that make "keys" come true. Seth goes further: a movement is a self-fulfilling prediction about how your body will feel. To touch your nose with your eyes shut, the brain predicts the feeling of finger on nose and turns down the errors that say the finger isn't there yet. (ch. 5)

Why would a brain weigh its signals instead of simply believing its senses?

Sensory signals are noisy, and how reliable they are keeps changing with fog, distance, and darkness. Weighting each signal by its reliability lets the brain lean on what it already knows when the input is poor, and on the input when it is good. That is exactly what Bayes' rule does with precise and imprecise curves. (ch. 5)

A common mix-up"Prediction means guessing the future." In this theory a prediction is any use of a model to go beyond the data. It can be about the future, or about what is happening right now that the signals don't fully reveal. (ch. 5)

4. The beholder's share

In this part: experiments showing that expectations shape what you see, and how objects, change, time, and even realness are part of the guess.

Words to knowBeholder's share: the part of an experience that the perceiver supplies. Pareidolia: seeing patterns such as faces in things like clouds. Objecthood: the feeling that something is a solid thing out there, with sides you can't see.

In Vienna around 1900, the art historian Alois Riegl named the beholder's share, and Ernst Gombrich later made the idea famous: part of what you see in a painting comes from you. Up close, Camille Pissarro's Hoarfrost at Ennery (1873) is dabs of paint. Step back and you project a frosted field into them. Seth reads the Impressionist painters as early experimenters on predictive perception. (ch. 6)

Which square looks lighter?

Seth's lab tested expectations directly. Yair Pinto showed one eye a flickering pattern that faded out while the other eye's picture, a face or a house, slowly faded in. Before each trial a word, "face" or "house", set up an expectation that was right 70 per cent of the time. People saw the picture they expected sooner and more accurately: about a tenth of a second sooner. (ch. 6)

A team in the Netherlands used letters. A single letter such as U is easier to read inside the word HOUSE than inside a string like AEUVR. Brain scans showed the letter's signal in early visual cortex was sharper when it sat in a real word. Expectations reach right down to early stages of seeing. (ch. 6)

Most of us see faces in clouds, toast, or a bathroom sink. That is pareidolia, and it comes from strong built-in expectations about faces. Seth's lab built a "hallucination machine" to push this further: panoramic video, viewed in a headset, run through a neural network made to find dogs everywhere. Fixing different layers of the network gave different hallucinations: whole dogs, dog parts such as eyes and ears, or simple geometric patterns. (ch. 6)

Predictions also shape the deep structure of experience. Hold a tomato and you experience it as having a back you can't see; a photo of a tomato has none, and neither does the clear blue sky. Seth's explanation for this objecthood is that the brain makes many predictions about how the signals would change if you acted, such as turning the tomato round. Fewer predictions, less objecthood. A retinal afterimage, which moves with your gaze, feels like a glitch instead of a thing. In virtual reality, made-up objects that rotated as real objects do broke into awareness faster than objects that rotated at random. (ch. 6)

Change and time are inferences too. A still picture can seem to move, and slow real changes can go unnoticed, a failure called change blindness. Seth's group finds no evidence that the brain needs a clock to sense duration. Warrick Roseboom's team gave a neural network videos and had it estimate how long each lasted from how much its activity changed. Its estimates matched people's, biases included: busy city scenes seemed longer than quiet ones of the same length. A brain-scanning study then found that activity in visual areas predicted how long each video seemed. (ch. 6)

Even the sense that things are real is part of the guess. In Seth's "substitutional reality" set-up, volunteers wear a headset that shows the lab through a camera. When the live feed is secretly swapped for a recording, most people keep experiencing the scene as real. Seth thinks we experience our predictions as real things because that helps us act quickly. (ch. 6)

This matters for the hard problem. The feeling that experience could never be physical draws strength from the feeling that experience is direct contact with reality. If "seeming real" is itself explained as part of perceptual inference, Seth argues, the hard problem looks less hard. (ch. 6)

Why might it help to experience your best guesses as reality instead of as guesses?

Perception exists to guide action and keep you alive. If a bear seemed like "a hypothesis about a bear", you might hesitate. Experiencing your best guesses as solid, real things lets you respond quickly. Seth says we perceive the world as it is useful for us, and an experience of "out-there-ness" is part of that usefulness. (ch. 6)

A common mix-up"The brain keeps time with an inner clock." Many time researchers still assume one, Seth notes, but his team's work found no need for it. A model that tracks how much perception changes reproduced people's sense of duration, including their errors. Experiences of time look like best guesses, much like experiences of colour. (ch. 6)

5. The self is a perception too

In this part: the layers of the self, how each can come apart, and why the self feels so steady.

Words to knowBody ownership: the experience that this body is yours. Narrative self: the self with a name, a remembered past, and plans for the future.

In the summer of 2018 Seth's mother, then 83, was in hospital with a bowel problem when she developed delirium. She saw people crawling up the walls and became convinced that her son was running a cruel experiment on her. Up to a third of older patients in acute care develop this kind of hospital-induced delirium. Watching her slowly return to herself, Seth asked what a self must be, if it can be lost and found again. (ch. 7)

His answer: the self is not the thing that does the perceiving. It is another perception, a bundle of best guesses that evolution shaped to keep you alive. (ch. 8)

A thought experiment shows how odd selves are. In the future, a machine scans Eva in London down to every molecule, builds an exact copy on Mars, and vaporises the original. One day the vaporiser fails, and there are two Evas. Which one is real? Seth's answer, which he admits is strange: both. From that moment their experiences differ, and the two Evas slowly become different people. (ch. 8)

Seth breaks the human self into layers that usually feel like one thing. (ch. 8)

LayerWhat it isExamples in the book
EmbodiedOwning this body; emotions and moods; underneath, a plain feeling of being aliveDisrupted in phantom limbs and in somatoparaphrenia, where a limb feels like someone else's
PerspectivalSeeing from a point of view, usually just behind the eyesDisrupted in out-of-body experiences

Body ownership can shift in minutes. In the rubber hand illusion, first described in 1998, your real hand is hidden behind a screen and a rubber hand sits in front of you. Someone strokes both with paintbrushes. When the strokes are in time, many people feel that the rubber hand is somehow theirs; when they are out of time, the feeling doesn't come. Seth's explanation: the brain expects signals that arrive together to share a cause, so its best guess is that the rubber hand is somehow part of your body. (ch. 8)

Virtual reality can move the whole body. In one of two studies published in 2007, people saw a virtual image of their own back two metres ahead, being stroked in time with their real back. Many felt the virtual body was partly theirs and, asked to walk to where they felt they were, drifted toward it. Brain surgery has produced stronger effects. In the 1940s Wilder Penfield stimulated one patient's temporal lobe and she said she felt half here and half not here. One of Olaf Blanke's patients, stimulated near the same region, described seeing their own body lying in bed from above. (ch. 8)

Seth adds a caution about the illusions. His lab, with Peter Lush and Zoltán Dienes, found that how strongly people feel the rubber hand illusion tracks how easily they can be hypnotised. The set-up tells people what they are supposed to feel, and that expectation may drive much of the effect. Clinical cases such as phantom limbs and seizure-related out-of-body experiences are stronger evidence that the bodily self is built by the brain. (ch. 8)

The narrative self can be destroyed while the rest stays. In 1985 a brain infection damaged the musician Clive Wearing's hippocampus on both sides. Since then he has lived in a present of roughly 7 to 30 seconds. His diary fills with entries announcing that he has just woken for the first time, each earlier one crossed out. Yet his body ownership and point of view are intact, his love for his wife Deborah is undiminished, and at the piano he plays as fluently as ever. (ch. 8)

The social self is how you perceive others perceiving you. Your brain's model of a friend's mind includes a model of how she sees you. Seth suggests full self-awareness may even need other minds to bounce off. (ch. 8)

Perceptions of the world come and go, while the self feels like a steady centre. Seth argues you do change, slowly, but you don't perceive the change. His reason: we perceive ourselves in order to control ourselves, and knowing ourselves accurately comes second. Part 7 picks this thread up. (ch. 8)

Why does Seth say both Evas are the real Eva?

If the self is a bundle of perceptions and memories, there is no extra hidden ingredient that only one Eva can hold. At the moment of copying the two bundles are identical, so neither has a better claim. Afterwards each Eva has different experiences and lays down different memories, so they become two people, a bit like you now and you ten years from now. (ch. 8)

A common mix-up"The self is one indivisible thing behind the eyes, steering the body like a pilot." The layers can break one at a time: Clive Wearing lost his narrative self and kept his bodily self. Seth takes that as evidence that the self is a set of perceptions working together. (ch. 8)

6. Emotions are guesses from the inside

In this part: how Seth explains emotions and moods as the brain's best guesses about the state of the body.

Words to knowInteroception: sensing the body's internal condition. Exteroception: sensing the outside world. Interoceptive inference: best guessing about the causes of signals from inside the body.

Sight, hearing, touch, taste, and smell sense the outside world. Interoception senses the inside: heartbeat, blood pressure, blood chemistry, how tense the gut is, how breathing is going. These signals travel through the brainstem and thalamus to the insular cortex. Together they report how well the body is being kept alive. A third kind, proprioception, tracks where your limbs are and how they move. (ch. 9)

In 1884 William James, and separately Carl Lange, turned the common view of emotion upside down. We don't tremble because we are afraid, James argued; the perception of trembling, racing heart, and the rest is the fear. Critics answered that bodily states seem too alike to explain the full range of emotions. Appraisal theories, from the 1960s, added a second step: the brain reads the body, then a higher thinking process judges the situation and labels the feeling. (ch. 9)

Seth's own proposal, which took shape around 2010 as he learned about interoception from his Sussex colleague Hugo Critchley, is interoceptive inference. The brain has no direct access to the body's interior either; it has to infer the hidden causes of interoceptive signals, just as it does for light and sound. Emotions and moods are the content of those predictions. One process, working at many scales, replaces the two steps of appraisal theory, and there is no need for a sharp line between "reading the body" and "judging the situation". (ch. 9)

The evidence so far is indirect, Seth says, partly because inner signals are hard to measure and control. One study found that the brain's response to each heartbeat changes when people pay attention to it, as interoceptive inference predicts. In his own lab, a virtual hand that flashed in time with a person's heartbeat felt more like their own than one that flashed out of time. More research is needed. (ch. 9)

Why does Seth prefer one process to two?

A single process of best guessing is simpler than a reading step followed by a judging step. It also fits the brain better, which does not divide neatly into "thinking" and "non-thinking" parts. And it explains the range of emotions without needing a separate body state for each one, since context can shape the guess at every level. (ch. 9)

A common mix-up"Fear comes first and makes your heart race." It seems that way, but James argued the order runs the other way, and Seth goes further. On his view, fear is the brain's best guess about what is happening in and to the body, made while the heart is racing. (ch. 9)

7. Being a beast machine

In this part: why Seth roots all experience in the work of staying alive, and what the free energy principle adds.

Words to knowEssential variable: a body quantity, such as temperature or blood sugar, that must stay in a narrow range for life to go on. Allostasis: keeping things stable by changing ahead of need.

In the 1600s René Descartes called animals beast machines: bodies that worked like clockwork, without the rational soul that only humans had. A century later Julien Offray de La Mettrie said humans were machines too and treated the mind as a property of living bodies, which forced him to flee to Berlin. Seth sides with La Mettrie and turns Descartes upside down: we are conscious because we are beast machines. (ch. 9)

His argument goes through cybernetics, which Norbert Wiener described in 1948 as the science of "control and communication in the animal and the machine". One of its results, the Good Regulator Theorem (1970), says that every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system. Seth's example is home heating. System A switches the heat on when the house is cold and off when it's warm. System B has a model of the house and the weather, so it can predict how the temperature will respond and act ahead of time. (ch. 9)

W. Ross Ashby, one of the cybernetics pioneers, called the quantities a living body must keep in bounds essential variables: core temperature, oxygen, blood sugar, and others. Seth's proposal is that emotions and moods are perceptions aimed at control. Their job is to keep the essential variables where they belong, through outward actions such as running from a bear and inner ones such as raising the heart rate. (ch. 9)

Perception aimed at control feels different from perception aimed at finding out. A good cricket fielder doesn't calculate where the ball will land. They keep moving so that the angle of their gaze to the ball rises at a steadily slowing rate, and that strategy brings them to the ball. Their experience is of the ball's catchability more than its exact position. In the same way, fear has no shape you could walk around. Emotions present how well things are going for the body and how well they are likely to go. (ch. 9)

Living bodies do this ahead of time. Allostasis means stability through change: your blood pressure rises as you stand up so that you don't faint. If the body waited for, say, its blood to turn too acidic before responding, the response could come too late. (ch. 9)

Here is the core of Seth's theory. The most basic reason any creature has a brain is to keep itself alive. The deepest layer of the self is a shapeless feeling of simply being a living body, which on his view is a control-aimed prediction about the body's condition. Every other perception, of the world or of the self, is built on that foundation. (ch. 9)

This also explains why the self feels steady. To keep the body in its safe range, the brain must hold very confident expectations that the body stays in that range, and those expectations tend to make themselves come true. The cost is that we may perceive ourselves as more stable than we are. When this machinery fails, the self can seem unreal. In the rare Cotard delusion, people believe they are already dead. (ch. 9)

Seth is careful about what he is not claiming. He does not say that only living things could ever be conscious. He says we cannot understand why our own experiences are the way they are without seeing our nature as living creatures. (ch. 9)

The bigger frame: a fish in water

The neuroscientist Karl Friston's free energy principle starts with what it means to exist at all. A thing exists only if it stays distinct from everything else. A drop of ink in water spreads out at once; a drop of oil lasts a while; living things actively keep up their own boundaries. (ch. 10)

Physics says isolated systems drift toward disorder, or higher entropy. Living things hold that drift off for a while by staying in the states that are statistically expected for them. A fish is usually in water. Your body is usually near 37 °C. A fish out of water is in a state that, for a fish, does not last. (ch. 10)

An organism can't directly measure how surprising its own sensations are. What it can measure, the math says, is a quantity called free energy, and free energy works out to be essentially the same thing as sensory prediction error. So minimising prediction error is what keeping yourself in your expected states looks like from the inside. Seth sums it up as: I predict myself, therefore I am. (ch. 10)

Why not hide in a dark, silent room where nothing is surprising? Because other signals, such as hunger, soon drift out of their expected range. Staying alive means acting. Seth treats the free energy principle as mathematical philosophy: experimental data can't prove it wrong, so it should be judged by how useful it is. He is clear that it is not itself a theory of consciousness. Its value is as a tool for building such theories. (ch. 10)

Why does a good regulator need a model?

To keep something steady you have to know how it will respond to what you do. A model lets the regulator predict the effect of each action, choose the one that keeps the variable on target, and act before a problem arrives. In the toy above, System A only learns about the cold snap once the house is already cooling, and its slow heater can't catch up in time. (ch. 9)

A common mix-up"Staying alive means keeping everything in the body fixed." Living systems let some things change so that others can stay the same. Blood pressure rises when you stand so that blood keeps reaching your brain. That is allostasis. (ch. 9)

8. Free will as a perception

In this part: what Benjamin Libet's famous experiment does and doesn't show, and how Seth explains the experience of choosing.

Words to knowReadiness potential: a slow build-up of electrical brain activity that comes before voluntary movements. Determinism: the idea that every event is fully fixed by earlier causes.

When you choose freely, it feels as if you, and nothing else, made the action happen. Seth treats this experience of volition as one more kind of self-related perception, built the same way as the others. (ch. 11)

He first sets aside what he calls "spooky" free will: an immaterial self that steps into the brain and makes physical events happen that otherwise wouldn't. Without spooky free will, the old argument about determinism stops mattering. Whether physics is fully fixed or has randomness built in, a voluntary action is neither a miracle nor a coin toss. (ch. 11)

In the early 1980s Benjamin Libet asked volunteers to flex a wrist whenever they felt like it. They watched a dot circling a screen and reported where it was when they felt the urge to move, while EEG recorded their brain activity. Averaged over many trials, the readiness potential began hundreds of milliseconds before the reported urge. Libet tried to rescue free will with a last-moment veto, "free won't". Seth points out that a conscious veto would be just as much of a miracle. (ch. 11)

In 2012 Aaron Schurger offered a different reading. Readiness potentials are measured by starting from moments when an action happened and looking backward. Think of the fairground "high striker", where a hammer blow sends a puck up a pole toward a bell. If you only studied the swings that rang the bell, you would conclude that a rising puck always means a ring. Schurger suggested brain activity drifts up and down all the time, and an action fires when the drift happens to cross a threshold. (ch. 11)

Try it: a ramp made of noise

This model's activity is random drift; a "movement" fires whenever it crosses the threshold. Run it, then compare the two averages.

Controls

A toy version of Schurger's idea: a slightly leaky random walk with a threshold. Its numbers are not his data.

Schurger tested this. People made spontaneous movements but sometimes also had to move as fast as possible after a beep. When their beep responses were fast, a ramp much like a readiness potential appeared in the moments before, though they hadn't been planning anything; slow responses showed little ramp. That fits the idea that the activity was already drifting near threshold. (ch. 11)

What does choosing feel like? Seth lists three features. You feel you are doing what you want. You feel you could have done otherwise. And the action seems to come from within, unlike a reflex such as jerking back a stubbed toe. Seth chose tea over coffee because he wanted tea, though he did not choose to want tea. (ch. 11)

In Seth's account, voluntary action is the brain's ability to steer many degrees of freedom, many possible responses, in line with your beliefs, values, and goals. The neuroscientist Patrick Haggard splits this into three processes: what to do, when to do it, and whether to go ahead at the last moment. (ch. 11)

Why have the experience at all? It flags actions that came from within, so you can notice their consequences and do better next time. "I could have done otherwise" doesn't mean you really could have, with the universe exactly as it was. It is useful because next time, in a situation that is similar but never identical, you might. (ch. 11)

So is free will an illusion? Spooky free will isn't real. But the ability to act from your own beliefs and goals is real, and it can be damaged by brain injury or disease. And experiences of volition are real perceptions, as real as experiences of colour. (ch. 11)

Why does it not matter whether the universe is deterministic?

Seth's account needs no gap in the chain of physical causes for a self to slip through. A deterministic universe chugs along fine with experiences of volition in it. If instead the universe has randomness at bottom, that doesn't help either: voluntary actions don't feel random, and they aren't. (ch. 11)

A common mix-up"Libet proved free will is fake." His results count against spooky free will, which Seth already rejects. And Schurger's work suggests the early ramp may partly be a product of how readiness potentials are measured. (ch. 11)

9. How conscious? Measuring level

In this part: how scientists tell being conscious apart from being awake, how "zap and zip" works, and what integrated information theory claims.

Words to knowWakefulness: physiological arousal, being awake as opposed to asleep. PCI: a number for how complex the brain's echo to a magnetic pulse is. Φ (phi): in integrated information theory, how much a system as a whole adds beyond its parts.

Before reliable thermometers, people argued over what heat was and what to measure it against: one proposal for a fixed point was a cool Paris cellar, another the melting point of butter. Once measurement was solid, temperature turned out to be the average energy of moving molecules. Seth asks whether consciousness could get its own thermometer. (ch. 2)

The first step is separating conscious level from wakefulness. Usually they go together, but not always. When you dream you are asleep yet having vivid experiences. In the vegetative state a patient cycles between sleep and waking but shows no outward sign of awareness. (ch. 2)

What in the brain makes the difference? Not the number of neurons: the cerebellum has about four times as many as the rest of the brain put together but seems barely involved, and people born without most of one live largely normal lives. Not overall activity either. What matters is how parts of the cortex and thalamus talk to each other. (ch. 2)

Marcello Massimini, working first with Giulio Tononi, found a way to listen. A magnetic pulse (TMS) taps one spot of cortex, and EEG records the echo. In dreamless sleep and anaesthesia the echo stays local and dies quickly, like ripples from a stone. In conscious states it spreads across the cortex in complex, changing patterns. A compression program, the kind that zips files, then measures how complex the echo is. The result is the perturbational complexity index, PCI. In a 2013 study PCI tracked doctors' diagnoses: lower in the vegetative state than in the minimally conscious state, and normal in locked-in patients, who are fully conscious but paralysed. (ch. 2)

Try it: zip the echo

Pick a pattern of response, and see how small the zip program can make it. Lower numbers mean more predictable.

Brain response to a pulse

Stylised patterns, not recordings. The score is a real Lempel-Ziv compression count of the pattern on screen, scaled so that a random pattern scores about 100.

These measures matter in hospitals. In 2006 Adrian Owen's team asked a behaviourally unresponsive young woman, in a brain scanner, to imagine playing tennis and then walking around her house. Her brain lit up the same distinct regions as healthy volunteers', so she must have been following instructions. Later, in 2010, a patient answered yes-or-no questions by imagining tennis for yes and walking for no. One analysis suggests 10 to 20 per cent of patients diagnosed as vegetative may keep some hidden awareness. In Milan, a young man with a high PCI seemed unresponsive in Italian until an uncle spoke to him in Arabic; he smiled at jokes. (ch. 2)

Seth's group also measured the complexity of ongoing brain activity, without the pulse. It drops in early sleep and anaesthesia, and it stays near waking levels in dreaming REM sleep. With Robin Carhart-Harris and Suresh Muthukumaraswamy they found that psilocybin, LSD, and low doses of ketamine raised it above normal waking rest, the first time such a measure had gone up instead of down. (ch. 2)

That raised a puzzle, which the zip demo shows. A brain with every neuron firing at random would score highest of all, yet it would probably support no experience, the way free-form noise eventually stops being music. Back in 1998 Tononi and Gerald Edelman, Seth's former mentor, had noted that every conscious experience is both informative, because it rules out countless other experiences, and integrated, because it comes as one unified scene. Complexity in that sense sits in the middle between order and disorder. (ch. 2)

Tononi's integrated information theory (IIT) goes further. It defines Φ as how much information a system generates as a whole beyond what its parts generate separately, and claims Φ simply is consciousness, the way temperature is molecular motion. A single light sensor has at most one bit of information, so it has little Φ. A camera's millions of sensors don't affect each other, so the whole adds nothing and Φ is zero. Cut a network cleanly into two halves and the whole has zero Φ, though each half may have some. That would explain why the camera-like cerebellum contributes little while the densely connected cortex contributes a lot. (ch. 3)

Try it: what does IIT say?

Drag each system to the verdict IIT gives it.

Seth finds IIT ambitious and fascinating but has a serious worry. Φ depends on everything a system could do, including states it never actually enters, and for a real brain we can't know that. So the theory's central claim can't be tested in practice. It also makes strange predictions: adding neurons that never fire, but could, would subtly change experience. And it implies a limited panpsychism, with consciousness wherever there is integrated information. (ch. 3)

Seth's alternative is to keep the insight that experiences are informative and integrated, treat those as properties to explain, and drop the claim that Φ is consciousness. His group builds practical versions of Φ that work on recordings of what a system actually does. In his terms, consciousness looks more like life, a cluster of properties with different explanations, than like temperature. (ch. 3)

Why can't a high zip score alone prove that someone is conscious?

Zip-style scores reward unpredictability, and pure randomness is the most unpredictable thing there is. A brain whose neurons fired at random would score near the top while almost certainly having no experience. A trustworthy measure needs to reward integration as well as information. PCI partly does this, because a pulse only produces a widespread echo when regions are connected. (ch. 2)

A common mix-up"If a patient is awake, they're conscious; if they don't respond, they're not." The vegetative state shows wakefulness without signs of awareness, and Owen's scans show awareness without outward response. Behaviour alone can mislead in both directions. (ch. 2)

10. Other minds: animals and machines

In this part: how Seth reasons about consciousness in animals and in AI, and why he keeps consciousness and intelligence apart.

Words to knowAnthropomorphism: seeing human qualities where they may not be. Anthropocentrism: judging every mind by human standards. Garland test: whether a person who knows something is a machine still feels it is conscious.

From the 800s to the 1700s, European church courts put animals on trial: pigs, bulls, even weevils. Absurd as that was, Seth notes that it treated animals as having inner lives and minds that could choose, a sharp contrast with the Cartesian picture of animals as machines. (ch. 12)

Seth sets some ground rules for other minds. A creature's lack of language isn't evidence that it lacks consciousness. Intelligence is a poor test of consciousness. And two biases pull in opposite directions: anthropomorphism makes us see human-like minds where there may be none, and anthropocentrism blinds us to minds unlike ours. (ch. 12)

Seth believes all mammals are conscious. His reason is shared mechanisms, not how human they look. Mammal brains have the same kind of six-layered cortex, a thalamus wired into it, and the same brain chemicals. Their brain activity changes in similar ways in sleep and under anaesthesia. In 2005 he, Bernard Baars, and David Edelman listed 17 features of human consciousness that could be tested for in other mammals. (ch. 12)

The mirror test, developed by Gordon Gallup in the 1970s, puts a mark where an animal can't normally see it. If, after looking in a mirror, the animal checks the mark on its own body, it passes. Passes are rare. Among mammals they are limited to some great apes, a handful of dolphins and killer whales, and one elephant. Monkeys fail. Yet Seth, who spent a day among rhesus monkeys on an island off Puerto Rico, is sure they are conscious selves, just not furry little people. He also points to a video, made famous by Frans de Waal, of a capuchin flinging back a cucumber after its neighbour got a grape. Animals may also fail because they dislike mirrors or avoid eye contact. (ch. 12)

Octopuses push hardest at our intuitions. Our last common ancestor lived about 600 million years ago, so their minds evolved separately from ours. A common octopus has about half a billion neurons, roughly three fifths of them in its arms, and its brain has none of the insulating myelin that mammal brains use. It edits its own genetic messages on a large scale. It camouflages itself to match colour, shape, and texture while being colour-blind, and its skin can sense light. It tells its own arms from other things by taste: its suckers won't grip its own skin. Being an octopus might not come with a sense of owning a body anything like ours. (ch. 12)

Further out the evidence gets thinner. Bird brains differ from mammals' but map quite closely onto the mammalian cortex and thalamus. Injured zebrafish will move to a bare, bright tank if it holds a painkiller. Injured fruit flies stay extra sensitive afterwards, a little like chronic pain. Seth finds it hard to credit the nematode worm, with its 302 neurons, with meaningful experience. Responses to pain are a useful guide because they relate to staying alive. (ch. 12)

Try it: experience or just smarts?

Drag each finding to what it mainly suggests, by Seth's lights.

Many people expect AI to become conscious once it is smart enough. Seth says that rests on two assumptions. The first is functionalism, that the right processing is enough whatever it runs on; he is agnostic about it. The second is that consciousness comes along with intelligence, and that one he rejects. (ch. 13)

Theories disagree about machines. Some researchers say the right kind of information processing would be enough. IIT says any system with the right cause-and-effect structure would have some consciousness, whatever it looks like from outside. Seth imagines a robot built on his own principles, with predictive control and sensors for its battery and joints. Would it be conscious? His honest answer is that he doesn't know, but probably not. In living things, self-maintenance goes all the way down: every cell keeps rebuilding the conditions for its own survival. No current or near-future computer does that. (ch. 13)

In the film Ex Machina (2014), the test is whether a person who knows Ava is a robot still feels she is conscious. People now call this the Garland test, after the director, and like the Turing test it tests the human as much as the machine. In 2014 a chatbot posing as a 13-year-old Ukrainian boy fooled 10 of 30 judges. In 2020 the language model GPT-3 began writing fluent essays without understanding them. Lifelike robots called Geminoids, meanwhile, made fear the most common reaction among visitors in one study: they sit in the uncanny valley, close to human but not close enough. (ch. 13)

The ethics come sooner than real machine consciousness would. Machines that only seem conscious will change how we feel and act. If we did create real machine consciousness, it would have moral status and possibly forms of suffering we could not recognise. In 2019 the philosopher Thomas Metzinger called for a 30-year pause on research aimed at artificial experience. Seth also flags cerebral organoids, tiny brain-like structures grown from human stem cells; one study found waves of activity in them resembling those of premature babies. (ch. 13)

Seth ends where Copernicus and Darwin left us. Each time science moved humans out of the centre, it gave something back: a universe, a family tree of all life. A science of consciousness, he argues, places our minds within nature too. (Epilogue)

Why doesn't passing the Turing test settle whether a machine is conscious?

The Turing test asks whether a machine's text can pass for a human's, which is a test of one narrow kind of intelligence. Since Seth holds that intelligence and consciousness can come apart, a perfect score would tell you about the machine's skill and the judges' gullibility, and little about whether there is anything it is like to be the machine. (ch. 13)

A common mix-up"Once AI gets smart enough, it will wake up." On Seth's view consciousness tracks being alive more closely than being smart. Making computers smarter is not, by itself, going to make them feel anything. (ch. 13)

What this guide changes or leaves out:

  • The order. Seth's first section, on measuring conscious level and on integrated information theory, appears here as part 9, after the parts on content and self.
  • Two theories Seth mentions and sets aside, the global workspace and higher-order thought theories, are left out. IIT's axioms and the mathematics of the free energy principle are skipped; the free energy principle gets a short section inside part 7.
  • Several stories are shortened or dropped: the history of thermometers, Vienna around 1900, the trials of animals, the golem, the simulation argument, and the brain surgery Seth watches in the epilogue. His chapter on his mother's delirium appears only as the opening of part 5.
  • The interactives are toys with invented numbers. The hallucination dial, the gorilla curves, the wet-lawn table, the thermostats, the random-drift model, and the zip patterns illustrate Seth's ideas; none reproduces his data. The grey squares are a simpler cousin of the Adelson illusion he uses. Seth reports the bridge study without figures, so the page does too.
  • Where the page applies a theory to a case Seth doesn't discuss (for example, the three explanations of the bridge study), it says so.
  • Seth presents controlled hallucination and the beast machine as his own view, shaped by many others. He notes that interoceptive inference is hard to test, that body-illusion experiments can be driven by suggestion, that the free energy principle can't be falsified by experiment, and that claims about animal and machine consciousness are uncertain.
