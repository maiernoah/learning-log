This page teaches The Power of Focusing: A Practical Guide to Emotional Self-Healing by Ann Weiser Cornell (1996). It is a study guide written in plain language, not a copy of the book. About the source.

Whenever Jenny had to speak up for herself, her throat felt like it was choking. Job interviews and class presentations were nearly impossible. She had tried for years to get rid of the feeling. (p. 1)

In her first lesson, Cornell asked her to check the word "choking." Jenny found it was more like a hand squeezing. She said hello to it instead of fighting it, and it stopped hurting. Then she sat with it as she would sit with a friend. It seemed to tell her that it cared about her and was trying to keep her from making mistakes. The squeezing went away. (pp. 1–2)

Most of us have a feeling like Jenny's: a tight throat before speaking in class, a knot in the stomach before a hard conversation. We treat it as a nuisance. This book teaches the opposite move.

The skill is called Focusing. Cornell describes it as noticing how you feel, and then having "a conversation with your feelings in which you do most of the listening." (pp. 2–3) It grew out of research on why therapy works for some people and not others. Cornell taught it for years in workshops, and this book is her step-by-step manual.

A note before you startThis guide explains a self-help method. It is not therapy, and the publisher's note in the book advises seeking a professional when expert help is needed. If a feeling is too big to handle alone, talk to an adult you trust.

By the end you'll be able to

  • Explain what researchers heard on therapy tapes that let them predict who would get better.
  • Describe a felt sense and tell it apart from a thought or a plain emotion.
  • Walk through the steps of a Focusing session, from sensing into your body to saying "I'll be back."
  • Pick a helpful next move when Focusing gets stuck: a blank, a harsh inner voice, too many feelings at once.
  • Listen to a friend the Focusing way: say back what you hear instead of fixing.

1. What the therapy tapes revealed

In this part: the research question behind Focusing, and the surprising answer.

Words to know

Psychotherapy (therapy for short) is talking with a trained professional to work through emotional problems. A felt sense is a feeling in your body that is about something in your life, like a jittery stomach before a speech. You'll meet it fully in part 2.

The question

In the early 1960s, Eugene Gendlin, a professor at the University of Chicago, asked a plain question: why does therapy help some people but not others? (p. 3)

He and his colleagues recorded hundreds of therapy sessions. They taped whole courses of therapy, first session to last, with many different therapists and clients. (p. 3)

Then they sorted the tapes. The therapist, the client, and psychological tests each judged whether the therapy had worked. A case was used only if all three agreed. That left two piles: success and failure. (p. 4)

The first surprise: it wasn't the therapists

Common sense said the successful therapists must be warmer, more genuine, or more brilliant. They weren't. The researchers found no significant difference in how the therapists behaved in the two piles. (p. 4)

The second surprise: you could hear it early

The difference was in the clients. And it showed up in the first or second session. By listening to those early tapes, the researchers could predict whether the therapy would end up working. (p. 4)

So the successful clients weren't learning something from therapy. They walked in already doing it. (p. 4)

What they heard

At some point, successful clients slowed down. They became less smooth. They groped for words to describe something they were feeling right then: "It's... uh... it's not exactly anger... hmm." Often they pointed to their bodies: "It's right here in my chest." (pp. 4–5)

Unsuccessful clients stayed articulate the whole time. They explained, analyzed, and even cried about their problems. But they stayed "up in their heads" and never touched that vague, hard-to-describe feeling inside. (p. 5)

From discovery to skill

Gendlin didn't want to sit back and watch some clients improve while others stayed stuck. He found a way to teach the skill, and he called it Focusing. His 1978 book Focusing sold hundreds of thousands of copies. (p. 5)

People soon used it outside therapy too: as self-help, for decisions, and for creative work. (p. 5) Cornell lists other uses: getting unstuck in therapy, knowing what you feel and want, handling overwhelming emotions, releasing habits and self-criticism, and making decisions with your whole self. (pp. 6–8)

Cornell stresses that Focusing was discovered, not invented. It's a natural ability everyone is born with. Hurts in childhood and a culture that distrusts feelings make many of us lose it, so we have to relearn it. (p. 5)

Why does it matter that the difference showed up in the first sessions?

If the successful clients were already doing it on day one, then therapy wasn't teaching it. It was a skill some people brought with them. And a skill that people bring can, in principle, be taught to everyone else. That's what Gendlin set out to do. (pp. 4–5)

A common mix-up"The people who got better were the ones who talked about their feelings most clearly." The book says nearly the opposite. Clear, fluent talk, even about emotions, went with failure. Success went with slowing down and struggling to put a fresh body feeling into words. (pp. 4–5)

2. The felt sense

In this part: what a felt sense is, where to look for one, and a full session from start to finish.

Words to know

A felt sense is a body sensation that has meaning. It's "about" something in your life. A jittery stomach as you stand up to speak, or a tight chest before a hard phone call, are both felt senses. (p. 3)

What we usually do instead

When a feeling like that shows up, most of us try to get rid of it. We curse it ("Why now?"), put ourselves down ("A better person wouldn't freeze up"), breathe it away, or reach for a drink. (p. 3)

What doesn't occur to us is to listen to it. Cornell's claim is that when you do, the feeling is much more likely to relax and let you move on clearly. (p. 3)

Why the body?

Cornell argues that the body knows things the mind alone doesn't: how we're living, what we need, which people bring out our best, what has hurt us. (p. 8)

Her reason is about time. The mind lives mostly in the past and the future, remembering and worrying. But change can only happen in the present, and the body is always in the present. (p. 9)

So Focusing is a way to "listen to the whispers of your body before it has to shout." (p. 9)

Where to look

Felt senses can form anywhere in the body. But they are usually clearer in the middle: throat, chest, stomach, and belly. That's where a session starts looking. (pp. 12, 29)

What you find might be an emotion ("sad"), a sensation ("tight," "jittery"), or an image ("a knot," "a rock"). It can be vague or strong. It can even feel good. (pp. 29, 64)

One of Cornell's tips: anything can be something. Even "nothing" has a quality, like "a gray nothing" or "a tired nothing." (p. 29)

Not just "getting into your feelings"

Focusing includes emotions, but it isn't the same as having them. If you feel sad, the Focusing move is to sense where in your body the sad is, and what it's like there. (p. 30)

It also isn't meditation. You don't need a special posture or to sit still, and you don't aim for deep relaxation. You need to stay aware of what your body feels. (pp. 26, 28)

Try it: follow Ted's first session

Ted's wife kept asking how he felt, and he drew a blank. Step through his session to see each move, and watch what happens in his stomach. This is a preview; parts 3 to 6 teach each move properly. (pp. 11–14)

Ted's session, one step at a time

Step 1 of 8

Why did Ted think he felt nothing at first?

His stomach had been clenched for so long that clenched felt "normal." He wasn't feeling nothing; he was feeling something he'd stopped noticing. A gentle open question, "What wants my awareness now?", let it come forward. (p. 12)

A common mix-up"A felt sense has to be strong and clearly unpleasant to count." Cornell says the opposite. Felt senses are often subtle, often hard to describe, and sometimes pleasant, like warmth or openness. Waiting for something dramatic is one of the main reasons beginners think they feel "nothing." (pp. 63–65)

3. Sitting next to the lake

In this part: the inner climate that lets feelings change, and why you sit with a feeling instead of in it.

Words to know

Dis-identification means stepping back slightly from a feeling, so it is part of you, not all of you. "I am sad" becomes "Part of me is sad." (pp. 17, 97)

The shy animal

Cornell opens with a picture. You stand at the edge of a forest, and a shy animal peeks out. If you want it to trust you, you don't run at it shouting. You stay still, patient, and interested. (p. 15)

A felt sense is like that animal. It may need to find out you're safe before it shows you anything. (p. 15)

Cornell often talks about felt senses as if they were people, and apologizes for it. Her point is practical: they respond to how you treat them, much as a person would. (p. 19)

Let it be as it is

When we notice a feeling, we tend to do one of four things (pp. 15–16). We judge it ("I shouldn't feel this"). We reason with it ("There's no reason to be scared"). We talk ourselves out of it ("Others have it worse"). Or we analyze it ("Why do I always feel this way?").

Cornell says none of these help you change. You stay in the same place and often feel worse. (p. 16)

Her central claim is that feelings change when they are allowed to be as they are. She quotes Gendlin: "What is split off, not felt, remains the same. When it is felt, it changes." (p. 16)

Many people fear that allowing a feeling will make it bigger. Cornell reports the reverse: feelings grow louder when they're not allowed, and settle down when they are. She says she has seen this hundreds of times and "never seen it fail." That's her teaching experience, stated with great confidence, not a measured result. (p. 16)

With it, not in it

Think of your emotions as a big lake. You can plunge in, or you can sit beside it. Focusing works best from the bank. (p. 17)

From inside the feeling, you can't sense it as a whole, just as a fish can't easily know water. Gendlin liked to say that if you want to know how the soup smells, don't stick your head in it. (p. 17)

There's a second reason. If you are the feeling, nobody is left to listen to its story. Being beside it makes you its listener. (p. 17)

Try it: from "I am" to "part of me"

Cornell's practical tip: when you catch yourself saying "I am sad," change it. Try "Part of me is sad," or "I'm aware of something in me that feels sad." (p. 17)

Type a sentence that starts with "I am," "I'm," or "I feel," or pick an example.

Rewrite a feeling sentence


Say each version slowly to yourself and notice any difference in your body. Cornell's point is that the feeling stays just as real, but now there's room around it. (pp. 17, 97)

Being a good listener to yourself

Remember being truly listened to, and how it helped you understand yourself? Now remember getting criticism or advice when you wanted to be heard. Cornell's point: advice isn't listening. (p. 17)

Focusing is giving yourself the first kind. She names four qualities of good listening (p. 18):

  • A welcoming presence: interest in whatever you find, assuming it has a good reason to be as it is.
  • Holding the space: keeping your attention there, as if saying "I'm here and I'm staying."
  • Hearing the essence: listening for what is longing to be heard.
  • Staying in present time: coming back to how your body feels now, even when the topic is past or future.
Why would naming a feeling as "part of me" make it easier to bear, not weaker?

Cornell says dis-identification lets you feel something just as strongly, but from a slightly different spot. You become its witness instead of being swamped. A feeling that is part of you can be kept company; a feeling that is all of you can't. (pp. 17, 97)

A common mix-up"To change a strong feeling, you have to dive in and feel it as intensely as possible." Cornell says many people believe this, and call themselves "resistant" when they hold back. Focusing isn't about maximum intensity. It's about a relationship with the feeling, which works best from beside it. (pp. 16–17, 36)

4. Saying hello

In this part: the simplest and most important move in Focusing, and what to do when you can't be friendly.

Words to know

The feeling about the feeling is a reaction to a feeling that gets in the way of accepting it: angry about your fear, afraid of your sadness, impatient with a stuck place. (p. 20)

Every relationship begins with hello

When you notice a feeling, say to it inside: "Hello. I know you're there." It sounds almost silly. Cornell insists it's one of the most powerful moves there is. (p. 18)

We usually ignore our felt senses, fight them, or argue with them. She compares this to treating them like unwelcome party guests, talked about but never spoken to. (pp. 18–19)

Her examples: Rebecca felt a constriction in her chest getting tighter and tighter. She said hello to it, and it eased quite a bit. (p. 19)

Catherine had a tightness in her shoulders for weeks. In a workshop she grew frustrated that it "wouldn't talk." Then she realized she'd shamed it, fought it, and tried to fix it, but never said hello. Once she did, it began to release. (p. 19)

When you can't feel friendly

Sometimes you just don't like the feeling. That's fine. Move your hello to the part of you that doesn't feel friendly. (p. 20)

Tom felt an old fear in his belly. When he tried to be friendly to it, angry impatience rose up: "I'm so sick of always getting scared!" So he said hello to the impatience instead. (p. 20)

Cornell warns that pushing past a feeling about the feeling won't work; the session will stall right there. Often it relaxes and lets you return to the first feeling. Sometimes the whole session is about it, and that can touch something central in your life. (p. 20)

All the voices, and not knowing

Mixed feelings are natural. Part of you wants closeness; part of you is scared of it. You don't have to pick one. Each can be heard, and something new can come from hearing them all. (pp. 20–21)

Cornell also asks for an attitude of not knowing. If you think "I already know why I'm afraid," you've stopped listening. Her test: as long as a felt sense still wants your attention, there's something about it you don't know yet. (p. 21)

Our culture rewards fast, clear answers. Focusing values knowledge that starts vague and takes time. Cornell compares it to a treasure hunter who is most excited by the chests not yet opened. (pp. 21–22)

Finally, you follow; you don't steer. You can't make a felt sense tell you anything or change, any more than you can force the shy animal to be your friend. (p. 22)

Every session is different. One may feel like skimming the surface, the next like deep-sea diving. Cornell's reassurance: if you trust it, the felt sense will only bring you what you can deal with. (p. 23)

Try it: what keeps a feeling stuck?

Sort these responses to a hard feeling. Which ones, by the book's account, keep it the same, and which let it change?

Why would saying hello make a tightening feeling ease?

Cornell admits she's speaking of felt senses as if they were people. Her explanation: they're there to communicate, and they want to be heard. Rebecca's chest may have been tightening out of a kind of panic about being ignored. Once it was acknowledged, it could relax a little. (p. 19)

A common mix-up"If I can't feel kind toward a feeling, I'm doing Focusing wrong and should stop." No. The unfriendly reaction simply becomes the next thing to greet. Cornell calls this the feeling about the feeling, and says it often leads somewhere important. (p. 20)

5. Finding it and naming it

In this part: the first six steps of a session, and the move you'll repeat all the way through, checking back.

Words to know

Checking back means offering a word, image, or sentence back to the felt sense to see if it fits. (p. 32) Proprioception is your sense of where your body parts are without looking. (p. 27)

Getting ready

At first, find a quiet time and place. Half an hour is ideal, but even ten minutes is worth it. Sit rather than lie down, so you don't fall asleep. Keep a sweater nearby, and maybe a notebook. (pp. 25–26)

Once you know how, you can focus with your eyes open, on a walk, or while doing the dishes. (p. 25)

Cornell gives each step a short phrase you can say to yourself. She stresses that the steps are suggestions, not rules. You may skip stages or add new ones, and a session can end at any stage. The early steps do help the later ones go well. (pp. 25, 43) Here are the first six.

1. "Which way today?"

Decide whether to work on something you choose, like a decision, a habit, or a reaction to someone, or to wait and see what comes. If chores keep nagging you, jot them down so you can let them go for now. (pp. 26–27)

2. "I'm sensing into my body."

Get comfortable. Notice where you touch the chair, then your feet, hands, and shoulders. Then bring your attention to the middle: throat, chest, stomach, belly. Check your throat the way you'd check for a sore throat. (p. 27)

If that's hard, close your eyes and lift your arm; notice you know where it is. That's proprioception. Or start at your toes and work your way upward. If you get so relaxed you drift off, open your eyes or stretch, then come back. (pp. 27–28)

3. "What wants my awareness now?"

Or, if you chose an issue: "How am I about that issue?" Then wait and notice anything. (p. 28)

Cornell says the biggest barrier here is wondering whether you "really" have a felt sense. Her advice is to forget the term and just look for "something." Your body may also bring up a different issue than the one you picked. Either path is fine. (p. 29)

4. "I'm saying hello to what's here."

Greet it, then notice how it responds. It might ease, settle "like a bird on a branch," or get clearer. Don't rush, and come close enough for a real hello rather than shouting across a canyon. (p. 30)

5. "I'm finding the best way to describe it."

Find a word, phrase, image, sound, or gesture for how it feels right now. Cornell compares this to tasting a new gum flavor: "It's not exactly cinnamon... not quite mint... I know!" (pp. 30–31)

When a description fits, you may feel the sense get stronger, feel a little relief, or feel a full release. Don't strain for the word, though. Loving the felt sense matters more than understanding it. (pp. 31–32)

It's easy to get lost hunting for words and lose touch with the feeling. When that happens, bring your attention back inside and repeat the last description that felt close. (p. 32)

6. "I'm checking back with my body."

Offer the word back to the feeling: "Squeezing? Is that right?" Cornell says one of three things happens, and each moves you forward (pp. 32–33):

  • Yes: a sense of rightness, and it's easier to stay with.
  • Partly, but there's more: "squeezing... and also scared." Offer the new word too.
  • Not right: that's useful too, because "not right" points toward what is.

Keep checking all session long. Feelings change, and a word can go out of date. Something you called "fear" may have turned into excitement. (p. 37)

Try it: offer words to a felt sense

This is a toy, not a real felt sense. Imagine a "something" in your throat. Offer words one at a time and read how it answers. In real Focusing, your own body answers, and the answer can be anything.

There's something in your throat. Offer it a word.

    Try it: put the first six steps in order

    Cornell is blunt here. Many readers of self-help books read the exercises and never do them. She asks you to stop and actually try these six steps before reading on. (p. 33) You can use steps 1 to 6 of the guided session in part 6.

    Why is "not right" a useful answer instead of a failure?

    Feeling that a word doesn't fit is your body telling you something. The sense of "no, it's more..." steers you toward a better word. In the book's example, "not squeezing... it's more like constriction!" (p. 33)

    6. Keeping it company

    In this part: the rest of the conversation, from sitting with a felt sense to ending a session well.

    Words to know

    Interested curiosity is the attitude you bring: open wonder about what's there, with no agenda. (p. 36)

    So far you've met the felt sense and learned its name. Now you sit down and hear its story. (p. 35)

    "Is it OK to just be with this right now?"

    Before anything else, check that it's OK to simply keep it company. You're not in a hurry and not pushing your own agenda. (p. 35)

    "I'm sitting with it, with interested curiosity."

    Cornell likes the word sitting. Standing says "I only have a minute." Sitting says this conversation can take its time. And you sit with it, not in it. (p. 36)

    What comes next may be small. "Squeezed" might become "squeezed and resentful." You don't need to know yet what it resents. Stay with what you do know. (p. 36)

    She loves the word curiosity because it's hard to imagine "judgmental curiosity." (p. 36)

    "I'm sensing how it feels from its point of view."

    This is a subtle shift. Cornell's student Jeanne said her throat felt "uncomfortable." That's how it felt to Jeanne. When asked how it felt from its side, she said, "Oh! It feels scared." (pp. 37–38)

    You can sense this the way you sense a friend's mood when they aren't talking. (p. 38)

    "I'm asking..."

    Questions can help, but don't expect a literal reply. Cornell once thought her inner place should "open up a little mouth" and answer. When it didn't, she felt hopeless. Then she saw that the question's real job is to help you hold a friendly, curious attitude, so you sense more. (pp. 38–39)

    She offers four questions that most often help (pp. 39–40):

    • Does it have an emotional quality? A scared tightness? An angry one?
    • What gets it so ___? Use its own word: "What gets you so jumpy?"
    • What does it need? Not first, because that's like trying to fix the feeling before hearing it.
    • How would "all OK" feel? Let your body show you now how it would feel if this were resolved.

    A test for the answer: you haven't really heard the felt sense until you hear something you didn't already know. (p. 39)

    Other questions can work too, like "What do you want me to know?" And sometimes no question is needed at all. Questions are there to help the felt sense, not to satisfy your curiosity. (p. 41)

    Try it: the order to try the questions

    These are tools to pick from, not a checklist. Still, when you do use them, Cornell suggests an order. Put her four questions in that order.

    Two traps

    Asking too soon. The most common reason a question gets no answer is that it came before hello, a fitting description, and simply being with it. (p. 40)

    Asking "why." "Why" belongs to the logical mind, which will happily take over. It also sounds like criticism. Turn it into "what": "Why are you angry?" becomes "What gets you so angry?" (p. 41)

    If you hear yourself say "I think," "probably," or "it must be," that answer likely came from your head. Check it with your body. Body answers come slower. (p. 41)

    Ending well

    Ask the felt sense whether it's OK to stop in a minute or two, or whether there's something more it needs you to know first. That's not the same as asking if the problem is solved. Change comes in steps. (p. 42)

    At the start, tell your body how much time you have. Cornell says it has an amazing capacity for timing itself. There's usually a natural resting point every five to ten minutes. (pp. 42–43)

    Leave time to stop. Barbara McGavin's rule of thumb: about one to two minutes of stopping for every ten minutes of Focusing. (pp. 42–43)

    Tell it "I'll be back," and keep that promise. You can "mark the place" with an image or phrase, like a bookmark. Then thank your body. (pp. 43–44)

    Cornell's measure of success is generous: if you stayed with something you felt in your body, you succeeded, even if nothing else happened. (p. 44)

    Try it: a guided practice session

    When you're ready, use this to try a session. Read a step, close your eyes, and open them when you're ready for the next. Skip any step that doesn't fit, and stop whenever your body says it's enough. The step phrases are Cornell's own, from her summary list; the instructions and tips are condensed. (p. 44)

    Guided session

    Why ask "What does it need?" late, not early?

    Asked first, it's an attempt to make the feeling go away before you've heard what it's saying. Cornell puts it after the emotional-quality and "what gets it so..." questions for that reason. (p. 40)

    A common mix-up"The session failed because the problem isn't solved." Cornell says the problem may not be resolved, and certainly not the way your logical mind expected, yet it can still be a good time to stop. Change comes a step at a time. (p. 42)

    Check yourself

    7. Gifts and uses

    In this part: what change feels like when it comes, and how people use Focusing on real problems.

    Words to know

    A felt shift is Gendlin's name for the good feeling that comes when something changes inside. An action block is anything you want to do but don't. (pp. 7, 45)

    The felt shift

    Gendlin calls it the felt shift. Ted's stomach letting go in part 2 was one. Cornell describes it as your whole body-and-mind rearranging around a new understanding. (p. 45)

    Unlike some methods, you don't have to take it on faith that something changed. Your body tells you right away, by feeling good in a special way. You don't even need to understand what happened. (pp. 45, 48)

    It can feel like fresh air in a closed room, energy moving, relief, an "aha!" Often there's clarity, peace, and a warm, spacious well-being. (p. 45)

    Her advice is to receive it. We tend to treat problems like patients in a waiting room: when one eases, we call "Next!" Instead, take time to feel it, and bring it with you when you stop. (pp. 45–46)

    If you don't feel better

    That probably doesn't mean Focusing failed. You're building a relationship, and that takes time. Big life issues can take many sessions over weeks or months. (p. 46)

    Small shifts count. Cornell asks you to be a gardener who is glad when a green shoot appears, long before it flowers, and to protect it from the critical voice that says it isn't enough. (pp. 46, 48–49)

    After a big, transforming session, she says to "ground" it. Imagine old situations and notice how you feel now. Write in a journal, make art, or tell a friend who will listen without advice. (pp. 46–47)

    Change in steps: Cornell's writer's block

    Cornell's own story shows how slow and surprising this can be. For years she struggled to write. In her first session on it, not writing felt like hiding. Next time, it felt like ducking, as if something would get her if she raised her head. (p. 57)

    Then came an image of a shooting range, and the word "sniping." It brought back her father's sarcastic voice: "Who do you think you are?" Writing got easier after that, but not easy. (pp. 57–58)

    Years later, she found a tight band in her chest that felt like a stubborn teenager saying "You can't make me!" She admired it and listened instead of fighting it. The next day, to her surprise, that same energy pulled her toward the computer. (pp. 58–59)

    Uses, through the book's stories

    Chapter Seven tells how people used Focusing on particular problems. These are Cornell's case stories, not controlled studies. (p. 51)

    Turning fear into want

    Any fear can be asked what it wants. "I'm afraid I'll be too tired" can become "I want to have energy." "I'm afraid people won't like my work" can become "I want my work to be appreciated." Both may be true, but the second points forward. Then ask your body to show you how it would feel to have that want fulfilled. (p. 62)

    Try it: action block or addiction?

    Cornell gives two fill-in sentences. An action block is "I want to ___, but I don't." An addiction runs in reverse: "I want to stop ___, but I don't." (p. 7) Sort these.

    Sorting is just the start. The Focusing move is the same for both. Invite a felt sense of the part of you that wants the habit, or the part that doesn't want to act. Usually that part has had no voice, because you've been busy condemning it or excusing it. Listen from its point of view. (pp. 54, 59)

    Two more questions can help: "Is there something hidden under this?" and "Is there something that wants to live?" (p. 54) And be ready for surprises. One woman with a ten-year block against sports found she actually hated sports. She joined a dance class instead, and the block was gone. (p. 59)

    A common mix-up"Focusing cures physical illness." The book doesn't claim that. Cornell tells stories where body symptoms eased, but she says that quite often the insight is true and the symptom doesn't change. Kay's sneezing came right back the next day. (pp. 47–48, 57)

    Why feel each option separately when making a decision?

    A pros-and-cons list uses only logic, and Matt finished his as unsure as ever. Feeling one option at a time gives each its own felt sense to compare, so the whole of you weighs in. Cornell adds that big decisions usually take several sessions plus practical research. Focusing may even say a choice is right but the timing isn't. (pp. 8, 54–55)

    Check yourself

    8. When it gets stuck

    In this part: the common sticking points, and Cornell's move for each.

    Words to know

    The inner Critic is the harsh inner voice that attacks you. The Doubter is the voice that says your experience isn't real. Clearing a space is a way to set many feelings aside, one by one. (pp. 65, 70–71)

    Cornell says that if Focusing has been easy for you, you're unusual. Most people hit sticky spots, because it runs against how our culture trains us. (p. 63)

    The problemCornell's suggestion
    "I feel nothing."Check for something positive, something subtle, or something hard to describe. Give possible felt senses the benefit of the doubt. (pp. 63–65)
    "Too many things at once."Clear a space: greet each thing and imagine setting it somewhere safe nearby, then ask "What else?" (pp. 65–66)
    "I'm feeling good. Does that count?"Yes. Greet it, describe it, savor it. Ask: "Is there something you want to show me?" (pp. 66–67)
    "I see pictures."Notice whether the image is in your body or in front of your eyes. Show a visual image to your body and ask how it feels. (pp. 67–68)
    "It needs something I can't give."Hearing a need isn't a promise. Say "I hear you" and keep listening. (pp. 68–69)
    "I feel bad about myself."Name it: "That's my Critic." Say hello, then ask what it's afraid of. (pp. 70–71)
    "I'm just making this up."That's the Doubter. Don't argue; check with your body. (pp. 71–72)
    "My head answers my questions."Thank your head and ask the body again. (p. 72)
    "I'm impatient with it."Notice the impatience and greet it, or focus on it. One woman found she was treating her felt senses the way her father had treated her tears. (pp. 72–73)
    "Two feelings at once."Say hello to each. Ask: stay with both, or is one calling me more? (pp. 73–74)
    "One good feeling, one bad."Don't choose. Stay in the good feeling and sense the other from there, as Bryan did with warmth and a tight stomach. (p. 67)
    "I'm afraid of what I'll find."Turn toward the fear itself and keep it company. (p. 73)
    "My mind keeps wandering."Speak aloud, write, draw, walk, or focus with a friend. (pp. 74–76)

    "I feel nothing"

    When Cornell was learning, she felt a total blank, and was ashamed, depressed, and anxious about it. Then she noticed: that's a lot of feeling! She just hadn't counted it. (p. 63)

    Felt senses can be subtle. She compares it to someone used to loud music who is set down in a quiet forest. At first it seems nothing is happening. With time, the forest becomes fascinating. (p. 64)

    One man said he felt nothing. Asked for detail, he had noticed a shaky throat, a possible tight stomach, and an open feeling in his chest, and dismissed each one. (pp. 64–65)

    And if it's there but hard to describe, congratulate yourself. That's exactly what went with success in Gendlin's research. (p. 65)

    Too many things: clearing a space

    Greet each thing without going into it. Imagine it finding a safe place outside you, on a shelf or a pillow, where you can find it later. Then ask "What else?" (pp. 65–66)

    Watch for a background feeling too, like "always tired." It's like wallpaper you've stopped seeing. (p. 66)

    You can invite feelings to step aside, but you can't force them. If one clings, ask what it needs so it can wait. (p. 66)

    Wanting and doing are different

    Cornell imagines Focusing and feeling that the answer is to fly to Tahiti today. Arguing with that would stop the process. So she listens. It turns out she needs to relax without obligations, and the urge to fly away fades. (p. 69)

    But the other side matters too. If your body keeps pointing to the same action and you never take it, you'll stay stuck. Ask for a small step that feels possible. A lonely man first only phoned about a dance, then peeked in the door, then stayed for one song. (pp. 69–70)

    Three voices that get in the way

    The Critic attacks: "You'll fail like always." Pushing it away brings only short relief. Cornell treats it as an unhealed part that is always afraid of something. Ask what it fears and what it wants, and just hear the answers. (pp. 70–71)

    The Doubter says you're making it up. It can raise doubts but never settle them. Your body can: check whether what came feels right. Where it came from doesn't matter. (pp. 71–72)

    The head rushes in with quick, familiar answers to questions meant for the body. Thank it, and ask again more slowly. (p. 72)

    Try it: who is talking?

    Sort these inner remarks by which voice is speaking.

    Why does the Doubter lose its power when you check with your body?

    The Doubter only knows how to doubt; it has no way to confirm anything. Focusing does have one: bring the words back to the felt sense and notice if they feel right. That swaps an argument you can't win for a check you can actually do. (p. 71)

    A common mix-up"Focusing needs a vivid imagination." Cornell says some people get images easily and some never do, and it has nothing to do with Focusing ability. Images are welcome but not required. (p. 68)

    Check yourself

    9. Focusing with a friend

    In this part: how two people take turns, and how to listen without fixing.

    Words to know

    In a Focusing partnership, two friends split the time and swap roles. The Focuser does the Focusing, out loud. The listener keeps them company. (p. 77)

    An equal exchange

    A partnership isn't like therapy, where one person is paid and the other gets attention. Each person gets equal time in each role. (p. 77)

    As Focuser, you say at least some of what you feel aloud. That usually helps you concentrate. But if speaking would interrupt you, stay quiet. Your job is your process, not entertaining the listener. (pp. 77–78)

    You are not the expert

    Before starting, the listener senses into their own body. If they notice felt senses of their own, they greet them and let them know their turn will come. (p. 78)

    The listener's first job is to be present, with no agenda for the other person. You don't need them to change or to have a "good" session. (p. 78)

    Cornell calls this the hardest and most important thing to remember: you are not the expert, the healer, or the fixer. The Focuser is responsible for the session, and for their own life. (pp. 78–79)

    A note from this guide, not the book"Not fixing" is about Focusing practice. If a friend tells you they are in danger or might hurt themselves, getting help from a trusted adult isn't fixing. Do it.

    Practical listening: say it back

    The listener's second job is to say back some of what they hear. "I feel sad." "You're sad." It may feel like being a parrot. Cornell says to wait until it's your turn to focus. Then you'll know how good it feels. (p. 79)

    The real key belongs to the Focuser. Take the listener's words back down into your body and check them. Don't just say "Yes, that's what I said" to be polite. Maybe "tightness" is really "squeezing." (pp. 79–80)

    What to say back

    You don't repeat everything. Listen for (pp. 80–81):

    • Feelings: body words like "tight" and emotion words like "scared."
    • Present time: what they feel now, not last week.
    • The last thing: after a long stretch, the last thing said is usually most helpful.
    • Anything said twice: repetition means they want to hear it back.

    Two small changes help even more (pp. 81–82). First, turn "I am sad" into "Part of you is sad," which gives the feeling some room. Second, turn "I don't know what this is" into "You're feeling something," which points to the body. Cornell's line: "I don't know" is in the head, but "I feel something" is in the body.

    Guiding, lightly

    Sometimes a partner needs a gentle suggestion, like "Maybe notice how your body feels right now." You may suggest steps in the process, never what to do in their life. Offer one, then go back to listening, and be glad if they say no. (pp. 82–83)

    If you use only one suggestion, Cornell says, make it "Take some time to say hello to that." (p. 84)

    Another useful one is coming back to the body. When your partner feels lost, or drifts into thoughts, images, or a flood of emotion, you might suggest that they notice how their body feels right now. (pp. 84–85)

    And if they can't accept a feeling, suggest they say hello to the part that can't. (p. 86)

    The listener also doesn't ask questions directly. Instead they suggest a question the Focuser can ask inside: "You might ask it, 'What gets it so sad?'" Direct questions pull the Focuser out of their inner process. (pp. 86–87)

    Try it: listener practice

    Each line is something a Focuser says. Pick the best reply a Focusing listener could give.

    A common mix-up"A good listener gives good advice." In a Focusing partnership, advice and analysis get in the way and wear you out. Cornell says it's more relaxing and more helpful to remember that the Focuser is in charge, and your job is to be present and say back. (pp. 78–79)

    Check yourself

    10. In therapy, from both chairs

    In this part: how therapists can invite Focusing, and how clients can ask for it.

    Not a therapist? The ideas below show part 1's research from the other chair. If you'd rather skip ahead, the section for clients is near the end.

    Words to know

    The edge of experience is Gendlin's term for those moments of groping for words for something felt right now. An attunement is a short spoken guide into body awareness at the start of a session. (pp. 93, 97)

    For therapists: "easy" and "tough" clients

    Every therapist has clients who move and clients who seem stuck. Cornell suggests stuck clients are often out of touch with the source of change. They are either analyzing in their heads or caught in repetitive emotion. (pp. 89–90)

    Her claim: thoughts and emotions can come with change, but the source of change is the body as sensed in the present moment. (p. 90)

    She suggests building a bridge in three steps (pp. 90–91):

    • Own perspective: after a story about others, ask "And how was that for you?"
    • Present time: "Are you feeling that right now?"
    • The body: "Where in your body do you feel that?"

    Some phrases may sound odd in an office. One therapist found "say hello to it" awkward; "You might want to acknowledge that" works too. (p. 92)

    Listening for the edge

    This is the research from part 1, put to work. When a client slows down and gropes for words, that's the edge. Clients often rush past it toward something clearer. The therapist can help them stay. (p. 93)

    Cornell's clue: words like "think" and "guess," and pop-psychology labels like "denial" or "resistant," usually come from the head. Halting, groping description usually comes from a felt sense. Reflect the second kind (say it back, as in part 9), not the first. (pp. 93–94)

    Appreciating "No"

    When a client says "No, that's not quite right," Cornell says not to read it as resistance. It means the client is in touch with something more than the words caught, and trusts you enough to keep sensing. (pp. 94–95)

    Because a therapist's words carry extra weight, she suggests inviting clients to check the therapist's words, too, against their body. (p. 94)

    Too little feeling, or too much

    Too little: try feeling a difference. Is the blank in your throat different from the blank in your chest? Or look for something positive, like open or peaceful. (pp. 95–96)

    Too much: use dis-identification. "I'm so terrified!" becomes "There's a part of you that's so terrified." The feeling stays just as strong, but there's a witness now. (pp. 96–97)

    More tools

    An attunement guides the client's attention from hands and feet into the middle of the body, then asks "What's here now?" Speak slowly. (pp. 97–98)

    Clearing a space asks "What's in the way of feeling really wonderful about your life?" Each thing is set down. To choose one, imagine a magnet in the middle of your body and notice which pulls most. (p. 98)

    Therapists can also help clients be gentle with themselves, and build a caring relationship with a shy or tender felt sense, without needing to picture it as an "inner child." (pp. 99–100)

    Inner awareness should finally lead to new action. When a client feels it's time, the therapist can help them sense a commitment in the body and let concrete steps come. (pp. 100–101)

    The therapist's own felt senses matter too. Staying aware of your body can sharpen intuition and help you notice your own reactions. Cornell's closing line for therapists is that clients can grow only as much as you do. (pp. 102–103)

    For clients: training your therapist

    The last chapter speaks to people in therapy. You can ask for what Focusing needs, even if your therapist has never heard of it (pp. 105–107):

    • Start some sessions by quietly sensing in your body.
    • Ask for times of quiet while you find words.
    • Treat confusion as a signal to slow down and sense.
    • Feel free to say "No, that's not quite right." You, not the therapist, are responsible for your life.

    Between sessions, when strong feelings come at awkward times, say hello and acknowledge there's a good reason for them. If you can't stay with them right now, make an inner appointment, and keep it. (pp. 107–108)

    She lists signs of healing: feeling things when they happen instead of days later, trusting your own view, holding mixed feelings, and being able to feel happy without waiting for disaster. (pp. 108–109)

    Cornell expects progress to go "two steps forward, one step back." Old patterns may come back strongly right before they let go. Over time, she says, Focusing helps you become your own primary support. (pp. 108–109)

    Why would "You think this is denial" be a less helpful reply than "You're feeling something vague"?

    Reflecting the head's label keeps the client analyzing. Reflecting the vague, felt part invites them to stay at the edge, where the book says change happens. (p. 94)

    About the source

    Ann Weiser Cornell, The Power of Focusing: A Practical Guide to Emotional Self-Healing. Oakland, CA: New Harbinger Publications, 1996. ISBN 978-1-57224-044-5. The book at New Harbinger.

    Cornell first met Focusing at Eugene Gendlin's Chicago meetings in 1972. She found it hard to learn, and learned it with the help of friends. She later taught alongside Gendlin, and wrote this book as her teaching manual after fifteen years of workshops. (pp. vii–viii)

    What this guide simplifies or leaves out

    • The research is told briefly. The book describes Gendlin's therapy-tape studies in a few pages, with no numbers or citations. This guide repeats that account and adds nothing to it. From outside the book: the researchers rated clients' talk with a scale later called the Experiencing Scale. The book lists a professional paper on that topic (Hendricks, 1986) in its resources.
    • Strong claims are the author's. Lines like "I've never seen it fail" come from Cornell's teaching experience, not from controlled tests. The guide marks them as her claims.
    • Stories are case reports. The healing stories in Chapters Six and Seven are anecdotes. Cornell herself notes that physical symptoms often don't change.
    • The step phrases are Cornell's. The sixteen short phrases in the guided session and the ordering activity are quoted from her summary list (p. 44). The instructions and tips around them are condensed. Other short quotations are marked and cited.
    • Chapters Ten and Eleven are condensed. The therapist chapter has many more sample dialogues than shown here.
    • Left out: the book's resource list of organizations and readings.

    The client lines in parts 1 and 10, the Focuser lines in the part 9 practice, and the word-offering toy in part 5 were written for this guide. They are modeled closely on the book's own examples (pp. 33, 80–84, 93–94), but reworded.
