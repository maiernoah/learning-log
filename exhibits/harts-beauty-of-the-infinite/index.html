A guided course on David Bentley Hart, The Beauty of the Infinite (2003)

Can you persuade someone without forcing them?

That question sits underneath a long, demanding book of theology. A group of modern philosophers answer no: every sermon, argument, and sales pitch is a quiet grab for power. David Bentley Hart, an Eastern Orthodox theologian, answers yes, and his reason is beauty.

This course walks through his case in ten modules and gives his opponents their best lines too. You don't have to agree with him. You do have to understand him well enough to argue.

Before you start: is every attempt to persuade someone a kind of power play?

Your answer stays in this browser. The last section asks again.

Why this order: trying to answer before studying improves later recall even when the first attempt fails (Richland, Kornell & Kao, 2009). For teachers

One module per class session works well. The Pushback panels make good debate prompts, and the Explain it boxes work as exit tickets. Run the mixed quiz as a warm-up on later days to space the practice. Nothing here is graded or sent anywhere; answers stay in each student's browser.

Hart's book is written for graduate readers. The summaries here simplify, and they mark the places where Hart's reading of history or of other thinkers is disputed.

Module 1 · Introduction

The Question

Christianity calls itself a message of peace. A group of modern thinkers say every message is secretly a grab for power. Hart's whole book answers them.

Checked

Key terms

Gospel
"Good news." The Christian message about Jesus.
Rhetoric
The art of persuading. Hart thinks Christianity is rhetoric through and through.
Postmodern
Hart's loose label for thinkers suspicious of any big story that claims to explain everything.
Totality
The world pictured as a closed system that explains itself.
Infinity
In Hart's sense, the world received as a gift from a God beyond it.

Warm-up

Two people want you at their party. Ana says, "Come, or I'll tell everyone your secret." Ben shows you photos from last year: the music, the food, the view from the roof at sunset. Both are trying to change your mind.

Is Ben's way just a nicer version of Ana's?
Hold on to your answer. A whole school of modern philosophy says Ben is doing what Ana does, only more cleverly. Hart thinks Ben's kind of persuasion can be real peace, and he gives the difference a name: beauty. If you picked "it depends on Ben's motive," you've already found the place where the two sides fight.

Picture it

Ana works by threat. Ben works by showing you something good and leaving the choice with you. Hart's book asks which kind of persuader Christianity is.

His opponents say Ben is Ana with better manners. The photos are a softer kind of pressure, and the goal is still to get his way. Hart says Ben's kind of persuasion can be the real thing, and the rest of the book tries to show how.

The idea

Christians describe their faith as a gospel of peace: reconciliation with God and with one another, offered as a gift.

Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) proposed that beneath every value lies a will to power, a drive to dominate. Later thinkers often grouped as postmodern (Jacques Derrida, Gilles Deleuze, Michel Foucault, Jean-François Lyotard) turned this into a habit of suspicion. Take any sermon, law, or argument apart, they say, and you'll find a strategy of control. If they are right, Christian preaching is a fraud: the friendly face of an empire.

Hart grants that the challenge is fair. He admits Christianity's historical record is mixed. He also says the Christian message invites the test, because it claims that the world is ruled by violence and that one man, Jesus of Nazareth, has overcome it.

Then he makes his move. Christianity has never mainly won people with airtight proofs. It tells a story, points to a person, and asks people to see that person as good and lovely. So the real question is whether beauty can persuade without violence. Hart says yes, on one condition: beauty has to run through the whole Christian story, from God to creation to salvation to the end of time, without contradiction. If beauty is only packaging, it's a trick.

How Hart argues. He thinks no neutral logic can settle a contest between whole stories about reality. So he tries to show that the Christian story hangs together and is more beautiful, and lets that do the persuading.

Try it

In his Introduction Hart lists six features of beauty. Read each one, guess what it means, then tap to check your guess.

Pushback

The strongest objection

Beauty gets used to manipulate all the time. Advertising runs on it. So does propaganda, from royal portraits to the staged rallies of the 1930s. Hart himself admits that beauty can sit beside atrocity: flowers growing over killing fields, music played in death camps. Why trust beauty at all?

Hart's reply

He agrees that beauty in general proves nothing. His claim is narrower. The particular beauty in the Christian story has the shape of a person who wins by giving himself away. So Hart owes his readers a demonstration that this beauty stays peaceful at every step. Part 2 of the book is that demonstration.

Your turn

Is the narrower claim enough? What would count as evidence for or against it?

Check yourself

Explain it

Try a sentence or two first. A rough attempt helps you remember, even if it's wrong. Tap again to see it anyway.

A model answer

The suspicion says: whenever someone tries to convince you, ask what they gain. A school assembly about "community values" might really be about keeping students easy to manage. Nietzsche's followers apply this test to everything, religion included. On their reading, Christian talk of love and humility is a clever way for the powerless to gain control over the powerful.

Check yours: did you name the hidden goal (power) and show how a nice-sounding message could serve it?

Part 1 · Thesis

Dionysus against the Crucified

Hart spends the first part of the book describing the story his opponents tell: a world whose deepest truth is conflict. He wants to understand it from the inside before he answers it.

Module 2 · Part 1, section I

Two Stories of the World

Hart thinks every big picture of reality tells one of two stories about the deepest level: a story of violence or a story of peace.

Checked

Key terms

Ontology
The study of being: what it means for anything to exist at all.
Metaphysics
Hart uses it loosely for any big theory of what is real at the deepest level.
Apollo
Greek god of order, reason, and form; patron of the walled city.
Dionysus
Greek god of wine, ecstasy, and the wild country outside the walls.
Privation
Evil understood as a lack, like a hole in fabric: real damage with no substance of its own.

Warm-up

Imagine the universe at its deepest level. Which picture feels closer to the truth?
There's no wrong answer here. Hart says these two pictures are the two great stories people tell about reality, and that you can't prove either one from evidence alone. The rest of the book is about which one deserves belief.

Picture it

Picture an ancient Greek city: walls, laws, temples to Apollo. Outside lies the wild country of Dionysus. The city survives by holding the wild back, by force when it has to: soldiers, punishments, executions. Peace inside the walls is a truce enforced against the chaos outside.

Old philosophy mostly stood on the walls and sided with order. Many postmodern thinkers cheer for the wild. Hart's point is that both keep the same map, with order and chaos locked in a struggle that never ends. He compares them to two mirrors facing each other: the corridor between them looks endless and only repeats one image.

The idea

The theologian John Milbank (Theology and Social Theory, 1990) named this map an ontology of violence: the belief that conflict is the deepest truth, and peace is always a pause between battles. Hart builds on Milbank's diagnosis.

Hart traces the map back to two early Greek thinkers. Parmenides taught that true being is one and unchanging. Heraclitus taught that everything flows and that war is the father of all things. Hart reads them as the two ends of one picture. Western philosophy mostly sided with Parmenides and order; the postmodern turn sides with Heraclitus and flux. Turning a picture upside down leaves the same picture.

Christianity, Hart argues, tells a different story. In Genesis there is no chaos God has to defeat. God creates freely, from nothing, and looks at the result: "behold, it was very good" (Genesis 1:31). Violence enters afterward, as sin, an intruder with no right to be there. Peace comes first and violence second. Evil is a privation, like a hole torn in cloth.

Augustine (354–430) set the two stories side by side in The City of God. In Roman legend, Romulus founded Rome by killing his twin Remus for jumping over the boundary line he had marked out. Augustine paired the story with Cain killing Abel. The earthly city belongs to the brother who kills. The city of God belongs to the brother who was killed.

The story of violence
  • Conflict is basic.
  • Peace is a truce enforced by power.
  • Order must be imposed on chaos.
The story of peace
  • Goodness is basic.
  • Violence is a wound that can heal.
  • Differences can fit together in harmony.

Try it

Which story does each statement belong to? Drag each one into its box.

  • Every civilization is a fragile wall against the chaos outside it.
  • God looked at what he had made and called it very good.
  • War is the father of all things.
  • Evil is like a hole in fabric: real damage, nothing in itself.
  • Every peace treaty is only a pause in a war that never ends.
  • People's differences can fit together like parts in a choir.
  • The bravest response to life is to celebrate the endless clash of forces.
  • The true city belongs to the victim.

Pushback

The strongest objection

Look at nature: predators eat prey. Look at history: wars everywhere. The story of violence sounds like realism, and the story of peace sounds like wishful thinking.

Hart's reply

Both stories look at the same evidence. The story of peace has a place for violence (as sin and damage), and the story of violence has a place for peace (as a pause). Neither can be proved from the evidence alone, because nobody can stand outside reality and inspect its foundations. Hart admits that at this level reason alone can't decide, and each side has to persuade. Critics call this circular. Defenders call it honest. You'll meet the issue again in Module 10.

Check yourself

Explain it

Try a sentence or two first. A rough attempt helps you remember, even if it's wrong. Tap again to see it anyway.

A model answer

Story of violence: In our group project two people clashed, and the final version was whatever the louder one forced through. The "teamwork" was a truce between competing egos.

Story of peace: The group was meant to combine different strengths, and the clash was a breakdown of that purpose. Their ideas could have fit together, and the fight was the thing that needed fixing.

Check yours: in the first telling, is conflict the truth about groups? In the second, is conflict a failure of what groups are for?

Module 3 · Part 1, section II

The Veil of the Sublime

Many postmodern thinkers say the deepest truth can't be pictured or put into words. Hart thinks they've made the formless the master of the beautiful.

Checked

Key terms

Sublime
The experience of something too vast or powerful to take in.
Unrepresentable
What can't be captured in an image, an idea, or words.
Différance
Derrida's coined word: meaning keeps differing and being put off, never fully arriving.
Immanence
Everything lies inside the world; nothing stands beyond it.

Warm-up

Think of something too big to take in: a storm at sea, a canyon, the night sky far from city lights. What was the feeling closer to?
Philosophers call the overwhelming version the sublime. The question for this module is which feeling tells the truth about reality. Hart's opponents say the overwhelming one does. Hart says the two belong together, and that delight is the larger of them.

Picture it

A garden, and a thunderstorm seen from a cliff edge. The garden is beautiful: shaped, graspable, pleasing. The storm is sublime: too big and powerful to take in, thrilling and a little frightening. Immanuel Kant separated the two in his Critique of Judgment (1790). For Kant, the sublime first overwhelms your imagination, then makes you aware that your mind has a power nature can't crush.

Now picture beauty as a painted curtain and the sublime as the storm behind it. Many postmodern thinkers say the storm is the truth and the curtain is a pleasant lie.

The idea

Hart sorts these thinkers into four "narratives of the sublime." He admits the sorting is his own and somewhat arbitrary. Each names a different hidden truth behind the curtain.

The differential sublime
Jacques Derrida

Meaning never fully arrives. Every word points to other words, forever. Derrida's spelling différance, with an a, names this constant differing and delay.

Hart's worry: Derrida still describes the origin of meaning as a kind of violence.

The cosmological sublime
Gilles Deleuze, Michel Foucault

Beneath the world's order churns a chaos of forces. Deleuze celebrates it. Foucault traces how power shapes what people accept as knowledge.

Hart's worry: if force is the final truth, nothing in principle separates a hospice from a death camp except preference.

The ontological sublime
Martin Heidegger, Jean-Luc Nancy

Being shows itself only by hiding. Things arrive and pass away in an event no one controls.

Hart's worry: existence becomes fate, with destruction built into it.

The ethical sublime
Emmanuel Levinas

The other person is infinitely beyond anything I can grasp, and their face commands me before I can think.

Hart's worry: an Other with no features can be obeyed, but not loved.

According to Hart, all four share one assumption: beauty belongs to the surface and can't be trusted. The truth is formless, and every form covers it over. So beauty is at best a rest stop and at worst a lie.

Hart's alternative: the infinite has form. In the Christian God, infinity is a fullness of love and beauty. The sublime still exists, as the experience of more beauty than you can take in. It sits inside beauty as one of its moments.

Try it

Match each statement to the thinker whose "narrative of the sublime" it describes. Try it without looking back at the cards above.

Pushback

In fairness to Levinas

Hart's harshest pages are aimed at Emmanuel Levinas, so hear Levinas's side first. Levinas was a Jewish philosopher whose parents and brothers were murdered in the Holocaust. He had watched ideologies sort human beings into categories and then destroy them. He wanted an ethics no system could swallow: a demand that comes from another person before any theory gets a chance to label them. That's why he said the best way to meet someone is not even to notice the color of their eyes.

Hart's reply

A demand from a faceless Other can be obeyed, but it can't be loved. Real responsibility, Hart says, often begins exactly when you notice the color of someone's eyes. Many scholars think Hart's reading of Levinas is unfair and far too severe.

Your turn

Does noticing details about a person help you see them clearly, or tempt you to put them in a box?

Check yourself

Explain it

Try a sentence or two first. A rough attempt helps you remember, even if it's wrong. Tap again to see it anyway.

Two model answers

Agree: At the end of To Kill a Mockingbird, Scout stands on Boo Radley's porch and sees the street from his side. Up close he stops being a neighborhood legend and becomes a particular person, and that's when she feels responsible to him.

Disagree: Noticing details can trigger stereotypes. A judge or a teacher may treat people more fairly by setting appearance aside, which is Levinas's worry.

Either side works if your example shows how seeing (or not seeing) the particular person changes what you owe them.

Module 4 · Part 1, section III

Nietzsche's Challenge

Nietzsche set Dionysus against the Crucified. Hart thinks Nietzsche understood how strange Christianity is better than most Christians do, and then judged it with bad taste.

Checked

Key terms

Will to power
Nietzsche's name for life's basic drive: to grow, overcome, and dominate.
Ressentiment
French for "resentment." The grudge of the weak against the strong, dressed up as morality.
Slave revolt in morality
Nietzsche's claim that the weak flipped the values of the strong, calling meekness good and strength evil.
Platonism
The belief that the true world is a separate world of perfect, unchanging ideas beyond this one.

Warm-up

Have you ever seen someone use "being nice" as a way to win?
Nietzsche built a whole theory on that experience. He thought Christian love was, at bottom, "being nice" as a weapon. Keep your own examples in mind as you read his case, and ask whether they prove what he thinks they prove.

Picture it

Two gods. Dionysus, Greek god of wine and wild ecstasy, is torn apart in myth and reborn, like a vine cut back to the root that grows again each spring. For Nietzsche he stands for life saying yes to itself, cruelty and suffering included. The Crucified is Jesus on the cross, who for Nietzsche stands for life condemned: suffering treated as an accusation against the world.

Nietzsche ended his last book, Ecce Homo (written 1888), by setting them against each other: Dionysus versus the Crucified. Hart borrows the phrase for the title of Part 1.

The idea

Nietzsche's case, at its strongest

  1. A slave revolt in morality. Ancient nobles called strength, pride, health, and beauty good. The weak couldn't beat them, so they flipped the values: humility, pity, and meekness became good, and the strong became evil. Christianity spread this revolt across the world (On the Genealogy of Morals, 1887).
  2. Ressentiment. Behind Christian love sits the bitter grudge of the powerless.
  3. Will to power. Life is growth, conquest, and expansion. A morality that shelters the weak works against life.
  4. Hatred of the body. Christianity invents a soul and another world so that people will despise this one. Nietzsche called it Platonism for the people.
  5. The church betrayed Jesus. Jesus himself, Nietzsche says in The Antichrist, was a gentle dreamer who lived in an inner world of peace and could found nothing. There was only one Christian, and he died on the cross.

Hart takes all of this seriously. He says theology owes Nietzsche a debt, because Nietzsche saw how shocking the Christian message really was: a God who dies like a slave, and good news for the weak.

Hart's replies

  1. The will to power is a story too. "Life is essentially overpowering" is a claim about the deepest level of reality. That is metaphysics, the very thing Nietzsche claims to have left behind.
  2. Christianity loves the world. Creation is called good, the body will be raised, and the central rite is a shared meal of bread and wine. The body-haters of the ancient world were mostly pagan philosophers and gnostics.
  3. Two wines. Compare them in the activity below.
  4. Nietzsche's Jesus is made up. The Jesus of the Gospels drives the money changers out of the temple, argues with sharp irony, and makes demands. A passive dreamer doesn't fit the texts.
  5. In the end, it's taste. The Gospels record that after Peter denied knowing Jesus, "he went out, and wept bitterly" (Matthew 26:75). Classical literature rarely treated a common man's tears as serious; they were usually material for comedy. The literary critic Erich Auerbach made the same point in Mimesis (1946). The Gospel treats Peter's grief as grave, and that reversal of taste is what Nietzsche hated. Hart's verdict:
Nietzsche had atrocious taste.Hart, end of Part 1, section III

Try it

First, tap each wine to compare them.

Hart adds a joke: Nietzsche didn't drink, so he couldn't judge either vintage.

Now run a debate round. For each of Nietzsche's accusations, pick the reply Hart actually gives.

Pushback

The strongest objection

If the contest comes down to taste, Hart hasn't won anything. He has announced his preferences, and Nietzsche announced his.

Hart's reply

He accepts part of this. There is no neutral proof here. He thinks the Christian story can make sense of Nietzsche's story (as one more tale of power), while Nietzsche's story can only dismiss Jesus as impossible. He also concedes a great deal: Christians have often acted from resentment, and the word ressentiment hits home for a Christian conscience.

Your turn

Is "my story can explain yours, and yours can't explain mine" a good reason to prefer a story?

Check yourself

Explain it

Try a sentence or two first. A rough attempt helps you remember, even if it's wrong. Tap again to see it anyway.

A model answer

Nietzsche: Christianity is what happens when people who can't win decide to make winning wicked. Calling humility a virtue lets the weak feel superior to the strong, and it drags everyone toward a smaller, sicklier life.

Hart: "Life is will to power" is a belief about reality that nobody has proved, and the Gospels' Jesus doesn't fit Nietzsche's picture of a weak dreamer. The Christian story treats creation, the body, and even a fisherman's tears as precious, and Nietzsche's dislike of that is a matter of taste.

Module 5 · Part 1, section IV

The Christian Interruption

Hart's version of Western history: Christianity broke into Greek philosophy with the idea that the world is a free gift, and modern philosophy has been fighting that idea ever since.

Checked

Key terms

Creation ex nihilo
Latin for "from nothing." God makes the world without using anything that already existed.
Transcendence
God's being beyond the world as its source, never one thing among others.
Necessity
What must be, and could not have been otherwise.
Nominalism
A late medieval movement that stressed God's sheer will and treated only individual things as real.

Warm-up

A paycheck and a birthday present can both hand you $50. What's the difference?
Hart's whole reading of history turns on that difference. If the world exists the way a paycheck exists (because it must), you study it like a machine. If it exists the way a present does (because someone wanted to give it), the right first response is gratitude.

Picture it

A clock and a present. A clock's gears turn because they must; remove one gear and the whole thing stops. A present exists because someone wanted to give it. Much Greek philosophy pictured the world like the clock: always existing, unable to be other than it is. The Jewish and Christian teaching of creation from nothing pictures it like the present: it didn't have to exist, and it exists because God freely wanted it.

The idea

Hart calls this a Christian "interruption" of Western thought. Three ideas came with it.

  1. The world is a gift. Nothing forced God to create, and God gains nothing by creating. The world's existence is pure generosity.
  2. God is truly beyond the world. In much ancient philosophy, the highest principle was the top rung of the cosmic ladder. In Christian thought, as Hart reads it, God is the source of the whole ladder's being. That also makes God closer to each thing than it is to itself; Augustine said God is more inward than his inmost self (Confessions 3.6.11).
  3. Knowing starts in wonder. If the world is a gift, you know it best by first receiving it with delight. Hart puts it strongly: beauty is where true knowledge begins and ends.

Then comes what Hart sees as a long forgetting. Late medieval nominalists pictured God as raw, all-powerful will, more like a giant ruler. René Descartes (Meditations, 1641) set out to find certainty by shutting his eyes and ears and doubting his senses; truth would start from the thinking self. Immanuel Kant argued that we know things only as our own minds shape them. Kant called this his Copernican revolution. Hart says it should be called Ptolemaic, after the old earth-centered model of the universe, because it puts the human knower back at the center.

G. W. F. Hegel then pictured God and history as one great process pushed forward by conflict. After him, Nietzsche and Heidegger described being as a war of forces or a fated coming and going. Hart's summary: modern philosophy is theology's rebellious child. It inherited Christianity's questions and keeps trying to answer them without God.

Contested. This is Hart's reading of history, and historians argue about it. Some think it gives nominalism too much blame. Many admirers of Descartes and Kant see their caution as honesty after the scientific revolution showed that the senses can mislead.

Try it

The steps of Hart's history are scrambled. Drag them into order, earliest at the top, then check.

  1. Plato: the true world is made of perfect, unchanging Forms; this world is their shadow.
  2. The Christian interruption: creation from nothing, the Trinity, a God beyond the world (Gregory of Nyssa, Augustine).
  3. Nominalism: God pictured as raw will; the world's beauty cut loose from God.
  4. Descartes: shut your eyes; certainty starts from the thinking self.
  5. Kant: we know things only as our minds shape them.
  6. Hegel: God and history as one process driven forward by conflict.
  7. Nietzsche and Heidegger: being as a war of forces, or a fated coming and going.

Pushback

The strongest objection

After Galileo, people learned that the senses can fool you: the earth feels still, and it moves. Starting from what you can be sure of looks like intellectual honesty. Descartes and Kant were being careful.

Hart's reply

Every act of knowing rests on trust, he says, borrowing an idea from the eighteenth-century thinker J. G. Hamann. You trust your senses, your language, and your teachers. Trying to doubt everything first moves all your trust onto yourself.

Check yourself

Explain it

Try a sentence or two first. A rough attempt helps you remember, even if it's wrong. Tap again to see it anyway.

A model answer

If the world exists by necessity, it's a machine, and the best way to know it is to take it apart and control it. If it's a gift, it says something about the giver, and the best way to know it starts with gratitude and delight. Hart thinks modern philosophy chose the machine and lost the ability to see the world as beautiful.

Part 2 · Story

The Beauty of the Infinite

Now Hart tells the Christian story himself, in four moments that follow the Nicene Creed (381): Trinity, Creation, Salvation, and Eschaton (the last things). He notes that the early church learned them in the opposite order. Easter came first; then the church worked out what salvation means, then what creation is, and last, who God must be.

Module 6 · Part 2, I: Trinity

The Infinite Is Beautiful

Christians say God is three persons sharing one life of love. Hart argues that this makes God's infinity full of form and delight, and so beautiful.

Checked

Key terms

Trinity
One God who is Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.
Perichoresis περιχώρησις
pair-ee-kho-RAY-sis. The way the three persons live in one another.
Apatheia ἀπάθεια
ah-pah-THAY-ah. God's life is so full that nothing outside can wound it or add to it.
Epektasis ἐπέκτασις
ep-EK-ta-sis. The soul's endless stretching toward God.
Theophany
A showing of God.

Warm-up

Which picture of love fits better?
Hart's God is the fountain: already complete, and giving anyway. Human love is usually both: it needs and it gives. Watch how Hart uses the difference.

Picture it

Picture three dancers so perfectly in step that they share one motion, while each stays distinct. The dance needs no audience to be complete. If it invites an audience onto the floor, that's generosity.

The early church's word for this shared life is perichoresis. It comes from a Greek verb meaning "to make room" or "to contain." It also sounds like the Greek word for dancing, and theologians, Hart included, enjoy the pun.

The idea

  1. God is love because God is three. The Father gives himself wholly to the Son, the Son answers, and the Spirit is the joy and overflow of that love. So delight, regard, and friendship exist inside God before any world exists.
  2. The baptism of Jesus. As Jesus comes up from the water, a voice says: "Thou art my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased" (Mark 1:11). Hart calls this scene a theophany: the Father speaks, the Son is addressed, the Spirit descends like a dove. When God speaks his own Word, what he says is pleasure. Delight sits at the center of God.
  3. Apatheia. The English word apathy comes from this Greek word, which misleads readers. Hart uses it to say that God's life is so full of love and joy that nothing outside can wound it or add to it. God loved us before we existed. Hart insists on this because a God who needed history to become himself would make every evil in history part of God's own story. He borrows a test from Ivan Karamazov in Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov (1880): would you accept a final harmony that required the torture of one child? A God built out of history's suffering fails that test.
  4. The infinite is beautiful. Most Greek philosophers treated the infinite (apeiron, "the boundless") as shapeless and bad. Gregory of Nyssa (c. 335–c. 395) called God infinite in the best sense: endless goodness, endless beauty. Plotinus (c. 204–270) had already called the One infinite in a positive way. Hart argues Gregory's infinite differs because it is the Trinity: full of form, relation, and love.
  5. Epektasis. Paul writes of "reaching forth unto those things which are before" (Philippians 3:13). Gregory built a spirituality on that verb. Since God is infinite, the soul never reaches the end of God, and that is the joy of it: every fulfillment opens a larger desire. Gregory's image is a vessel that grows as it is filled. Gregory's point: the more the soul receives of God, the more it is able to receive. You never reach "full," and that is good news, because the thing you want has no end.

Pushback

The strongest objection

Many modern theologians (Jürgen Moltmann, Robert Jenson, and others influenced by Hegel) argue that a God who can't suffer can't really love. After the Holocaust, they say, we need a God who suffers with us.

Hart's reply

In Jesus, God the Son really suffers and dies in his human nature. God's divine life remains untouched, and Hart says that's the good news: only a God who is not trapped inside suffering can heal it.

Go deeper: Rahner's rule

The Catholic theologian Karl Rahner (1967) gave modern theology a rule: the Trinity we meet in history (the Father sending the Son, the Spirit descending) is the Trinity as God really is. Hart accepts the rule with one warning. It must not be read as saying God becomes God through history. God acts in history fully as himself, and he would be the same God without a world.

A model answer

Normally, filling something ends your wanting: a glass of water fills and you stop. Gregory says that with God, receiving makes you able to receive more, so desire never gets bored or used up. Learning guitar feels like this: each song I learn lets me hear more in other songs, and I want to learn harder ones.

Module 7 · Part 2, II: Creation

Gift, Music, Language

Hart describes creation three ways: as a gift God didn't need to give, as music, and as speech.

Checked

Key terms

Agape and eros
Greek words for self-giving love and desiring love. Hart says real love includes both.
Logos λόγος
"Word" or "reason." In John's Gospel, Christ is the Word through whom all things were made.
Analogy of being
Creatures resemble God within an ever greater unlikeness.
Variation
In music, a new version of a theme that changes it while keeping it recognizable.

Warm-up

When someone gives you a gift, do you owe them something?
The philosopher Jacques Derrida thought this question makes a pure gift impossible: even a thank-you pays the giver back. Hart disagrees, and his answer tells you a lot about how he pictures God.

Picture it

Hold three images side by side. A present, which nobody had to give. A piece of music, where a short theme grows into many variations. And a sentence spoken aloud, where every word means something because of the speaker. Hart uses all three for creation.

The idea

Creation as gift

Creation adds nothing to God; Revelation says all things were created "for thy pleasure" (Revelation 4:11). Derrida (Given Time, 1991) argued that any gift creates a debt, so a truly pure gift would have to be given by no one, to no one, without anyone noticing.

Hart answers that the puzzle assumes a real gift must be free of desire. Real gifts come from wanting the other person, and they hope for a response. A gift that expects nothing at all is closer to indifference. Real gifts are also given again and again: an engagement ring means little without years of giving that follow it. In God, giving and receiving are one motion: the Father gives everything to the Son, and the Son gives everything back. Creation is let into that circle. That's why Hart rejects the old split between agape (self-giving love) and eros (desire), made famous by the Swedish theologian Anders Nygren in the 1930s.

Creation as music

Gregory of Nyssa called creation a hymn. Hart's favorite example is J. S. Bach. In the Goldberg Variations (1741), a short aria is followed by thirty variations on its bass line, and every third variation is a canon. Then the aria returns, and after everything in between it sounds deeper. Hart thinks the music of creation needs no chaos underneath it. Evil is a discord, damage to the music, with no place in the score. In his picture the Spirit works like a composer who can bring any line back into harmony.

Creation as language

"And God said, Let there be light: and there was light" (Genesis 1:3). Each creature is like a word spoken within God's eternal Word. That's why we can speak about God using words from creation (good, wise, beautiful, father) while knowing the words always fall short. Hart calls this the analogy of being: creatures resemble God within an ever greater unlikeness. Talk about God, he says, should sound more like praise than like a dictionary.

Try it

Theme and variations. These are the first eight bass notes of the Goldberg aria, with simple variations written for this course. Listen to the theme, then each variation, then the theme's return.

GF♯EDBCDG

Sound starts when you press a button. In Hart's picture, a dissonance that resolves belongs to the music; sin is a discord that refuses to resolve.

Pushback

Derrida's side

A gift that "hopes for a response" sounds like the very debt Derrida worried about. Love can turn possessive.

Hart's reply

He separates a debt, which you must pay, from a love you are invited to return freely. He grants that sin turns giving into control all the time, and he calls that a corruption of giving.

The problem of evil

If creation is God's music, why is there so much noise: disease, disaster, cruelty? Hart answers that evil is privation, damage to the music, and he refuses to call suffering a necessary part of the harmony. He wrote a short book on this after the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, The Doors of the Sea (2005).

A model answer

The trail behind my house looks different every season: snow, mud, wildflowers, dry grass. Each season is a variation, and the trail is the theme. Hart thinks creation works like that: every creature is a fresh variation on God's goodness, none of them repeats another, and together they deepen the theme.

Module 8 · Part 2, III: Salvation

The Story Retold

In Hart's reading, Jesus saves by living the human story rightly from beginning to end, and by giving himself as a gift that violence can't cancel.

Checked

Key terms

Recapitulation
From the Greek anakephalaiōsis, "summing up under one head" (Ephesians 1:10). Christ re-lives humanity's story and gets it right.
Kenosis κένωσις
Self-emptying (Philippians 2:7). Christ "made himself of no reputation."
Scapegoat
A victim whose expulsion or death keeps a group together (René Girard's term for a social pattern).
Atonement
How Christ's life and death reconcile people with God.

Warm-up

Someone owes a debt they can never pay. What should happen?
Western Christians have often explained salvation with the third answer. Hart thinks that answer is true as far as it goes, and that the story is bigger than any accounting. Keep your answer; you'll test it against two kinds of sacrifice below.

Picture it

Imagine a song that everyone learned wrong. Each generation taught it to the next with the same sour notes, until nobody remembered how it went. Then someone sings it again from the first bar, correctly, and teaches it to everyone who will listen. That is Irenaeus's idea of recapitulation (around 180 AD): Christ re-lives the human story from the beginning and gets it right, obedience where Adam disobeyed, faithfulness where humanity broke faith.

12345678

It's the same melody from Module 7's third variation. Listen for the sour notes in bars 2, 4, and 6.

The idea

  1. Christ is the form. Christians don't offer a theory first; they point to a person, a particular Jewish teacher from Nazareth. Hart says no general moral lesson can replace him (remember the sixth feature of beauty).
  2. Pilate's question. At the trial Pilate asks, "What is truth?" (John 18:38). Nietzsche admired Pilate's cool skepticism. Hart reads it as a dodge: Pilate turns from the truth standing in front of him to the one truth he is sure of: "knowest thou not that I have power to crucify thee?" (John 19:10). Power answers persuasion with a cross.
  3. Two kinds of sacrifice. In the first, a society keeps order by killing or expelling a victim; the high priest Caiaphas reasons that "it is expedient for us, that one man should die for the people" (John 11:50). In the second, a gift is offered freely to God, who keeps giving back. Israel's worship, Hart argues, was already moving toward the second kind. In the near-sacrifice of Isaac (Genesis 22), the gift is offered and then given back. At the cross both kinds meet: Rome's violence and Christ's self-gift. The resurrection shows which one God sides with.
  4. Anselm reread. Anselm's Why God Became Man (about 1098) is often summarized as: God's honor was offended, so someone had to die to pay. Hart argues Anselm meant something gentler: in God, justice and mercy are one, Christ freely gives a gift worth more than every debt, and the Father hands that gift on to us. Many readers still find Anselm harsher than Hart does.
  5. Against tragedy. Some British theologians (Donald MacKinnon, Nicholas Lash) wanted the cross read like a Greek tragedy, to keep Christians honest about suffering. Hart says tragedy is too comforting. It teaches you to accept loss as the way the universe works. Easter refuses that lesson. It treats death as an enemy that God has reversed, and it gives no death a hidden meaning that makes it acceptable.
Sacrifice as an economy of violence
  • Purpose: keep order.
  • The victim is destroyed and forgotten.
  • God (or the gods) must be paid off.
  • Last word: the cross.
Sacrifice as gift
  • Purpose: draw near to God in love.
  • The giver is given back.
  • God gives first and keeps giving.
  • Last word: the empty tomb.

Try it

Which kind of sacrifice is each of these? Drag each one into its box.

  • "It is expedient that one man should die for the people."
  • Israel's thank offering after a harvest.
  • Rome executing a troublemaker to keep a province quiet.
  • Christ's whole life given to the Father, all the way to the end.
  • A village blames one outsider for a plague and drives him out.
  • Bread and wine shared at the Lord's table.

Pushback

From René Girard's direction

Girard (The Scapegoat, 1982) showed how societies hide their violence behind sacred stories. Any talk of sacrifice risks making God want blood, so Christians should drop sacrificial language altogether.

From the tragic theologians

Easter triumphalism makes Christians glib about suffering ("everything happens for a reason").

Hart's reply

To Girard: sacrifice in the Bible has many meanings, and the gift kind can't be dropped without losing Israel's whole story. To the tragic theologians: he agrees evil has no "reason." That's exactly why he rejects tragedy, which finds a reason (fate, cosmic order) and asks you to accept it.

A model answer

A tragedy says: this is how the world works, so accept it. Easter says the world was never meant to work that way: God raised the murdered man and sided with the victim against the powers that killed him. So every death stays an outrage.

Objection: people are still dying, and Easter happened two thousand years ago. A grieving person might hear Hart's hope as a promise that hasn't been kept.

Module 9 · Part 2, IV: Eschaton

The Last Word

Christian hope looks for a new creation that keeps every good thing. Hart reads hell as the same light of God received by a closed heart.

Checked

Key terms

Eschaton ἔσχατον
"The last thing." The end of history and the new creation.
Kingdom of God
God's rule fully arrived: justice, peace, and joy for all creation.
Resurrection of the body
The Christian hope that people will be raised whole, flesh included.
Universalism
The belief that all will finally be saved.

Warm-up

If the world had a final chapter, what should it do with the earlier chapters?
Hart's answer is the second. The new creation keeps time, bodies, and every particular person, healed. That makes the question of who or what is left out urgent, which is where this module goes.

Picture it

An old saying: the same sun melts wax and hardens clay. The sun stays the same; the material differs. Many Eastern Christian teachers describe heaven and hell this way. There is one divine light. An open heart receives it as beauty. A heart that has sealed itself shut receives the same light as burning.

Same sun, same light. Only the receiving changes.

The idea

  1. The kingdom comes from outside history. "The day of the Lord so cometh as a thief in the night" (1 Thessalonians 5:2). Hart reads this as judgment on history. No political program builds the kingdom, and no "march of progress" earns it.
  2. Hell as refusal. Drawing on the Eastern tradition, Hart describes hell as God's glory received as an overwhelming sublime by a self sealed inward, unable to receive love as gift. Notice how this links back to Module 3.
  3. New creation. Paul writes that "the creature itself also shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption" (Romans 8:21). Augustine said that in the resurrection, God will be seen in all bodies. Hart pictures the kingdom as music that never ends: new variations forever, with the discords gone.
  4. All humanity as God's image. Gregory of Nyssa taught that the "humanity" God made in his image is the whole community of every person across all time. Each person is an irreplaceable part of it. Gregory also taught that all would finally be saved. Hart explains Gregory's universalism in this book and says his church's tradition does not authorize him to argue for it. (He later did argue for it, in That All Shall Be Saved, 2019.)

Pushback

The strongest objection

Most Christian traditions, Catholic, Protestant, and many Orthodox, teach that hell may be final. Critics of universalism also ask about freedom: if everyone is saved in the end, do anyone's choices really matter?

Gregory's reply, as Hart presents it

Freedom is the ability to reach the good. A will that rejects God is sick, and healing it gives it back its freedom. Evil is finite and runs out; God's goodness is infinite.

Your turn

Which picture of freedom do you find more convincing: freedom as choosing among options, or freedom as becoming what you were made to be?

The picture says God stays the same in heaven and in hell; people differ. Everyone meets the same love, and some experience it as joy while others experience it as pain. It gets right that, in this view, God stays loving toward everyone. It leaves unclear whether a closed heart can ever open, which is exactly where universalists and their critics disagree.

Module 10 · Part 3

Persuasion as Peace

Hart argues that the "neutral" suspicion of all big stories has a violence of its own, and that Christian witness can offer real peace if it never uses force.

Checked

Key terms

Hermeneutics
The art of interpretation. Here, a philosophy of permanent suspicion toward big stories.
Metanarrative
A big story that claims to explain all the others. Lyotard defined the postmodern as "incredulity toward metanarratives."
Martyr μάρτυς
Greek for "witness."
The market
In Hart's usage, the culture that turns everything into a price and a choice.

Warm-up

In arguments about the meaning of life, could anyone be a truly neutral referee?
Hart's answer is the third. Every referee brings a view about what counts as a fair rule, and that view is itself one of the positions in the contest.

Picture it

A referee wearing a team jersey. Some philosophers propose a referee for the contest of worldviews: everyone may tell their story, as long as no story claims to be the true story of everything. Hart points at the jersey. "No story is true for everyone" is itself a story about everyone.

The idea

  1. The war of persuasions. Ludwig Wittgenstein noted that when two whole worldviews meet and reasons run out, all that's left is persuasion, and each side tends to call the other a fool or a heretic (On Certainty, written 1949–51).
  2. The suspicious referee. John Caputo's "radical hermeneutics" tries to keep the peace by suspecting every big story. Caputo's goal is good: protect the weak from powerful stories that crush them. Hart's worry is that this quietly demands every faith shrink into a private hobby, and strips people of the traditions that make them who they are.
  3. The market. Who benefits when every big story becomes a private preference? Hart's answer: the global market. Everything gets a price and becomes one option among many. Desire is trained to want nothing in particular and keep shopping. Hart suggests that Nietzsche's will to power ends up looking like consumer choice.
  4. The gift of martyrs. For Hart, Christian persuasion has one legitimate shape: witness. Offer the gift, accept being refused, never force. The risen Christ meets two disciples on the road to Emmaus as a stranger and is recognized when he breaks bread with them (Luke 24). Violence can kill a witness, and in doing so it produces more witnesses.
  5. No synthesis. Hart offers one particular peace, the peace of Christ, and stakes everything on its beauty being persuasive. He admits there is no neutral ground from which to prove it.

Hart's concession. He says Christian history has often abandoned this way of persuading and been seduced by power. Whether Christians actually live by the gift of martyrs is the open question he leaves.

Try it

You are the market. Tap each item to stick a price tag on it. (The prices are invented for the exercise.)

Which tags felt wrong? Hart would say that feeling is the point. Some things can only be known as gifts, and the market can only see them as exchange. That's his case that the market is the real winner when every big story is pushed into private life.

Pushback

The strongest objection

A religion that wants to convert the whole world is still trying to conquer it, just patiently. Caputo's suspicion exists because big stories have crushed minorities again and again.

Hart's reply

The test is method. Force is forbidden; only offering, inviting, and willingness to suffer rejection are allowed. He also rejects two easy exits: political "realism" that accepts violence as normal, and a self-protective pacifism that lets others suffer so its own hands stay clean. Both, he says, give up on the peace Christ actually offers.
