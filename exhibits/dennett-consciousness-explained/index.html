This page teaches Consciousness Explained by Daniel C. Dennett (1991). It is a study guide written in plain language, not a copy of the original. About the source.

Right now it feels as if there is a screen behind your eyes and a "you" watching it. Dennett spent a whole book arguing that this picture is wrong. There is no screen and no watcher inside your head. Yet he insists your experiences are real and need explaining. If he is right, it changes how we think about minds, robots, animals, and ourselves.

By the end you'll be able to

  • Explain what Dennett means by the Cartesian Theater and why he rejects it.
  • Describe the Multiple Drafts model and use it on the color phi experiment.
  • Compare the Orwellian and Stalinesque stories and say why Dennett thinks the difference is empty.
  • Explain why the brain can show events in one order while it receives them in another.
  • Predict how Dennett would answer the inverted spectrum, Mary the color scientist, and the Chinese Room.
  • Explain what he means by calling the self a center of narrative gravity.

1. A story with no author

In this part: see how a brain could make a vivid hallucination without any inner storyteller.

Words to know

  • Hallucination: an experience of something that isn't there, such as seeing a figure in an empty room.
  • Combinatorial explosion: when the number of possible cases grows so fast that no one could prepare for them all.
  • Epistemic hunger: Dennett's phrase for curiosity, the questions a mind is actually asking.

The brain in a vat

Dennett opens with an old puzzle. Suppose evil scientists kept your brain alive in a vat and fed it fake signals. Could they fool you into thinking you were at the beach? (ch. 1.1)

The easy part is passive input: music, warmth, the feel of sand. The hard part starts when you move. Each wiggle of your finger needs new, matching feedback, worked out instantly. Every choice you make branches into more choices. The cases multiply too fast to compute or store. That is a combinatorial explosion. (ch. 1.1)

So, Dennett argues, a fake world you can freely poke and test is out of reach. A "strong" hallucination, like a solid ghost you could walk around and touch, should be rare. Reports of them deserve doubt. (ch. 1.1)

So how do real hallucinations happen?

People do have vivid hallucinations. One clue is that hallucinators tend to hold still and stare. They rarely probe. If the illusion never has to answer your questions, it never has to prepare the answers. (ch. 1.2)

Dennett explains the rest with a party game. One guest leaves the room. He is told that while he is out, someone will describe a recent dream. When he returns, he must work out the dream by asking yes-or-no questions, then use it to guess who dreamed it. In fact no one tells a dream. The others answer by a fixed rule that depends on the last letter of each question. A strange story builds up anyway. (ch. 1.3)

Who wrote that story? In one sense the guest did, through the questions he chose to ask. In another sense no one did, and Dennett says that is the whole point. (ch. 1.3)

Many scientists think perception works by guess and check. The brain forms a guess ("Is that a deer?") and the senses confirm or reject it. Dennett suggests a hallucination is what happens when the checking side starts answering at random. Your guesses come from your hopes and fears, so the story fits them. A hunter on the last day of deer season "sees" a deer where there is only a cow. (ch. 1.3)

This explains three more facts. Hallucinations are common when the senses get no input at all. They usually start simple and grow more detailed. And a drug can't contain a story; it can only change how easily the brain says "yes." (ch. 1.3)

    An analogy, and where it breaksA film set only needs to be built where the camera will point. If the director knows the camera path, the back of the set can stay empty. Dennett makes this comparison himself. It breaks down because a brain doesn't plan anything in advance; it simply never builds what no one asks about. (ch. 1.2)

    Why do hallucinations so often match what the person fears or wants?

    Because the questions come from the person. The random side only supplies yes and no. A worried mind asks worried questions, so the story that builds up is about its worries. No inner playwright needs to know your psychology. (ch. 1.3)

    A common mix-up"A hallucination must be stored somewhere as a finished movie." Dennett's point is the opposite. The detail gets made only as it is asked for. The brain never prepares what no one is curious about. He calls this principle the satisfying of epistemic hunger, and it returns later in the book. (ch. 1.3)

    2. A dot that changes color too soon

    In this part: test a real visual illusion and use it to compare the Cartesian Theater with Dennett's Multiple Drafts model.

    Words to know

    • Color phi: an illusion where two flashing dots of different colors look like one dot moving and changing color.
    • Cartesian Theater: Dennett's name for the idea of one place in the brain where experience "comes together" for an inner viewer.
    • Multiple Drafts model: Dennett's alternative, in which many brain processes interpret input at once and no single version is the final, official one.

    The experiments in this book happen fast. They are measured in milliseconds (ms), thousandths of a second. Dennett gives some benchmarks (ch. 5.1):

    EventAbout how long
    A signal on a fast nerve, fingertip to brain20 ms
    One frame of a movie42 ms
    Starting and stopping a stopwatch as fast as you can175 ms
    Saying one syllable200 ms
    A signal on a slow nerve, fingertip to brain500 ms

    The puzzle

    Flash a dot, then flash a second dot a little to the side. People see one dot move. That is the long-known phi illusion. The philosopher Nelson Goodman asked what happens if the dots are different colors. Paul Kolers and Michael von Grünau tested it in 1976. (ch. 5.2)

    Try it: predict first

    A red dot flashes on the left. Then a green dot flashes on the right. What will people see?

    Try it: the color phi lab

    Rest your eyes on the small cross, press Play, and watch the space between the dots.

    Run the experiment

    Set to the timing Kolers and von Grünau used: 150 ms per dot, 50 ms gap.

    What people see at each gap varies. The ranges in these messages are rough guesses for a screen, not figures from the book.

    In the original experiment, people saw the first dot start to move and then switch color abruptly, about halfway along its path. They could even point to where the switch happened. (ch. 5.2) Dennett adds that with a long enough gap, you see two separate flashes and only infer motion. At some in-between gaps, the two flashes and the motion are seen at once. (ch. 5.3)

    Here is the puzzle. The switch to green appears before the dot reaches the green dot's place. But the brain can't know the second dot will be green until the green light arrives. Goodman asked how the brain could fill in the path before the second flash had even happened. (ch. 5.2) Dennett sharpens the question: did the experience wait for the green dot, or was it rewritten afterward? (ch. 5.4)

    The old answer: a theater

    René Descartes, in the 1600s, thought the senses sent signals to one small organ deep in the brain, the pineal gland. There, a nonphysical mind took them in. That view is dualism. Dennett rejects it because no one can say how something with no mass or energy could push neurons around. Dualism, he says, amounts to giving up on explaining the mind. (ch. 2.4, 5.1)

    Most scientists today are materialists. But Dennett thinks many keep a hidden piece of Descartes: the idea of a finish line somewhere in the brain. Cross it, and a signal becomes conscious, in the order it arrived. He calls this Cartesian materialism, and the imagined place behind that line, where "it all comes together," the Cartesian Theater. (ch. 5.1)

    The problem is that the brain has no such center. Signals travel many paths at different speeds. If signal A beats B to one spot but B beats A to another, which finish line sets the order you experience? There is no answer, because there is no single line. (ch. 5.1)

    Dennett's answer: multiple drafts

    In the Multiple Drafts model, many specialist processes work on the input at the same time. Each one makes its call, such as "something moved here" or "this patch is green," in its own place and time. A call made once doesn't need to be shown again to an inner viewer. (ch. 5.2)

    These calls are edited and revised as new information comes in, like drafts of a story. At any moment there are many partial drafts. What you report depends on when and how you are asked. Dennett calls the asking "probing." No draft counts as the final, official version of what you experienced. (ch. 5.2, 5.5)

    For color phi, the brain simply concludes that a dot moved and changed color. It doesn't need to paint the in-between frames for anyone to watch. The conclusion is already the result. (ch. 5.4)

    Try it: probe the drafts

    Slide the probe to ask the brain, at different moments, "What did you see?" The times are made up to show the idea; they are not measurements. Dennett adds that asking early can itself change what the brain goes on to settle.

    When do you ask?

    Nothing to report yet.

    Why can't the brain show the color switch before the green dot arrives?

    Causes come before effects. No process in the brain can use the information "green" until light from the green dot has reached the eye and been processed. So the content "red turning green partway" must be formed after green has been registered. The puzzle is only how it ends up placed earlier in the story you tell. (ch. 5.2)

    A common mix-up"Dennett says you don't really see the dot move." He doesn't. He grants that it seems to move and that you sincerely judge so. What he denies is that there is a second, inner showing of the motion, on a screen somewhere, in addition to that judgment. (ch. 5.4)

    3. Taking people at their word, but only so far

    In this part: learn Dennett's method for studying experience, which treats what people say like the world of a novel.

    Words to know

    • Introspection: looking inward to report on your own thoughts and feelings.
    • Heterophenomenology: Dennett's method of studying other people's experience through their sincere reports, without assuming those reports are true or false.
    • Zombie: in philosophy, an imagined person who acts exactly like us but has no experience at all.

    Ground rules

    Dennett sets three rules for himself. No "wonder tissue": he won't appeal to unknown forces or substances. No pretending experiences don't happen: if he denies some feature of experience, he must show why it seems to be there. And no fussing over every lab detail, since the science keeps changing. (ch. 2.5)

    We are often wrong about ourselves

    Try this with a deck of cards. Stare at one spot and hold a card far off to the side. You can't tell if it's red or black. Bring it slowly toward the center. It gets surprisingly close before you can name it. Most people are surprised, because the edges of their vision seem sharp and colored. (ch. 3.2)

    Your eyes hide this by darting about, unnoticed, to whatever looks interesting. Other surprises: much of what we call taste is really smell. And we "hear" silent gaps between spoken words, though the sound wave often has no gaps there. (ch. 3.2)

    Dennett concludes that introspection is not simple looking. When we describe our inner lives, we are partly making up a theory on the spot, and we don't notice we are doing it. (ch. 4.1)

    Try it: which one is made up?

    Dennett lists five claims about experience and admits he invented at least one. Four are real findings. Which is the fake?

    Pick the claim you think Dennett invented

    The method

    Science has to work from the outside, with data anyone can check. So Dennett starts with recordings of what subjects say. Typists turn the sounds into text. Then we read the text as sincere speech: claims and answers from someone who believes them. Dennett calls this reading things as an agent with beliefs and desires the intentional stance. (ch. 4.3)

    Next comes the key move. Treat the person's account like a novel. In a Sherlock Holmes story it is true that Holmes lives on Baker Street. We don't ask how the author knew, and we don't argue. The story decides. In the same way, the subject's sincere reports decide how things seem to the subject. Dennett calls the result the subject's heterophenomenological world. (ch. 4.4)

    Dennett compares the scientist to an anthropologist studying a tribe that believes in a forest god called Feenoman. The anthropologist writes down and organizes what the believers say about Feenoman, without believing in him and without challenging the believers. (ch. 4.5)

    The subject is the final authority on how things seem. The subject is not the authority on what is causing those seemings. That second question goes to science. Later we may find brain events that match what people describe, the way the anthropologist might find a real person behind the legend of Feenoman. Or we may find that people were simply mistaken. (ch. 4.6, 4.8)

    The method is neutral on zombies. A zombie that talked just like your friend would produce the same text and the same "world." Heterophenomenology can't tell them apart, and it doesn't pretend to. (ch. 4.8)

    Try it: who gets the last word?

    Sort each statement by who settles it, according to Dennett's method.

    An analogy, and where it breaksA novel's world is fixed by its text. A subject's world is fixed by their reports. The analogy breaks because a novelist knows the story is made up. Subjects sincerely believe what they say. Dennett calls them "unwitting" creators of fiction: their reports are honest accounts of how things seem to them, even where those accounts turn out to be wrong. (ch. 4.7)

    Why treat sincere reports like fiction instead of just believing them?

    Because people are sometimes wrong about their own minds, as the card trick shows. Treating reports as a story lets scientists agree on exactly what needs explaining, without first settling whether it is true. It also lets theorists who disagree about the brain, or even about souls, work from the same data. (ch. 4.4, 4.8)

    A common mix-up"Heterophenomenology says experiences are fake." It doesn't. It sets the question aside at the start. Dennett says the method is a waystation: once we find what the brain is actually doing, we can confirm many reports, perhaps in a slightly stretched sense, and correct others. (ch. 4.5, 4.7)

    4. Orwell or Stalin?

    In this part: compare two ways of explaining an illusion and see why Dennett says no possible evidence could choose between them.

    Words to know

    • Orwellian revision: you really did experience one thing, but your memory was rewritten right afterward.
    • Stalinesque revision: the false version was put together before you became conscious, so you experienced the false version itself.

    Two ways to fake history

    In George Orwell's novel 1984, a government office rewrites old records so that the past seems different. Under Stalin, the Soviet government staged show trials with false evidence and forced confessions. One fakes the record after the fact. The other stages a fake event and records it honestly. In everyday life we can usually tell which happened. (ch. 5.3)

    Now shrink the time scale. A woman with long hair runs past you. A second later, a memory of some other woman, one with glasses, gets mixed in. Asked a minute later, you sincerely say she wore glasses. (ch. 5.3)

    • Orwellian story: For a brief moment you saw her without glasses. Then your memory was rewritten.
    • Stalinesque story: The glasses were added on the way in, before you were conscious of her. You consciously saw glasses from the start.

    Color phi again

    The same two stories fit the dot that changes color. The Stalinesque version says the brain holds the "film" for a moment, splices in the in-between frames, then shows the finished version. The Orwellian version says you saw a red dot and then a green dot, with no motion, and a fraction of a second later your memory was rewritten to include the motion. (ch. 5.3)

    Can an experiment decide? Suppose subjects press a button as soon as they see red. They respond fast, too fast to hide a long delay. That seems to favor Orwell. But the Stalinist replies that the button press was started unconsciously, before the experience. Every result gets a reply like this from one side or the other. (ch. 5.3)

    Dennett concludes that this is not a mystery we are too weak to solve. It is a difference that makes no difference. The two theorists can agree on every event in the brain, in order. They disagree only about where to draw one line: which events count as "before consciousness" and which as "after." At this scale, nothing in the brain marks such a line. (ch. 5.3)

    He compares it to publishing in the age of email. Drafts of a paper circulate and get revised. Most readers see an early draft. Which version was "the published one"? You can pick one, but the choice is arbitrary. (ch. 5.3)

    Try it: move the line

    The brain events below stay fixed. Slide the dashed line that marks "the moment of consciousness" and see which story you are telling.

    Where do you draw the line?

    The button press comes fast.They were briefly conscious of plain red.The press started before consciousness.
    The subject is sure it moved from the start.Of course; the rewrite leaves no trace.Of course; that is what they saw.

    Why can't the subject settle it from the inside?

    Both stories predict the same inner feel. A wrongly built experience, remembered accurately, and a correct experience, remembered wrongly, would seem exactly alike to the person. So no amount of careful introspection could tell them apart. (ch. 5.3)

    A common mix-up"Dennett thinks we just don't have good enough tools yet." No. His claim is stronger: if there is no Cartesian Theater, there is no fact for better tools to find. The Orwell/Stalin question only makes sense if there is one finish line where things become conscious. (ch. 5.3, 5.4)

    5. How the brain keeps time

    In this part: separate when a brain event happens from what time it is about, and use that to explain some strange timing experiments.

    Words to know

    • Metacontrast: when a brief shape is followed fast by a second shape, people often report only the second.
    • Cutaneous rabbit: an illusion where taps at a few spots on the arm feel like evenly spaced hops.
    • Readiness potential: a slow buildup of electrical activity, measured on the scalp, that comes before a voluntary movement.

    Two strange effects

    In metacontrast, a disc flashes for about 30 ms, and then a ring appears around where it was. People report seeing only the ring. Yet they guess above chance that there was something before it. Did they see the disc and forget it, or never see it? That is the Orwell/Stalin question again, and Dennett gives the same answer. (ch. 6.1)

    The cutaneous rabbit was reported by Frank Geldard and Carl Sherrick in 1972. A device taps the arm several times at the wrist, then near the elbow, then on the upper arm, with gaps of 50 to 200 ms. People feel the taps hop evenly up the arm, like a small animal. Strangest of all, the second wrist tap already feels as if it has moved away from the wrist. If the elbow taps never come, the wrist taps all stay at the wrist. (ch. 6.1)

    So how did the brain "know" to move the second tap, before the elbow taps happened? It didn't. It settled on its interpretation after the later taps arrived. Asking when the experience happened, as if at one instant, is the theater question in disguise. (ch. 6.1)

    Try it: the cutaneous rabbit

    Press Play to see where taps land and where people say they feel them.

    Tap the arm

    The date on the letter

    Dennett's key distinction is between the time a signal happens and the time it represents. He uses history. The Battle of New Orleans was fought on January 8, 1815, fifteen days after the peace treaty ending the War of 1812 had been signed in Belgium. The news simply hadn't arrived. (ch. 6.2)

    Then he adds a part he openly says he made up. Imagine the British commander in chief far away in India. News of the battle reaches him first. News of the treaty comes later, by a slower route. But each letter carries a date. So he can work out that the treaty came first, without anyone restaging the events in order. (ch. 6.2)

    The brain can't put dates on its signals, Dennett notes, because its parts don't "know the date" when they send them. But the principle carries over. The brain can represent "A happened before B" without A's signal arriving first, by working out the order from what the signals say. Timing still matters in two ways. Sometimes arrival order is the only evidence, as when the brain decides which way something moved. And signals must arrive in time to guide action. Within those limits, the brain is free. (ch. 6.2)

    Try it: put events in the order they happened

    Use Dennett's version of the story, with the commander in India.

    Libet's experiments

    The neuroscientist Benjamin Libet worked with patients during brain surgery. He compared a small pulse to the hand with a pulse delivered straight to the part of the cortex that feels the hand. Either way, he reported, it took about half a second of brain activity before the person felt a tingle. Yet the hand tingle was felt as happening sooner than the tingle made in the cortex, even when the cortex was stimulated first. Libet said the hand experience is "referred back" in time to the brain's first response. The neuroscientist John Eccles took this as evidence that the mind is not the brain. (ch. 6.3)

    Dennett notes that Libet's methods were sharply criticized and the experiment had never been repeated by others. He sets that aside and asks what would follow if the results were real. (ch. 6.3)

    Libet also asked people to flex a wrist whenever they chose, and to note a clock's position at the moment they decided. The readiness potential began 350 to 400 ms before the moment they reported. Many concluded that the brain decides before "you" do. (ch. 6.4)

    Dennett says both readings mix up the time an event happens with the time it represents. There is no single moment when a sensation or decision "enters consciousness," because there is no finish line to enter. The brain can date a sensation without anything being projected backward in time. (ch. 6.3, 6.4)

    He adds a related study by W. Grey Walter. Patients advanced a slide projector by pressing a button. Secretly, a signal from electrodes on their motor cortex did the advancing instead. Patients felt that the projector was reading their minds, changing the slide just before they had decided. Dennett's reading: the brain expected to see the slide change a little later, and the early change set off an alarm. That tells us how the brain times its expectations. It doesn't tell us the moment a decision "became conscious." (ch. 6.5)

    Why wouldn't the brain just wait until every signal has arrived, then line them up?

    Waiting costs time. Signals from your toes take longer to reach the brain than signals from your forehead. Holding every signal back for the slowest one would make the whole system as slow as its worst case. When survival depends on speed, that would be a bad design. (ch. 6.2)

    A common mix-up"If two signals arrive in order B then A, you must experience B then A." Not so. Arrival order can be the evidence the brain uses, but what you experience is the order the brain concludes, which may be different. (ch. 6.2)

    6. From genes to a virtual machine

    In this part: follow Dennett's story of how evolution, learning, and culture together built the kind of mind you have.

    Words to know

    • Baldwin effect: the way animals that can learn help evolution find good designs faster.
    • Meme: a unit of culture, such as a tune, a catch-phrase, or a way of building an arch, that spreads by being copied from mind to mind.
    • Virtual machine: a set of habits imposed on a computer by a program, which makes the hardware act like a different machine.

    Reasons, boundaries, and producing the future

    Dennett tells the story from the start. Before life, there were causes but no reasons. Once things began copying themselves, some events became good or bad for them. That gave the world its first reasons. It also gave the first boundary between "me" and "everything else," the line a cell or an immune system defends. (ch. 7.2)

    Brains exist to steer bodies, and to steer you must anticipate. Simple creatures react only on contact. Better ones duck when something looms. Many animals have a built-in alarm for patterns with mirror symmetry, which in nature usually meant another animal facing you. The psychologist Odmar Neumann compares a sudden alarm to a ship's call of "All hands on deck!": every sense is woken up at once. (ch. 7.2)

    Neumann speculates that animals found these alerts so useful that they started triggering them more often, until it became a habit. Dennett builds on this: regular alertness turned into exploring for its own sake. Animals began gathering information they might need later. The psychologist George Miller called such creatures informavores. (ch. 7.2)

    Three speeds of design

    Dennett describes three ways a mind gets designed, each faster than the last (ch. 7.3–7.6):

    • Genes: natural selection changes wiring over many generations.
    • Learning: a flexible brain rewires itself within one lifetime, by trial and error.
    • Culture: ideas that others already tested get copied into your brain in an afternoon.

    The first two interact through the Baldwin effect. Picture a brain with 10 switches, each set to A or B. Only one setting out of 1,024 gives a big advantage, the "Good Trick." Without learning, being close to it counts for nothing. With learning, a creature born close to the trick can try settings and find it in its lifetime. So creatures born closer do better, and evolution can climb toward the trick step by step. Nothing learned is passed down directly. (ch. 7.3)

    Run evolution with and without learning and compare how the switches change. This is a version of the 1987 computer model by Geoffrey Hinton and Steven Nowlan that Dennett adapts.

    How it works: this follows the original model, which differs from Dennett's telling in two ways. First, each switch here is set right, wrong, or "learnable" at birth, and only learnable switches can be tried out, so a creature with any wrong switch can never find the trick. In Dennett's telling every switch can be rewired. Second, there are 20 switches instead of Dennett's 10, because with 10 a lucky guess finds the trick too easily to show the difference. Each creature with learnable switches gets up to 1,000 random tries, and finding the trick sooner means more offspring.

    New software on old hardware

    Dennett argues that the human brain reached roughly its present size about 150,000 years ago, before language and long before farming and cities. So the huge change in what humans can do since then can't come mostly from new brain hardware. It must come from new ways of using the same brain. (ch. 7.4)

    One way may have started with talking to yourself. Imagine an early human who asks for help out loud when no one is near. Hearing the question can trigger the answer in another part of the same brain. Later the talking goes silent. Drawing a picture for yourself works the same way. Dennett calls these tricks autostimulation. (ch. 7.5)

    Many such tricks are memes. The biologist Richard Dawkins coined the word in 1976. Evolution happens whenever three things hold: there is variety, there is copying, and some versions get copied more than others. Ideas meet all three. A meme spreads because it is good at spreading, which is not always good for us. Dennett sums up this unsettling view with a slogan: a scholar is just a library's way of making another library. (ch. 7.6)

    Here is his central hypothesis. A standard computer does one step at a time through a narrow channel. A brain does millions of things at once. Human consciousness, Dennett says, is like a one-thing-at-a-time virtual machine running on the brain's many-things-at-once hardware. Culture and training install it. He calls it the Joycean machine, after James Joyce's stream-of-consciousness novels. (ch. 7.7)

    Why would talking to yourself ever help, if you already know what you know?

    Because one part of the brain may hold information that another part needs, with no direct connection between them. Saying it out loud, and hearing it, uses the ears as a path around the gap. Dennett calls this a "virtual wire." (ch. 7.5)

    A common mix-up"The Baldwin effect means learned skills get passed down in the genes." No. That would be Lamarck's discredited idea. Learning only changes which creatures survive. Those born closer to the trick win, so their genes spread. (ch. 7.3)

    7. Words without a boss

    In this part: compare two models of how speech gets made and see why Dennett doubts there is a Central Meaner who decides what you mean.

    Words to know

    • Central Meaner: an imagined inner boss who decides exactly what you mean before any words are chosen.
    • Pandemonium: a design in which many small processes compete, and whatever wins takes control for a while, with no boss.
    • Confabulation: sincerely telling a made-up story without realizing it is made up.

    Otto's objection

    Dennett invents a critic named Otto, who speaks for common sense. Otto says: when I speak, I first know what I mean. Then I check words against my experience until I find the right ones. Surely there must be a "me" in the middle doing that. (ch. 8.1)

    The Dutch psycholinguist Willem (Pim) Levelt built a careful model of speaking. A part he calls the Conceptualizer sends a "preverbal message" to a Formulator, which picks words and grammar. Then an Articulator speaks. Dennett exaggerates this into a strict chain of command, a "bureaucracy," to make the contrast clear; he grants that Levelt's real model is more flexible. He then notes a problem. If the message already says exactly what to say, the Conceptualizer did all the hard work, and no one has explained how. (ch. 8.2)

    So Dennett sketches the opposite extreme, a pandemonium. Word-making processes start throwing out fragments. Other processes, tuned to your current mood and situation, favor some fragments over others. Candidate sentences form and compete, and one that fits well enough gets said. The meaning partly comes out of the process instead of starting it. Where the truth lies between these two extremes is a question for experiments, but Dennett suspects it is close to the pandemonium end. (ch. 8.2)

    When words lead

    Some evidence suggests words help create meaning. In a slip Freud reported, a man proposing a toast asked people to "hiccup" to their boss's health instead of drinking to it. The AI researchers Lawrence Birnbaum and Gregg Collins point out that no plan to insult someone would pick "hiccup" in advance. A hidden wish must have grabbed a chance when it appeared. (ch. 8.3)

    Abraham Lincoln's famous line about fooling people "some of the time" (Dennett notes it is also credited to P. T. Barnum) can be read two ways. Either some people can always be fooled, or at any time someone can be. Dennett suggests Lincoln may have liked how it sounded and never chose either meaning. Writers often find out what they think by seeing what they write. (ch. 8.3)

    Sometimes the parts disagree. Dennett once served as an umpire and found himself signaling "out" with his thumb while shouting "Safe!" He couldn't say from the inside which he had meant. In an experiment by Tony Marcel, a patient with blindsight (more on that in Part 8) was asked to signal whenever he thought a light had flashed, in three ways at once: say yes, press a button, and blink. Sometimes the three answers disagreed. (ch. 8.3)

    Brain damage can remove the checking entirely. In jargon aphasia, people speak fluent nonsense and seem untroubled. In confabulation, often after severe memory loss, people tell detailed false life stories with total sincerity. Speech can run without a boss steering it. (ch. 8.3)

    Reports make the knowledge

    Common sense, as the philosopher David Rosenthal lays it out, says a report like "I'm in pain" expresses a second-order thought, that is, a thought about your own state: the belief that you are in pain. That belief seems to come first, from looking inward. Dennett reverses the order. The belief about your state is created in the process of framing the report. You don't first observe an experience and then describe it. (ch. 10.3, 10.5)

    Why is it a problem if the Conceptualizer already knows exactly what to say?

    Because then we have only explained the easy part. We'd need a second, smaller model inside the Conceptualizer to explain how it came up with its message, and another inside that one. Explaining a mind by putting a smarter mind inside it never ends. (ch. 8.2)

    A common mix-up"No boss means speech is random." Not in Dennett's picture. Candidates are judged by how well they fit your current goals and mood, and that selection makes the result orderly and usually apt. He compares it to evolution, which designs well without a designer. (ch. 8.2, 8.3)

    Check yourself

    8. Filling in, or just not asking?

    In this part: test your blind spot and your mind's eye, and see why Dennett says the brain often ignores gaps instead of filling them.

    Words to know

    • Blind spot: the patch of each eye's retina where the optic nerve leaves, with no light sensors at all.
    • Blindsight: after damage to the brain's visual areas, people say they see nothing in part of their view yet guess well about what is there.
    • Neglect: after some brain injuries, people ignore one side of the world and don't notice that they do.

    Pictures in the head?

    In a famous 1971 study by Roger Shepard and Jacqueline Metzler, people judged whether two drawings showed the same 3D shape. The bigger the turn between them, the longer people took, as if they were rotating an image at a steady speed. So something image-like seems to happen. (ch. 10.1)

    But mental images are much weaker than real ones. Dennett imagines a drawing computer for blind engineers that first draws real pictures on a screen for a camera to look at. A better version skips the screen and camera and passes the picture along as numbers. A better one still stops drawing everything and adds labels to parts of the picture instead, like a diagram. The brain, he suggests, mixes these: it shows some things and only tells about others. (ch. 10.1)

    Try it: a crossword in your mind's eye

    Picture a 3-by-3 grid. Write these words going down, one per column, left to right: GAS, OIL, DRY. Now, without looking at any paper, read the three rows across.

    Type the three words you read across

    Columns, left to right: GAS, OIL, DRY

    On paper, the rows would jump out at you. In your head, most people struggle. A real diagram lets your visual system find patterns for free. A mental image can't hold still long enough. (ch. 10.1)

    The blind spot

    Each eye has a blind spot about 6 degrees across. You never see a hole there, even with one eye closed, even against a patterned background. The usual story is that the brain "fills in" the gap with matching color. (ch. 11.2, 11.5)

    Dennett says no filling in is needed. That part of the eye has never sent signals. So no part of the brain is waiting for news from it, and nothing complains when none comes. In his words, the absence of information is not the same as information about an absence. (ch. 11.2, 11.5)

    Try it: find your blind spot

    Close your left eye. Look at the cross with your right eye. Move closer or farther until the dot disappears.

    Start at about a forearm's length from the screen. On a phone, hold it closer. When the dot vanishes, notice that you see stripes or checks running straight through where it was.

    More of the same

    Walk into a room papered with hundreds of identical portraits of Marilyn Monroe. You see at once that the wall is covered with them. But only the center of your eye has fine detail, and your eyes can only jump a few times a second. You can only have checked one or two portraits. Dennett says the brain doesn't copy that detail across the wall. It labels the region "more of the same." The detail is on the wall, not in your head. (ch. 11.5)

    Dennett tried this kind of setup himself. An eye tracker can detect when your eye starts one of its jumps, called saccades, and swap a word on the screen before the eye lands. Readers notice nothing. Dennett sat waiting for the experiment to begin, and asked why they hadn't switched it on. It had been running the whole time. (ch. 11.7)

    Blindsight and neglect

    People with blindsight, studied by Lawrence Weiskrantz and others, say they see nothing in one region. Yet when told to guess, they name simple shapes or motions there far better than chance. In standard tests they must be prompted, though. Unprompted, they don't act on what they "see." People with hysterical blindness, in contrast, do worse than chance, which suggests they are using visual information to get it wrong. (ch. 11.2)

    Dennett asks what would happen if a blindsight patient learned to guess when to guess, without prompts. One patient, called DB, volunteers reports of fast-moving lights without being prompted. He insists he consciously sees them: how else could he report them? (ch. 11.2)

    Neglect shows the other side. After damage to one side of the brain, a person may ignore everything on the left, even people trying to talk to them, and not notice. Dennett follows the neuropsychologist Marcel Kinsbourne: the brain processes that would have "cared" about the left have been killed or outvoted, so nothing is left to complain. We all have mild versions, such as missing typos in our own writing. (ch. 11.6)

    Paul Bach-y-Rita built devices that turned a camera image into a grid of buzzing points on the skin of blind people's backs. After training, users stopped noticing the buzzing (though they could attend to it if asked) and began perceiving objects out in front of the camera. When an experimenter suddenly zoomed the lens, one user lurched backward to protect his head. (ch. 11.4)

    Why doesn't Dennett need the brain to fill in the blind spot?

    Filling in would only matter if something inside were looking at the gap and would notice it. Without an inner viewer, the brain only needs to avoid raising an alarm. Since no process expects information from that spot, no alarm is raised. (ch. 11.5)

    A common mix-up"Our experience must be continuous, since it seems continuous." Dennett says this has it backwards. Experience is full of gaps: the blind spot, the moments during each eye jump. It seems unbroken because the brain doesn't represent the gaps at all. (ch. 11.5)

    9. Qualia disqualified

    In this part: see why Dennett denies that experiences have private inner qualities, and how he answers the inverted spectrum and Mary the color scientist.

    Words to know

    • Qualia (singular: quale): the supposed private, inner "feel" of an experience, such as the redness of red as you see it.
    • Secondary quality: John Locke's term for a power of an object, like color or taste, to produce a certain effect in observers.
    • Epiphenomenal: being a mere side effect. The word has two very different senses, explained below.

    Where did the colors go?

    Light waves aren't colored, so science seems to have taken color out of the world. Many people conclude it must be inside, in the mind, as qualia. Dennett states his view plainly: he denies that any such properties exist. He agrees that there seem to be qualia. (ch. 12.1)

    His alternative: colors are properties out in the world after all. They are complicated ways that surfaces reflect light. What ties them together is the way a certain kind of observer reacts to them. That is roughly Locke's idea of a secondary quality. (ch. 12.1)

    Colors were made to be seen

    Why don't colors match any simple physical property? Dennett's answer is evolution. Flowers evolved colors to be noticed by the insects that pollinate them, and fruits to be noticed by the animals that eat them. Those animals' color vision evolved to notice the colors. Colors and color vision were made for each other. (ch. 12.2)

    He credits this way of seeing color to the philosopher Kathleen Akins. His comparison is the spies Julius and Ethel Rosenberg, who tore a cardboard Jell-O box in two to use as a password. Each ragged half fits only its mate. Ask what shape the edge is, and the only practical answer is "the shape that fits that one." Colors are like that: the properties our eyes are tuned to. (ch. 12.2)

    He sorts qualities into two kinds. A lovely quality depends on how some kind of observer would react, so it can be true of something no one has yet seen. A suspect quality needs someone to actually react. Nobody is a suspect until someone suspects them. Colors, he says, are lovely qualities. (ch. 12.2)

    Try it: lovely or suspect?

    Why some colors feel good

    Our likes and dislikes carry history. Monkeys given a choice avoid red light. People and other primates react strongly to snakes. Dennett suggests these built-in biases, later reshaped by culture, explain why experiences please or disturb us. An inner "feel" added on top would explain nothing. (ch. 12.3)

    The inverted spectrum

    An old puzzle asks: what if red things look to you the way green things look to me? We'd still use the same words. Philosophers made the puzzle sharper. Suppose surgeons rewire your eyes overnight, so grass looks red. Then you slowly adapt, until all your reactions are normal again. Are your colors still swapped, deep down? (ch. 12.4)

    Dennett says the question has no answer. The thought experiment needs a line between "how it looks" and "how I react to how it looks." That is the Cartesian Theater again. He compares it to beer. Many beer drinkers say it is an acquired taste: they didn't enjoy their first sip, and later they did. Does beer now taste different, or do they like the same taste? Beer drinkers disagree, and none of them can just look inward and tell. (ch. 12.4)

    Mary the color scientist

    The philosopher Frank Jackson imagined Mary, a scientist raised in a black-and-white room. She knows every physical fact about color vision. When she first sees a red rose, surely she learns something new, so physical facts can't be the whole story. (ch. 12.5)

    Dennett says nearly everyone imagines this wrong. "Every physical fact" is unimaginably more than "a lot." He continues the story: her captors show her a blue banana as a trick, and she spots it at once. Knowing everything, she had already worked out exactly how yellow and blue would affect her. His point is not that this proves she learns nothing. It is that the usual telling proves nothing either. (ch. 12.5)

    Two meanings of "epiphenomenal"

    The biologist Thomas Huxley probably started using the word for a byproduct with no function, like a steam engine's whistle. Such byproducts still have effects: the whistle makes a noise. Philosophers use the word for something with no physical effects at all. Dennett says the second sense is useless. If qualia had no physical effects, even your own reports of them couldn't count as evidence for them, because you'd say exactly the same things without them. It is like claiming invisible gremlins live in car engines while doing nothing. (ch. 12.5)

    The same goes for zombies. Dennett imagines a zimbo: a zombie that monitors its own states and so forms beliefs about them. It would believe it was conscious, and on the common-sense analysis of consciousness that may be enough to make it so. If a zombie differed from us in no way anyone could detect, the difference would be epiphenomenal in the useless sense. (ch. 10.4, 12.5)

    Why can't Mary's story, as usually told, prove that physical facts leave something out?

    Because it asks us to imagine someone who knows all the physical facts, and nobody can picture that. We picture someone who knows a lot, and then of course she learns something. The intuition comes from failing to follow the instructions, not from an insight into what is possible. (ch. 12.5)

    A common mix-up"Dennett says nothing looks like anything." He doesn't deny that things look red or that you enjoy colors. He denies that there is an extra, private property of your experience, separate from everything your brain does with color information. (ch. 12.1, 12.3)

    Check yourself

    10. The self as a center of gravity

    In this part: see what Dennett thinks a self is, and how he applies his view to robots, bats, and what matters morally.

    Words to know

    • Center of narrative gravity: Dennett's name for the self, a useful abstraction that the stories we tell about ourselves seem to come from.
    • Homunculus: a "little person" inside the head, used in explanations. Harmless if it is much simpler than the whole; empty if it is just as smart.

    Selves are made of stories

    Every living thing keeps a boundary between itself and the world. We feel it in odd ways. Swallowing your own saliva is fine. Spitting it into a glass and drinking it is disgusting, as the psychologists Paul Rozin and April Fallon found. (ch. 13.1)

    Animals build things that extend their boundaries. Spiders spin webs. Beavers build dams; one in an experiment tried to stop recorded running water by plastering mud over the speaker. Bowerbirds decorate courtship bowers with objects that are mostly blue. None of them needs to understand what it is doing. (ch. 13.1)

    Humans spin stories, especially about who we are. Dennett says we don't consciously write these stories; to a large degree they write us. The stories seem to come from one source, so listeners, and we ourselves, posit a single agent behind them. That agent is the self. (ch. 13.1)

    He compares it to a center of gravity in physics. An object's center of gravity is not a bit of matter you could dig out. But it is real enough to predict whether the object will tip over. The self is like that: an abstraction, not a thing in the brain, and still very useful. Dennett even calls the self a "theorists' fiction," meaning a hardworking abstraction that earns its keep, like a center of gravity. (ch. 13.1, 13.3)

    How many selves to a body?

    Dennett discusses Multiple Personality Disorder, now called dissociative identity disorder. Writing in 1991, he said the evidence showed it almost always followed severe, long-lasting abuse in early childhood. He and Nicholas Humphrey had studied it and found it reached well beyond the sufferer's own brain. Dennett notes that several selves in one body is no stranger, in principle, than one. (ch. 13.2)

    He also says split-brain patients, whose brain halves have been surgically separated, are often described too dramatically. In a few patients a second center appears only briefly, during careful experiments. A self, like a club in the philosopher Derek Parfit's example, can lapse and later restart, with no sharp facts about whether it is "the same." (ch. 13.2)

    Try it: which boat is you?

    New York Harbor once tried a shared radar system: one radar on land, with its picture sent to small TVs on boats. Lost in fog, you see several moving dots, and one is your own boat. How can you tell which? Dennett's answer: turn quickly in a tight circle and watch which dot makes a little "O." It works most of the time, unless other boats try the same trick at once. (ch. 13.3)

    Find your boat on the radar screen
    That's me:

    Watch the dots, then turn your boat.

    Brains face the same problem: which signals are about me? A chimpanzee can learn to reach for food through a hole by watching its own arm on a TV screen. To do it, the chimp has to notice that the arm on the screen moves when it moves its own. Dennett says we learn about our inner states in a similar way, largely by telling stories and checking them. (ch. 13.3)

    Robots, rooms, and bats

    A friend once told Dennett he couldn't imagine a conscious robot. Dennett replied that he easily could: robots in movies do it for us. What he couldn't imagine was how a robot could be conscious. But it is just as hard to imagine how a brain could be. Dennett's book is an attempt to show how. (ch. 14.1)

    The philosopher John Searle imagined himself in a room, following a rulebook to answer Chinese questions without understanding Chinese. So, he argued, running a program can't create understanding. Dennett replies that we are invited to imagine a far too simple program. A program that could really hold a Chinese conversation, getting jokes and explaining them, would be vast and layered. The understanding, if any, would belong to the whole system, not to Searle as one cog in it. (ch. 14.1)

    The philosopher Thomas Nagel asked what it is like to be a bat, which perceives by echoes. He argued that we could never grasp it. Dennett disagrees: research on what bats can and can't detect tells us a great deal. He thinks creatures without language have much simpler minds than ours. A bat, for instance, has no way to wonder what day of the week it is. (ch. 14.2)

    Minding and mattering

    Does this view make suffering matter less? Dennett argues it still gives us strong reasons to care. Many animals can suffer to a significant degree, though suffering comes in degrees. Suffering is having your desires and plans blocked, so it depends on having them. The biologist Marian Stamp Dawkins tested what hens care about. Given a choice, they preferred cages with litter for scratching. But when they had to push through a curtain to reach the litter, they barely bothered. Asking what an animal will pay for tells us what matters to it. (ch. 14.3)

    Finally, has Dennett explained consciousness, or explained it away? He says any real explanation has to leave something out: it explains a thing using parts that don't have it. A model of pain with a box labeled "pain" in it hasn't started explaining. Life is explained in terms of things that aren't alive, and that doesn't make living things lifeless. (ch. 14.4)

    If the self is an abstraction, do you exist?

    Yes, in Dennett's view, the way a center of gravity exists. It isn't a hidden nugget in your brain, but it is real enough to organize everything you say and do. What he denies is a self that is a special thing separate from that organization, a pearl of soul-stuff. (ch. 11.8, 13.3)

    A common mix-up"Dennett says robots are already conscious." He doesn't. He says there is no reason in principle why a suitably organized robot couldn't be, since a brain is also a machine. Whether any actual machine is conscious depends on how it is organized. (ch. 14.1)

    About the source

    Daniel C. Dennett, Consciousness Explained, illustrated by Paul Weiner. Boston: Little, Brown, 1991. Paperback editions from Back Bay Books and Penguin. The book at its publisher.

    Dennett (1942–2024) was a philosopher at Tufts University. This guide follows the book's main argument across all fourteen chapters, in plain words and its own examples. Every activity and diagram was made for this guide; none reproduces the book's figures, though the crossword uses Dennett's word sets.

    What this guide leaves out or simplifies

    • Chapter 9's survey of models of the mind (production systems, Fodor's modules, Minsky's society of mind) and most of chapter 3's tour of experience.
    • Many shorter thought experiments, including the robot Shakey, the CADBLIND machines in detail, and Hide the Thimble.
    • The appendices for philosophers and scientists.
    • The times in "Probe the drafts" are made up to show the idea. The motion cutoffs in the color phi lab are rough guesses for screens, not figures from the book.
    • The Baldwin effect activity follows Hinton and Nowlan's 1987 model: 20 switches, some fixed right or wrong at birth. In Dennett's telling there are 10, and every one can be rewired.
    • The radar activity is a game built on Dennett's short example, not a reconstruction of the harbor system.

    Reading it today

    The book is from 1991, and much of its science has moved on. Libet's findings, blindsight, and the evolution of the brain have all been studied further since. Dennett's views on qualia were, and remain, widely disputed. So are the causes of dissociative identity disorder; Dennett's 1991 summary is not the last word. A well-known reply is David Chalmers's The Conscious Mind (1996), which argues that consciousness poses a "hard problem" that functional explanations can't reach. These notes on later work come from outside the book.

    References in this guide, like (ch. 5.2), point to the chapter and numbered section of the book, since page numbers differ between editions.
