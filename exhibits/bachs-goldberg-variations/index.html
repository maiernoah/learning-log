This page teaches Bach: The Goldberg Variations by Peter Williams (2001): its Introduction, Chapter 2 ("Overall shape") and Chapter 3 ("The movements"). It is a study guide written in plain language, not a copy of the original. About the source.

Bach's Goldberg Variations is a set of thirty keyboard pieces, framed by a slow Aria at the start and the end. Williams notes one plain way to state its size: the largest single keyboard work published anywhere in the 1700s (p. 1). All of it grows from one short line of 32 bass notes.

This page shows how that works. You'll hear the bass under different surfaces, see the plan Bach built on paper, and build a canon yourself. You'll also see where the plan bends, and why Williams wonders whether it was meant to.

Some parts play sound. Nothing plays until you press a Play button.

By the end you'll be able to

  • Explain what the Goldberg Variations actually vary, and what they leave behind.
  • Describe Bach's plan of ten groups of three, and point to where he breaks it.
  • Build a canon at a chosen interval and predict what changes as the interval grows.
  • Compare the shape a listener hears with the shape on paper, and sort a performer's choices by which shape they serve.
  • Judge the evidence behind the famous story of the sleepless Count.

1. The sleepless Count

In this part: weigh the famous story behind the name "Goldberg" against the dates.

Words to know

A harpsichord is a keyboard instrument whose strings are plucked. A manual is one of its keyboards; the Goldberg asks for an instrument with two. Clavierübung means "Keyboard Practice," the title Bach gave his published keyboard books.

What the title page says

Bach's printed title page never mentions anyone named Goldberg. In German it reads, roughly, "Keyboard Practice, consisting of an Aria with diverse variations for the harpsichord with 2 manuals" (p. 3). It adds that the music was prepared "for the soul's delight of music-lovers."

Williams says that phrase carried more weight for a Lutheran believer than it does for us. Refreshing the spirit was a way to prepare for further work, done for the sake of one's neighbor. The volume was offered as a pious gift, not a show-off's product (pp. 3–4).

The story

The nickname comes from J. N. Forkel, who wrote the first major book about Bach in 1802 (p. 4). Forkel says Count Keyserlingk of Dresden was often ill and couldn't sleep. A young musician in his household, Johann Gottlieb Goldberg, spent those nights in the next room, playing for him.

The Count asked Bach for keyboard pieces "so gentle and somewhat merry" that they might cheer his sleepless nights. Bach chose to write variations, though he had thought them a thankless task "because of the basic harmony always being the same" (pp. 4–5).

Why Williams doubts it

No dedication to the Count appears on the title page or on any known copy. Williams admits that alone doesn't settle it (p. 5).

The dates are harder to explain. Bach probably wrote the set around 1739–40, and Goldberg was born in 1727 (p. 5). That makes the player in the story about twelve years old, young for music this demanding.

Williams's likeliest explanation is simpler. In November 1741 Bach stayed with the Count in Dresden (p. 4). He probably brought a signed copy of the newly printed set and gave it to him. The tale may have reached Forkel through Bach's son Wilhelm Friedemann, and grown along the way (p. 5).

Two more details bother Williams. "Gentle and somewhat merry" hardly describes the whole work. And Bach had already mastered variations on a repeating harmony: the Chaconne in D minor for violin and the Passacaglia in C minor for organ. Calling that kind of writing "thankless" doesn't fit (p. 6).

An analogyThink of a family story about how a grandparent once met someone famous. Each retelling can be sincere and still add details. Where the analogy breaks down: Williams isn't accusing anyone of lying. He is checking a story against dates and documents.

Williams notes that only Forkel's last clause implies the set was written for the Count. The rest could mean Bach already had the variations and found them suitable (p. 5).

2. Variations on a bass, not a tune

In this part: find out what actually stays the same from one variation to the next.

Words to know

A variation is a new version of a piece of music that keeps some things and changes others. The bass line is the lowest part. A chord is several notes sounded together; a series of chords is the music's harmony.

The puzzle in the title

The title promises "an Aria with diverse variations." Yet the Aria's tune never comes back, not even in disguise, until the whole Aria returns unchanged at the very end (p. 35). Bach doesn't even write it out again. The score just says Aria da capo è fine: play the Aria from the beginning, and stop (p. 35).

Williams's answer is that the variations are built on the Aria's harmonies, a series of chords, and not on its melody (p. 35). He says he never hears the Aria's tune in them, and assumes you aren't meant to.

The real theme

What carries over is the bass: 32 notes, one per bar. Williams prints a reduced version and calls it the fundamental bass (p. 36). Bach never sets it out this plainly. In each movement it is "coloured": decorated, moved, or replaced by another note of the same chord (p. 36).

Even the Aria is one of these treatments. Williams calls it a melodious setting of the harmonies over a model bass, more a variation than a theme (pp. 35, 54). It is written in the style of a slow, tender sarabande (p. 54).

This was a real choice. The usual variations of the time decorated a tune and its simple chords with stock patterns. Williams agrees that writing those would have been a "thankless task," and says none of Bach's mature variation sets is of that kind (p. 6).

The bass is also unusually singable. It falls step by step, then climbs to a cadence, a phrase ending that sounds like arriving (pp. 36–37). Williams calls it "curiously melodious," no mere stock bass.

An analogyA jazz band can read one chord chart and play a dozen different tunes over it, and the song is still recognizably the same song. Where it breaks down: jazz players improvise, while Bach wrote every note. He also bends the bass itself, moving it, inverting its chords and, in places, making it chromatic.

Why? Why would a bass make a better theme than a tune for thirty movements?

A tune fixes the top line and its rhythm, so every variation sounds like a decorated copy of the same song. A bass and its chords fix only the path of the harmony. Above that path Bach is free to write a canon, a fugue, a dance or a keyboard showpiece. Williams calls the thirty movements "distinct essays" in different kinds of music, all over one series of chords (p. 35).

A common mix-up"Each variation decorates the Aria's melody." That is how many variation sets of the time worked, including Bach's own early ones, where the point was to keep catching glimpses of the tune (p. 35). In the Goldberg the melody vanishes after the first movement. What returns thirty times is the bass and its chords.

3. Four notes become thirty-two

In this part: trace how an old four-note pattern grew into the 32-bar bass, and map where each phrase lands.

Words to know

A ground bass is a short bass line repeated over and over while the parts above it change. A chaconne is a piece built that way. The tonic is a key's home note (G, in G major); the dominant is its fifth note (D); the relative minor of G major is E minor.

An old opening

The Goldberg bass opens with four falling notes: G, F♯, E, D. In the 1600s those four notes were a standard repeating bass (p. 38). Corelli used them in G major, Biber in the minor, and Italian guitarists and French harpsichordists built long pieces on them (pp. 38–39).

Composers then stretched the pattern to eight notes. Williams shows eight-bar versions by Handel, Muffat and Purcell (pp. 37–38). Handel's two chaconnes in G use the same first eight bass notes as the Goldberg, in the same key (p. 43). Williams thinks it out of the question that Bach knew nothing of these traditions (p. 38).

Doubling, then doubling again

Bach went further. Sixteen notes take the bass to its halfway point on D. A matching sixteen bring it home to G. Williams puts it this way: it is as if the theme began as four notes, then became eight, then sixteen, then thirty-two (p. 37). The powers of two that run through the work start here.

The four eight-bar phrases end with cadences in G, D, E minor and G. The first lands in G, the tonic. The second lands in D, the dominant. The third lands in E minor, the relative minor. The last returns to G (p. 38). Williams calls the plan as simple as can be, and strong enough to survive the three variations in the minor.

Why length matters

A four- or eight-bar bass comes round again and again, like a loop. That is what makes a chaconne. A bass four times longer can't work as a short loop: each pass becomes a full, separate movement (p. 38). Bach also drops the chaconne's dance style; none of the variations sounds like the chaconnes of Purcell or Muffat (p. 39).

One more subtlety. Played alone, the bass sounds to most listeners as if it starts on an upbeat, but every movement treats it as starting on the downbeat. Williams suggests this sets up a kind of "metrical tension" in the whole work (pp. 36–37).

Why? Why would stretching the bass to 32 bars stop the variations sounding like a chaconne?

A chaconne's effect comes from a short loop heard many times in a row, so the ear tracks the loop. At 32 bars, one pass is a whole movement with its own two halves and four cadences. You hear a new piece each time instead of the next lap of a loop (p. 38).

A common mix-up"Bach invented the bass." Its opening was common property, used by Corelli, Biber, Purcell, Handel and others (pp. 37–39). Williams thinks such a shared bass pushed Bach to make an original gesture with it (p. 39). The originality lies in the doubling, the cadence plan and what he built on top.

4. The plan in threes

In this part: map the thirty variations into ten groups of three, and find where the pattern breaks.

Words to know

A canon is a piece in which one voice copies another, note for note, after a delay. A genre is a recognizable kind of piece, such as a dance or a fugue. Hand-crossing means one hand reaching over the other on the keyboard. Williams calls the bright hand-crossing variations arabesques.

Ten groups of three

Williams says the work has two shapes: one you hear, and one laid out on paper (p. 40). Part 9 covers the first. This part covers the second.

On paper, the thirty variations fall into ten groups of three. A dance or other genre piece comes first. A bright arabesque comes second, usually with crossed hands on two manuals. A canon comes third (p. 40). The canons rise by one step each time, from the unison in No. 3 to the ninth in No. 27 (pp. 41–42, 46).

The groups aren't marked in the score (p. 42), and they're often hard to hear. Some canons don't sound like canons (No. 3), while some pieces that aren't canons do (No. 2) (p. 40). In most canons a free bass part runs underneath, partly covering the copying (pp. 40–41).

Where the pattern breaks

The first group reverses the order: the hand-crossing piece comes before the genre piece. The last group is irregular too (p. 42). Williams calls the result "symmetrically irregular." He offers two readings. The reversal might mean the plan only took its final form gradually. Or perhaps Bach broke the pattern on purpose, to avoid a scheme that looked too calculated, or to make the work begin and end with energy (p. 42).

Inside each group, Williams says, the hardest task for the composer comes third and the hardest to play comes second (p. 47). But the groups take very different shapes, as if they were meant to stay in the mind rather than the ear. And one of the hardest variations to play well is the very first (p. 47).

Other regularities sit on top. Strict fugal pieces appear at Nos. 10, 16 and 22 (p. 46). Thirty is 3 × 3 × 3 + 3, and Bach's previous keyboard book had 27 pieces. Williams warns that this need not mean a deliberate link (p. 43).

Why? Why might Bach have made the groups so hard to hear?

Williams suggests the groups were meant to stay a plan in the mind, not a pattern for the ear (p. 47). The big differences in genre from piece to piece keep the work from feeling like a diagram (pp. 45–46). And the shared bass makes you hear each piece as a variation first, whatever its slot (p. 47).

A common mix-up"The variations get steadily harder and more exciting." That was common in the simpler variation sets of the day. The later hand-crossing pieces do have more voices than the earlier ones (p. 47). But Williams says the Goldberg's overall shape contradicts anything so straightforward (p. 47).

5. The canon ladder

In this part: build a canon yourself, then see how Bach's nine canons differ.

Words to know

An interval is the distance in pitch between two notes. Unison means the same note; a second is one step apart; a third, two steps; and so on up to the octave and the ninth. Inversus is Latin for "inverted": the follower plays the leader's line upside down. A suspension is a note held over into the next chord, where it clashes and then steps down to settle.

Two settings make a canon

In a canon one voice, the leader, is copied by another, the follower. A round like "Row, Row, Row Your Boat" is the simplest kind (that example is ours, not Williams's). Every canon has two settings. The interval says how far above or below the follower plays. The delay says how long it waits.

Bach varies both. He also varies which voice goes first, and in some canons the roles swap partway through. The delay ranges from half a bar to two bars (p. 48). The nine canons use eight different time signatures (p. 42). Williams reads all this variety as a deliberate survey of what canon can do (p. 48).

Climbing the ladder

The interval rises one step per canon: unison in No. 3, second in No. 6, third in No. 9, and so on to the ninth in No. 27 (pp. 41–42). Two canons answer inversus: the fourth (No. 12) and the fifth (No. 15) (p. 41). Williams points out a symmetry. Unison, second, third and an inverted fourth are followed by an inverted fifth, sixth, seventh and octave (p. 48).

Each interval brings its own problems. At the unison the two voices keep crossing (p. 58). In the canon at the second, almost every main beat is a suspension (p. 61). In No. 24 the follower answers an octave below, then later an octave above (p. 80).

The canons also have to fit the 32-bar harmonies. Williams treats canon as the hardest composing task in each group (p. 47). In most of them a third, free voice adds a bass, as in a trio sonata. Only No. 27, at the ninth, is a pure two-voice round with no bass (pp. 40–41, 85).

The ninth is an oddity. It has no bass. A ninth is a second plus an octave, so it repeats the interval of No. 6. And no canon at the tenth follows (p. 48). The scholar Werner Breig suggested the work was first planned with only 24 variations. Williams mentions the idea, but notes it leaves you guessing which were added, and why (p. 48).

Why? Why does a canon at the unison make the voices cross so often?

At the unison both voices sing the same tune in the same range, just at different moments. Whichever voice is on the higher stretch of the tune is on top, so the two keep trading places. Williams notes the closely crossing parts of No. 3 for this reason (p. 58). Try it above: set "unison" and look at the roll.

6. The plan in twos

In this part: count the twos, fours, eights, sixteens and thirty-twos that run through the work.

Words to know

A movement in binary form has two halves, each usually played twice. A phrase is a musical sentence; it ends at a cadence.

A catalogue of binaries

Alongside the threes, Williams finds "a whole catalogue of binaries" (pp. 43–44). The work has two halves, one beginning and one ending with the Aria. Every movement has two exact halves, each played twice. There are two modes (major and minor) and two manuals (p. 44).

The numbers keep doubling. Melody and harmony move in two-bar units, four-bar sub-phrases and eight-bar phrases. Each half of the theme has 16 bars, and each half of the work has 16 movements. There are 32 pieces on 32 pages, over a bass of 32 bars (p. 44).

Hidden, not shouted

No. 16, the Ouverture, begins the second half. In the original engraving its first bar alone is indented after the title, a discreet hint (p. 44). Musically the hint is loud: after the fade-away ending of No. 15, No. 16 bursts in (p. 44).

Equal halves were unusual. In most binary movements of the time the second half was longer, a habit that later grew into sonata form (pp. 44–45). Equal halves are unlikely to happen by chance (p. 44).

The two-, four- and eight-bar phrases are strikingly clear, yet the music never stops flowing (p. 45). The canons often overlap their phrase endings, which blurs the four-square pattern (p. 45). The music scholar Donald Tovey called the four-bar phrases "quatrains" (p. 44).

Bach doesn't make any of this obvious. Only 31 of the 32 movements are written out: the closing Aria is just an instruction (p. 44). Williams suggests the composer may simply not have cared to spell the symmetries out (p. 44).

Why? Why would equal halves have stood out in 1740?

Because the normal habit ran the other way: binary movements of the time almost always had a longer second half (pp. 44–45). Williams says exact halves were not at all common and unlikely to arise by chance (p. 44). So when every movement has them, that points to a plan. Why was the second half usually longer? Our own guess, not Williams's: it had more ground to cover, travelling away from the home key and back.

A common mix-up"The numbers are a secret code." Williams is wary of intricate symbolic schemes built around the work (p. 2) and of fanciful number-counting (p. 8). He notes that Bach hid his symmetries instead of displaying them (pp. 44, 92). The twos are structural. They grow out of the bass and its phrases.

7. Thirty kinds of piece, one upbeat

In this part: see how Bach made each variation a different kind of piece, and the rule that limited his choices.

Words to know

The downbeat is the first, strongest beat of a bar. An upbeat is one or more notes that come before it, like the "Hap-" in "Happy birthday." A quodlibet (Latin, "what you please") is a medley that weaves popular tunes together.

A mix with no precedent

Bach had already tried giving variations different characters in his early Aria variata, though its tempo differences were slight (p. 39). The Goldberg goes much further. Each variation is a strongly characterized, independent kind of piece. Williams says there was really no precedent for this "genre-mix" (p. 39).

The list includes a sarabande-like Aria, a gigue, perhaps a polonaise and a minuet, a little fugue, a French overture, nine canons, virtuoso duets, a slow arioso and a quodlibet (pp. 41–42).

The no-upbeat rule

Every movement treats the bass as starting on a downbeat (p. 36). So Bach needed 31 kinds of piece that could start with no upbeat, and he kept to this strictly (p. 46). That ruled out the allemande and courante, and the usual passepied, bourrée and gigue, all of which normally begin with an upbeat (p. 46).

Sarabandes, minuets, polonaises and marches were possible. So were gavotte-like or gigue-like pieces, as long as the written upbeat was dropped (p. 46).

You can see the rule at work. No. 4 has the cross-rhythms of a passepied but isn't a true one, since a passepied should start with an upbeat (p. 59). Bach's own copy marks No. 7 al tempo di giga, "in the time of a gigue," which surprised players who had heard it as a gentle siciliano (p. 62).

Fugue and overture

No. 10 is a small four-voice fughetta. Its subject paraphrases the opening of the bass, with entries every four bars (pp. 64–65). In 1777 Bach's former pupil Kirnberger singled it out as "even a regular four-part fugue" (p. 64).

No. 16 is a French overture: a grand opening in jerky dotted rhythms, then a short, dashing fugue (pp. 70–72). It starts with the biggest chord yet, as different from No. 15 as possible (pp. 70–71).

The one exception

No. 30 is the only movement with an upbeat (pp. 47, 88). It is a quodlibet, and the tunes at the start of each half aren't Bach's, so they keep their own upbeats (p. 47).

More generally, Williams isn't sure the dance-like variations are immediately recognizable as dances. The bass makes you hear them as variations (p. 47).

Why? Why does a bass that starts on the downbeat rule out upbeat dances?

The first note of the bass belongs on the first downbeat of every movement (p. 36). A dance that opens with an upbeat puts notes before that beat, and its character depends on that lead-in. Bach chose kinds of piece that needed no lead-in, or dropped the lead-in from those that usually have one (p. 46).

A common mix-up"A variation with a dance label is that dance." Several are only like the dance. No. 4 lacks a passepied's upbeat (p. 59). No. 7 is "in the time of" a gigue (p. 62). Williams calls the polonaise label for No. 1 a guess (p. 56).

8. Major, minor, chromatic

In this part: hear how Bach darkens the bass in the three minor variations.

Words to know

Chromatic notes are the half steps that fall outside a key's scale. The Chromatic Fourth is a bass that falls (or rises) four notes' distance by half steps. Affekt was the term of Bach's time for the mood a piece expresses.

Three in the minor

Three variations move from G major to G minor: Nos. 15, 21 and 25 (pp. 41–42). The bass plan survives the change easily (p. 38). Where the theme cadenced in E minor, No. 15's second half moves to E♭ major, whose beauty Williams says "cannot be missed" (p. 69).

Williams ties this to an old tradition. Louis Couperin's Passacaille in G minor moves into G major and later makes its bass chromatic (p. 49). Bach uses all three kinds of bass: major, minor and chromatic (pp. 49–50).

The Chromatic Fourth

The chromatic version fills the bass's opening fall, G down to D, with every half step: G, F♯, F, E, E♭, D (p. 83). This "Chromatic Fourth" was a familiar figure in the music of the time (pp. 43, 50, 77). It is clearest at the start of No. 21 (p. 50).

No. 25 is built on one Chromatic Fourth after another, falling and rising, in the bass and the tenor (pp. 50, 83). Bach marked it "adagio" in his own copy (p. 81). Williams calls it "unquestionably the emotional high point of the work" (p. 82). Its third phrase now cadences in E♭ minor, which he calls "a spectacular transformation" (p. 82).

No. 15, the first minor variation, is full of slurred pairs of falling notes, a "dragging motif" familiar from older organ chorales and meant to evoke a sad Affekt (pp. 51, 69). It ends on a bare open fifth, "left floating like the last voice in a children's round" (p. 70).

Sad, or just shaped?

Williams then asks a hard question. Does No. 25 express sadness, or is it doing something more purely musical: showing one more shape the same harmonic frame can take (pp. 83–84)? No. 25 can be called "sad" and No. 26 "happy," he says, but neither label is required (p. 84).

Why? Does the chromatic line sound sad because it is sad?

A common mix-up"The minor variations use a different theme." The bass plan survives the move to the minor (p. 38). Even in No. 25 the chromatic bass stays within the rule of one chord or note per bar (p. 82). Bach flattens some notes or fills the gaps with half steps.

9. Two shapes, and the last return

In this part: compare the shape a listener hears with the shape on paper, and decide how a performer might choose.

Words to know

Williams's perceptual shape is the shape you hear as the music passes. His conceptual shape is the plan on paper. An arch form rises to a center and comes back down.

The shape you hear

Heard in time, the work moves by sharp contrasts (p. 40). It reaches a semi-climax at the French overture in the middle. It sinks into the pathos of No. 25. Excitement builds toward the end, with festivity in No. 29 and a full, choral sound in No. 30. Then the Aria returns and dies away (p. 40). Williams adds that the build-up is certainly there "as usually played."

The shape on paper

The conceptual shape is static, always there on the page: ten groups of three that by themselves neither build nor release tension (p. 40). Its outline approaches an arch: Aria, Overture in the middle, Aria. In that shape there need be no crescendo into the finale (p. 40).

Players, Williams says, can reflect either shape, but hardly both. He suspects the arch is closer to what a composer had in mind before public recitals became normal (p. 40). Public performance, he adds, must always have favored the climax (p. 88).

The finale

Nos. 28 and 29 pair up as brilliant, climactic pieces, and No. 29 is the only variation that keeps the tempo and mood of the one before it (p. 87). No. 30, the Quodlibet, works like the chorale at the end of a Leipzig cantata, where everyone joins in, but in higher spirits (p. 89).

The Quodlibet weaves popular songs into the bass's harmonies. Forkel reported that Bach's family met to sing quodlibets and "laugh heartily" (p. 89). Words written into one copy suggest two of the tunes. In Williams's translation they are "I have been so long away from you" and "Cabbage and beets have driven me away" (p. 90). Williams cautions that other words may have been meant, especially for the first (p. 89).

The Aria again

Then the Aria returns, note for note (p. 92). Williams finds it changed all the same: once a greeting, now a farewell (p. 2). After the last five variations its melody stands out, and it can sound wistful or resigned, "the same notes but now final" (p. 92). He adds a practical point. A player who skips the repeats in the returning Aria breaks the work's binary pattern (p. 92).

Why? How can the same notes sound different the second time?

Williams says no such return can have a neutral Affekt (p. 92). What you have just heard changes what you hear: the Aria now arrives after thirty movements, and you know it is the end. He admits he isn't sure anyone can fully describe the effect (p. 2).

About the source

Peter Williams, Bach: The Goldberg Variations, Cambridge Music Handbooks (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2001). The book at Cambridge University Press.

This guide covers the Introduction, Chapter 2 ("Overall shape") and Chapter 3 ("The movements"). It leaves out Chapter 1, on the work's background and Bach's earlier "Keyboard Practice" books, and Chapter 4, on how later musicians received the work.

It also simplifies. Williams compares the Goldberg with dozens of other pieces; this guide keeps only a few. It doesn't reproduce his music examples. The bass diagrams redraw his Example 2 with one choice shown where he gives alternatives. The sound in the interactives is made by this page: the surfaces over the bass, the canon tune and the chords in Part 8 are illustrations, not Bach's notes. Williams's discussion of instruments and notation is shortened to a few points.

Many of Williams's points are his own readings, and he marks them as guesses ("perhaps," "I imagine"). This guide tries to keep those marks. The book appeared in 2001, and later research may have added to it.

Page numbers in this guide, like (p. 4), point to the printed page numbers of the original.
