What the Output Can't See

Rebuild a question-answering machine from wires, water pipes, squirrels, or neurons, and test whether consciousness belongs to the material or the pattern.

[ ◄ Noah's Learning Log | About ]

If your brain ran on water pipes, would you know?

Picture a machine that answers questions. Inside, signals run through copper wires. Now rebuild it so the same signals flow as water through pipes, in exactly the same pattern. Rebuild it again with trained squirrels carrying notes. Ask it anything, and the answers come out identical every time. Followed carefully, that fact says something surprising about where consciousness lives.

Part 1 · Two kinds of facts

Every fact about a machine lands in one of two bins

Start with chess. A game of chess is the same game whether the pieces are carved wood, plastic, or pebbles on a napkin. The moves are the pattern. The pieces are the material. Nothing that happens on the board depends on what the knight is made of.

Any machine can be looked at the same way. Treat it as a box with inputs (what it hears) and outputs (what it says). Then sort every fact about the box with one test: could this fact change what comes out?

Some facts pass. Somewhere in Claude's trained weights is the information that Paris is in France. Ask about Paris, and that stored fact shapes the reply. Call these facts the functional layer: the pattern of cause and effect that turns inputs into outputs.

Other facts fail. The exact weight of the power supply feeding the server rack never shows up in an answer. Neither does whether the chips are silicon or carbon, or whether the signals are electricity in wires or water in pipes. Call these the physical layer: the material the pattern happens to be built from. Philosophers call it the substrate.

Fine printThe machine has to keep working. Melt the chips and the answers change, because the algorithm stopped running. The claim covers any material that runs the same algorithm correctly. An algorithm here means a fixed set of steps: when this signal arrives, send that one on.

Part 2 · The storm

A simulated storm can't get anything wet

In 1980 the philosopher John Searle made a famous point: a computer simulation of a rainstorm leaves nobody drenched. He offered it as a reason to doubt that a simulated brain would really think.

The point is correct, and it shows the two layers at work. Wetness belongs to the physical layer. A program can compute rain all day, and nothing it computes makes the circuit board damp. The wall between the layers blocks traffic in the other direction too. A weather station inside the simulation has no way to measure whether the computer is built from copper or from plumbing.

So the argument asks which bin consciousness belongs in. If it works like wetness, a simulated brain would lack it, however perfect the simulation. If it works like the fact about Paris, any material running the right pattern would have it. Part 3 gives a reason to pick the second bin.

Part 3 · The argument

Consciousness leaves fingerprints on what you say

The whole argument fits in three steps. It needs only one fact about consciousness, and it's a fact you can check for yourself.

1

The material can't steer the output.

Established

Any algorithm can run on a different material: chips, pipes, squirrels. So no algorithm can depend on which material it runs on, and nothing the system says can depend on it either. The machine at the top of the page shows this directly.

2

Consciousness does steer the output.

Contested

When you notice your own awareness and say "I think, therefore I am," as René Descartes did in 1637, your consciousness is part of the cause of those words. Whatever else stays mysterious about consciousness, you know it does at least this much.

3

So consciousness belongs to the pattern.

Argument

It sits with facts like "knows Paris is in France": things that affect behavior and can't depend on the underlying physics. Keep the pattern of cause and effect, swap the material, and the consciousness comes along. The name for this view is functionalism, and the claim that any suitable material can host a mind is called substrate independence.

Part 4 · Run it on yourself

Swap your neurons for squirrels, one at a time

Your brain has roughly 86 billion neurons, according to cell counts by Frederico Azevedo and colleagues (2009). Imagine replacing one of them with a tiny station where trained squirrels pass notes. The station receives the same signals the neuron did and sends the same signals out, at the same moments. Then replace another, and another.

Argument Every neuron is now a squirrel station. The pattern hasn't changed, so on this argument, neither has your experience.

To notice the swap, something in your brain would have to fire differently than it did before. By construction, nothing does. Your reports, your memories, and your sense of being you all run on the same pattern. This is why nobody discovered neurons by introspection. Santiago Ramón y Cajal had to stain brain tissue and look through a microscope in the 1880s and 1890s. From the inside, the material is invisible.

The philosopher David Chalmers built a careful version of this thought experiment in 1995. Suppose experience did fade as the swap went on. Then partway through you would be losing your experience while sincerely reporting that everything feels the same. Chalmers argues that a mind that wrong about its own experience is too strange to accept.

Part 5 · The other side

Where the argument could break

Each step has serious critics. Here are the main objections as their defenders would put them, each followed by the reply from the argument's side.

Contested Targets step 2

Consciousness doesn't cause the words

Epiphenomenalists hold that experience is a side effect. Brain machinery produces the sentence "I think, therefore I am" on its own, and the felt experience rides along without steering anything.

ReplyThen your belief that you're conscious isn't caused by your being conscious. A version of you with no inner experience at all would say the same words for the same reasons. Chalmers calls this the paradox of phenomenal judgment. Some philosophers accept that cost. Others take it as a sign the view went wrong.

Contested Targets step 3

Brains have special biological powers

John Searle argued that brains cause consciousness through their specific biology, the way stomachs cause digestion. A simulation of digestion digests nothing, so a simulation of a brain would understand nothing.

ReplyDigestion changes physical stuff, so it sits in the physical layer with wetness. Consciousness changes what you say. If biology alone produced it and it also steered your words, then swapping the biology while keeping the pattern would have to change your words. In the squirrel swap, by construction, your words stay the same.

Contested Targets what counts as "the same pattern"

Same answers, different machine

Integrated Information Theory, developed by Giulio Tononi, says consciousness depends on how a physical system's parts constrain one another. Two systems can produce identical outputs with very different inner wiring. Tononi and Christof Koch (2015) argue that an ordinary digital computer simulating a brain would have almost no integrated information, and so almost no consciousness.

ReplyThe argument holds the whole chain of cause and effect fixed, step by step, as in the squirrel swap. It makes no claim about a lookup table that fakes the same answers through different steps. IIT's defenders answer that a digital computer never reproduces the brain's physical causal structure, even when it reproduces every computation. This dispute is open.

Contested Targets the idea that brains run algorithms

Brains might not be algorithms at all

The physicist Roger Penrose (The Emperor's New Mind, 1989), later with anesthesiologist Stuart Hameroff, proposed that some brain processes are quantum events no algorithm can reproduce. If so, there is no pattern to copy onto squirrels, and the argument never gets started.

StatusA speculative proposal. No experiment has shown non-computable processes in brains.

Part 6 · Back to machines

What this settles about AI, and what it leaves open

If the argument holds

One popular reason to rule out AI consciousness falls away: "it's only silicon." Material never decides the question. A mind made of chips and a mind made of cells are on equal footing.

Still open

Which patterns produce consciousness. A thermostat and a brain could both run on silicon, and this argument alone can't tell you whether the thermostat feels anything.

Researchers split on which patterns matter. Eliezer Yudkowsky, whose posts this page is built on, argues that a mind which truthfully says "I am sad" needs a self-model, an "I" to attach the sadness to. On that model he worries more about large language models, which reflect on and talk about themselves, than about mosquitoes or chickens. He presents this as a guess from a model, and says so.

Others weigh different evidence. The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness, released in April 2024 and signed by hundreds of scientists and philosophers, found strong support for conscious experience in mammals and birds, and a realistic possibility of it in all vertebrates and many invertebrates, insects included.

Where the seam isParts 1 through 4 make an argument about categories: which bin consciousness goes in. This part is about which mechanisms count. That second question is unsettled, and the first argument doesn't answer it.
