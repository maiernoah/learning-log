What it is

Before you give to a nonprofit you can read its 990, its annual report and a rating site. That tells you how it spends. It does not tell you what changed in the world because of it, how it is most likely to go wrong, what a gift is good for, or whether someone else already does the work better. A dossier answers those questions, in writing, with every claim tagged by how well it is supported and linked to its source.

The dossier is built in nine passes: identity and structure, the money, the ratios, the public record, the results, the field, the culture, four futures, and the verdict. Research agents gather and read; the arithmetic on filed numbers is done by code, not by a model, so every number traces to a filing. The verdict is the part you will actually read: a health score with its reasoning, a pre-mortem of the most likely failures, the risk of each kind of gift, and the questions to ask before you give.

How to read one

Every claim carries a tag. Evidence means an agent saw it in a source it fetched. Inference is a reading of evidence, and says what it rests on. Speculation is a hypothesis worth checking; those lines are blacked out until you click them. A filter on each page hides the weaker tiers. When something could not be found, the dossier says so and names what it looked for.

Corrections

Organizations and readers may request a correction to any factual claim. Corrections that check out are made and noted on the page.

The fine print

Dossiers are compiled from public records by research agents and arithmetic on filed numbers. Every claim is tagged evidence, inference or speculation and carries its sources. Nothing in a dossier is legal, financial, tax or investment advice, and the organization assessed has not reviewed it. Verify anything you intend to act on.
