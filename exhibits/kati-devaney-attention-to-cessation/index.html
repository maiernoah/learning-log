Kati Devaney's Path from Attention to Cessation

Follow one neuroscientist's papers from mapping vision in a single brain to recording meditation's deepest states, and try each key idea yourself.

[ ◄ Noah's Weblog | About ]

Start here

This page teaches nine papers by the neuroscientist Kati Devaney (published as Kathryn J. Devaney) and her colleagues, published from 2016 to 2026, in the order they appeared. It is a study guide written in plain language, not a copy of the originals. About the sources.

"…what is called our 'experience' is almost entirely determined by our habits of attention."
William James, 1890. Devaney chose this line to open her 2018 dissertation (p. iv, Devaney 2018).

You can't see attention. But a brain scanner can watch the brain doing it. Over ten years, one scientist's work moved from mapping where a single brain sees, to how attention and daydreaming take turns, to what is different in people who have meditated for thousands of hours. It ends at the edge of experience itself: states where meditators report that awareness switches off.

Following one researcher's papers in order also shows how science really moves. Each answer raises the next question. Some predictions fail. Later papers correct earlier ones.

By the end you'll be able to

  • Explain how a brain scan can map a region in one person, and why averaging many brains can hide it.
  • Describe the "seesaw" between the brain's attention network and its default mode network, and read a correlation like r = −0.55.
  • Compare what meditators' brains showed during tasks and at rest, and name the predictions that failed.
  • Calculate a walker's stride variability and explain how it related to the seesaw.
  • Define cessation and jhana, and judge how strong the evidence about them is so far.

1. A map inside one brain (2016)

In this part: see how the outside world is laid out on the back of the brain, and how scanning one teenager tied her blind area to a damaged pathway.

Main source: Merabet, Devaney and colleagues (2016), a case study. Devaney's role: second of six authors; all six analyzed and interpreted the data (p. 6).

Words to know

Your visual field is everything you can see while your eyes stay still. V1 is the first patch of the cortex (the brain's wrinkled outer layer) to receive visual signals. It sits at the very back of the head. fMRI is a scanner method that tracks changes in blood oxygen, which rise where brain cells are working harder.

The patient and the puzzle

Cerebral or cortical visual impairment, or CVI, is the leading cause of visual impairment in children in developed countries. The trouble comes from injury to the brain's visual pathways around birth, rather than from disease of the eyes alone (p. 1).

The patient was a 17-year-old girl born eight weeks early. A standard vision test showed that she could not see the lower part of her visual field in either eye. The loss appeared worse on the left (p. 2). The question was whether brain scans could explain that exact pattern.

The brain's map is flipped twice

The visual field is laid out across V1 like a map, called a retinotopic map. It is flipped two ways. The left half of what you see is handled by the right side of the brain. And the lower half of what you see lands on the upper (dorsal) bank of V1. So the lower-left corner of your view lives in the dorsal V1 of the right hemisphere (p. 4).

Try it

Pick a corner of the visual field and see which patch of V1 handles it. Then show the patient's scan results.

Three measurements that agreed

The team combined three kinds of evidence from the same person (pp. 2–4):

  • A clinical test of what she could see showed the lower field missing, worse on the left.
  • Diffusion imaging traced the optic radiations, the cables carrying signals to V1. Compared with a matched 17-year-old, she appeared to have far fewer traced fibers, especially along the upper branch of the optic radiations and on the right side (p. 3).
  • fMRI showed that her dorsal V1, which handles the lower field, responded at only about 22% of the control's level (21.5% on the right, 22.2% on the left). Her ventral V1, which handles the upper field, responded at about 90% (p. 4).

The authors call this, to their knowledge, the first report to tie a CVI patient's field loss to both structure and function this way (p. 5). They also list the limits. It is one patient. Fewer traced fibers does not prove the fibers are gone, since tracing can miss them. And fMRI measures blood flow, which is only an indirect sign of brain cells firing (p. 5).

An everyday comparison

Analogy: think of a pinhole camera. Light through a tiny hole paints the scene on the back wall upside down and swapped left to right. Where it breaks down: in the brain, the flips come from how nerve fibers are routed, and the two halves of the scene land in different hemispheres. Also, a camera gives every part of the picture equal space. V1 does not. (From outside the paper: the center of your gaze gets much more cortex than the edges.)

Why? Dorsal V2 lost less than dorsal V1 (about 34% versus 22% of normal). Why would the next area be less affected?

V2 cells each cover a bigger patch of the visual field than V1 cells do. So dorsal V2 also picks up a little of the upper field, which still worked (p. 5).

A common mix-up"If part of your view is missing, your eyes must be damaged." In CVI the main damage is in the brain's pathways. This study found the blind area by looking behind the eyes, at the cables and at V1 (pp. 1–5).

This paper shows a habit that returns in Devaney's TPJ work (part 4): measure the brain of one person carefully, and line it up with what that person can actually do.

2. The brain's seesaw

In this part: meet the two networks that most of this arc is about, and learn to read the number that says how they move together.

Sources: Devaney's dissertation (2018) and the papers in parts 5 to 7. This part sets up the idea that ties the arc together.

Words to know

A brain network is a set of separate regions whose activity tends to rise and fall together. A correlation, written r, is a number from −1 to +1 that says how closely two measurements move together. A negative r is an anticorrelation: when one goes up, the other tends to go down.

Looking out and looking in

Devaney's dissertation builds on a simple split in experience. Sometimes the contents of your mind come from outside, through the senses. Sometimes they come from inside: memories, plans, thoughts about yourself. She notes that the brain mirrors this split, with networks for outward and inward attention that move in opposite directions (p. 3, Devaney 2018).

The dorsal attention network (DAN) includes the intraparietal sulcus, a groove toward the top and back of the head, and the frontal eye fields. It supports sustained, focused attention (p. 2, Devaney 2021). The default mode network (DMN) includes the posterior cingulate, the middle of the frontal lobe, and the angular gyrus. Its activity drops during demanding tasks (p. 2, Devaney 2021). It is busy when you remember the past, imagine the future, or think about other people (p. 3, Devaney 2018).

A third network, the ventral attention network (VAN), works like a circuit breaker. When something sudden and important appears, it interrupts your focus and turns you toward it (p. 1, Devaney 2021). Its key hub, the temporoparietal junction, is the subject of part 4.

Watching networks at rest

Regions that work together during a task also keep fluctuating together when a person lies in the scanner doing nothing in particular. Researchers correlate these slow fluctuations to see which regions belong together. This is called resting-state functional connectivity (p. 2, Devaney 2021). At rest, the DAN and DMN are usually anticorrelated (p. 666, Tripathi 2024).

Several studies link a stronger anticorrelation with better attention and healthier aging. Devaney and colleagues call its strength a potential candidate marker of brain health, a careful phrase (p. 2, Devaney 2021). Later work adds a caution: the two networks are not strictly opposed, and their relationship shifts with mental state (p. e332, Lo 2021).

Why? Why might a strong seesaw help someone stay focused?

This is the authors' interpretation, not a proven mechanism. If the inward-looking network quiets down whenever the outward-looking one engages, inner chatter competes less with the task. Lo and colleagues describe the seesaw as reflecting a person's ability to switch between outside tasks and inner thought (p. e331, Lo 2021).

A common mix-up"Default mode" sounds like "doing nothing." The DMN is busy during remembering, planning, imagining, and thinking about other minds (p. 3, Devaney 2018). It is quiet mainly when your attention is pulled outward.

Another mix-up"Anticorrelated means one network switches off when the other switches on." An r of −0.55 describes a tendency across many moments, not an on/off switch. Both networks are always somewhat active. The number also depends on how the networks are defined, as part 7 shows (p. 671, Tripathi 2024).

3. Memory steers attention (2018)

In this part: see how an experiment pulled "memory guiding attention" apart from memory alone and attention alone, and what it found.

Main source: Rosen, Stern, Devaney and Somers (2018), Cerebral Cortex. Devaney's role: third of four authors.

Words to know

Long-term memory holds what you learned hours, days, or years ago. Covert attention means shifting your focus to a spot without moving your eyes. A control condition is a version of the task that keeps everything the same except the one ingredient being tested.

The puzzle

Attention can only handle a little at once. Yet people see very well in familiar places. The authors' answer is that long-term memory tells attention where to look (p. 2935). You don't scan your whole kitchen for the salt. You look where you know it is.

The design: remove one ingredient at a time

On day 1, 24 adults learned word–location pairs and word–object pairs (pp. 2936–2937). On day 2, in the scanner, each trial began with a cue, then a brief flash of nine objects. There were three conditions (p. 2937):

Task ingredients in each condition
ConditionWhat you doRecall from memory?Move attention covertly?
Memory-guided attentionSee a word, recall the location you learned, attend there✓ yes✓ yes
Stimulus-guided attentionAn arrow shows where to attendminimal✓ yes
Memory retrievalSee a word, recall its paired object, check the center✓ yesminimal

Each control matches one ingredient and drops the other. The team also made every image equally familiar, so familiarity alone could not explain a difference (p. 2937). The two attention tasks were equally accurate: 94.0% with memory and 95.0% with the arrow, a difference too small to count. Memory-guided answers were slightly slower, 921 versus 898 milliseconds (p. 2941).

What lit up

Three regions in the parietal lobe, on both sides of the brain, were more active for memory-guided attention than for either control. They are the lateral intraparietal sulcus, the posterior precuneus, and the posterior callosal sulcus (pp. 2941–2942). Three deeper structures showed the same pattern: the head of the caudate, the mediodorsal thalamus, and a part of the cerebellum (pp. 2942–2943).

The classic dorsal attention network was strongly active in both attention conditions, but it did not tell them apart (p. 2942). The hippocampus, often linked to memory, showed no clear role here (p. 2943). The authors propose that the six regions form a Memory-Attention Network that blends what you remember with what you see (p. 2944).

One detail connects to the rest of the arc. These thin parietal regions sit right beside default mode network regions. The authors note it is possible that earlier studies missed them for that reason, though their preferred explanation is that the regions serve memory-guided attention in particular (p. 2944). Thin regions that sit side by side need careful mapping. That is the problem part 4 takes on.

Try it

Sort each everyday moment by the condition it most resembles.

Why? Why did the team need two different control conditions instead of one?

With only the arrow condition, a region that lit up more for memory-guided attention might just be doing memory recall. With only the memory-retrieval condition, it might just be doing attention. A region that beats both controls is a candidate for combining them (p. 2936). The authors add a caution: in some regions the extra activity could simply be memory and attention added together, and only some regions argued against that (p. 2944).

"Minimal" because no control can remove an ingredient completely; each is just much less present (p. 2936).

A common mix-up"If the attention network is active, that's where memory-guided attention happens." The dorsal attention network was active in both attention tasks equally. The difference that mattered showed up somewhere else (p. 2942).

4. Finding the surprise detector (2019)

In this part: see why averaging brains can blur a small region, and how vivid surprise photos made it show up in each person.

Main source: Devaney, Rosen, Levin and Somers (2019). Devaney's role: first author; she and Somers designed the study and drafted the paper (p. 10). It grew out of her 2018 dissertation.

Words to know

The temporoparietal junction (TPJ) is where the temporal and parietal lobes meet, on the side of the brain. An oddball is a rare, unexpected event in a stream of ordinary ones. A group average lines up many people's brains on a template and averages them. A localizer is a short task used to find a region in one person before studying it.

A hard region to pin down

The TPJ has been linked to reorienting attention, to updating your picture of a situation, and to understanding other minds (p. 1). It is one of the parts of the cortex that expanded most between monkeys and humans. Its shape and connections also vary a lot from person to person (p. 1). Most studies found it by averaging many brains, which blurs exactly this kind of region (p. 2).

The standard test made things harder. In a spatial cueing task, an arrow points to where a target will probably appear. When the target appears somewhere else (an "invalid" trial), you must reorient. That contrast found the right TPJ in a group average, but single-person maps were "typically blank" (p. 6).

Try it: why averaging blurs

Each square below is a patch of cortex. Every pretend person has a strong spot, but in a slightly different place. Average more people and watch the spot fade.

The fix: vivid, novel oddballs

Devaney's team kept the cueing task and added a jolt (p. 3). On a random one in six trials, five full-screen color photos flashed behind the mask of letters that followed the target, 100 milliseconds each. No photo was ever shown twice, because responses to a repeated surprise shrink fast (pp. 2–3). Photos with prominent faces were left out, since a nearby region responds to faces (p. 3). They called it the vivid, novel oddball (ViNO) paradigm (p. 2). In a pilot study with four people, the response was most consistent and robust when oddballs came on one trial in six. Too often, and they stop being surprising. The authors took this rise-and-fall pattern as a sign that surprise, not just bright pictures, drove the response (p. 3).

Put the steps of one trial in order.

Results in ten people

Ten adults took part (p. 3). Reorienting cost time: 724 milliseconds after a misleading arrow versus 683 after a correct one (p. 6). The oddball contrast found two TPJ regions in individual brains (p. 7):

  • A posterior region along the superior temporal sulcus (TPJSTS), found in 10 of 10 right hemispheres and 9 of 10 left.
  • An anterior region in the supramarginal gyrus (TPJSMG), found in 6 of 10 right hemispheres and 5 of 10 left.

Two people were scanned three times over many months, and their regions stayed in the same places (pp. 6–7). The key check came next. The oddball-defined TPJSTS also responded to misleading arrows, on both sides of the brain. So the right TPJSTS matches the region that cueing studies had been finding. The right TPJSMG did not respond to the arrows (pp. 7–8). And in every person, oddballs drove the right TPJSTS harder than misleading arrows did, even when the region was defined by the arrow task itself (p. 8).

The authors note one limit: eye movements were not tracked (p. 9). Her dissertation used the same localizer to compare the TPJ across tasks. Attention overlapped with stopping an action in the front part of the TPJ, and with thinking about others' beliefs (theory of mind) in the back part (p. ix, Devaney 2018).

Why? Why did oddballs work better than the classic misleading-arrow trick?

The photos combined two strong drivers: a large, vivid image and real surprise. Earlier localizers leaned on one or the other. Together they made a signal strong enough to see in a single brain (pp. 2, 9).

A common mix-up"Averaging more brains always gives a clearer picture." Averaging helps when everyone's region sits in the same place. When positions differ, averaging smears the region and can merge it with neighbors that do something else (p. 2).

5. Meditators in the scanner (2021)

In this part: compare long-term meditators with matched controls, and sort the team's predictions into the ones that held and the ones that failed.

Main source: Devaney, Levin, Tripathi, Higgins, Lazar and Somers (2021), Brain Sciences. Devaney's role: first author; the study was part of her PhD (p. 17).

Words to know

Vipassana is a Buddhist meditation tradition. The style studied here is focused attention meditation: holding attention on subtle body sensations and calmly returning whenever it drifts (p. 2). An effect size says how big a difference is, not just whether it is likely real. A p-value says how surprising the data would be if there were no real difference.

Meditation as attention training

The authors define meditation, for research purposes, as non-judgmental attention to what you sense in the present moment (p. 1). A typical Vipassana retreat lasts 10 days, with 10 to 12 hours of practice a day (p. 2). Because the practice means sustaining attention and refusing to follow distractions, the team treated it as years of training for sustained attention (p. 2).

Who took part

Sixteen meditators came from a Vipassana center in Massachusetts (p. 3). On average they had practiced for 7 years, about 14 hours a week, and had spent 291 days on silent retreats. Their estimated lifetime practice ranged from 1,300 to 50,000 hours (p. 4). Fourteen controls were matched on age, gender, education, handedness, and number of native languages (p. 3).

One clever detail: the meditators knew they had been picked for their skill, which could make them try harder. So controls were told they had been picked for a skill they rated themselves highly on, such as swimming. These are sham-expertise controls (p. 4).

Two tasks, four predictions

In multiple object tracking, four of twelve discs flash red, then all twelve drift around for 12.7 seconds while you track the four. In "passive" trials all twelve discs flash red and then move the same way, so there is nothing to track. Comparing the two isolates attention (pp. 4–5). The second task was the oddball task from part 4 (pp. 5–7).

The team predicted that meditators would (p. 3):

  1. track the discs better;
  2. filter out the surprise photos better;
  3. show a bigger gap between DAN activation and DMN quieting while tracking;
  4. show less ventral attention network activity to the oddballs.

Try it

Before reading the results, sort these findings. Did the study support them or not?

What the study found

Only prediction 3 held. Tracking scores did not reliably differ (a score of 1.25 versus 0.82, p = 0.28) (pp. 8–9). But the gap between DAN activation and DMN quieting was larger in meditators, with a large effect size of 0.88 (p. 10). Taken on its own, only the DMN difference was statistically significant: meditators quieted it about twice as much as controls (p. 11). The DAN rose more in raw terms, but with a smaller effect size (0.65 versus 0.82), so its difference was only a trend (pp. 10–11).

On the oddball task, the extra time meditators needed after a misleading arrow was about the same as controls' (42 versus 57 milliseconds), and their ventral attention network reacted about the same (pp. 11–13). Their TPJ response to oddballs was slightly smaller, a small effect the authors call an initial trend (p. 16). A surprise: meditators were slower, by 125 milliseconds on correctly cued trials, and by a non-significant 110 on the others. More years of practice went with slower answers (pp. 11–12). Since the extra time was similar on every trial type, it does not mean slower reorienting. The authors suggest the groups may simply have chosen different speed strategies (p. 16).

Another twist: in controls, brain activity tracked task scores, as expected. In meditators the link was weaker or absent (pp. 11, 15). The paper reports these numbers inconsistently, so treat this as tentative. The authors call it a decoupling and suggest that training may change attention networks in different ways for different people (p. 15).

At rest

Thirteen meditators also had resting scans (p. 14). They were told not to meditate (p. 4). Their DAN–DMN correlation averaged −0.55, versus −0.41 for 21 lab controls and −0.39 for 168 people from the Human Connectome Project (p. 14). The two control groups did not differ from each other. So the stronger seesaw showed up during a task and at rest.

Why? Why compare meditators with two different control groups at rest?

Some of the original matched controls dropped out, so the team added people scanned on the same machine. The large public sample checks that the lab controls weren't unusual. The two control groups matched each other, and meditators differed from both (pp. 13–14).

A common mix-up"The study shows meditation causes a stronger seesaw." It compared groups at one point in time, a cross-sectional study. People who choose years of meditation may differ in other ways. The authors' own figure caption goes further than the design allows, saying practice "brings long-term changes" (p. 14). In their conclusions they call for longitudinal studies that scan people before and after training (p. 16).

6. The seesaw and walking (2021)

In this part: calculate how steady a walker's strides are, and see how steadiness related to the seesaw in three different groups.

Main source: Lo, Halko, Devaney, Wayne, Lipsitz and Manor (2021), Journals of Gerontology. Devaney's role: third of six authors; she helped analyze and interpret the data (p. e333).

Words to know

Stride time is the time from one heel strike to the next heel strike of the same foot. The standard deviation measures how far values typically sit from their average. The coefficient of variation (CV) is the standard deviation divided by the average, times 100. It turns spread into a percentage.

Why stride timing matters

No two strides are exactly alike. How much stride time varies, called gait variability, predicts future falls, frailty, and cognitive decline in older adults (p. e328). Walking also takes attention: you keep track of your body and the path while ignoring distractions (p. e329). An earlier small study had found that older adults with steadier strides had a stronger DAN–DMN seesaw. This study asked whether that link would show up again in new groups, alongside the original one (p. e329).

Worked example

Five strides, in seconds: 1.00, 1.02, 0.98, 1.01, 0.99 1. Average: (1.00 + 1.02 + 0.98 + 1.01 + 0.99) ÷ 5 = 1.00 s.
2. Differences from the average: 0, +0.02, −0.02, +0.01, −0.01.
3. Square them and add: 0 + 0.0004 + 0.0004 + 0.0001 + 0.0001 = 0.0010.
4. Divide by one less than the number of strides (4), then take the square root: √0.00025 = 0.016 s. That is the standard deviation.
5. CV = 0.016 ÷ 1.00 × 100 = 1.6%.

Try it

Change the stride times, or load a preset, and watch the CV change.

Three groups, one pattern

The team pulled data from three earlier studies, each a separate cohort (p. e329). The original group had 12 older adults who walked slowly and had mild-to-moderate trouble with planning and focus. A new group had 12 healthy older adults matched to them by age and sex. The third had 15 people with early Parkinson's disease.

In all three groups, people with steadier strides had a stronger negative correlation between the DAN and DMN at rest. This held after accounting for age and sex (p. e331). No other pair of networks showed the link in all three groups (p. e331). One participant with r = −0.86 had a stride CV of 1.98%. Another, with r = 0.01, had 3.87%. Both came from the Parkinson's group (p. e332). You can load both on the seesaw explorer in part 2.

The authors read this as evidence that steady walking draws on the same seesaw that supports sustained attention (p. e331). They list the limits. The groups were small. The groups were scanned on different machines and their walking was measured two different ways, so the data could not be pooled. And the data come from one point in time, so they can't show what causes what (pp. e332–e333).

Why? Why is finding the link in three separate small groups worth more than finding it once?

The first group is where the link was first reported. The healthy and Parkinson's groups are two replications. The link held across three different scanners, two ways of measuring walking, and people with and without health problems. A pattern that survives those differences is less likely to come from one lab's equipment or one odd sample (pp. e329, e332).

A common mix-up"So a weak seesaw makes people fall." Falls were not measured here. Gait variability predicts falls in other studies, and this study shows only that steadiness and the seesaw go together (pp. e328, e333).

7. Silence and the language network (2024)

In this part: see what changed when the team split the default mode network more finely, and what hundreds of days of silence might do to the brain's language network.

Main source: Tripathi, Devaney, Lazar and Somers (2024), Mindfulness. Devaney's role: second of four authors; she worked on the methods, data collection, and data management (p. 672).

Words to know

The language network is a set of regions, mostly on the left side, that process, produce, and understand language (pp. 666–667). A parcellation is a brain atlas that divides the cortex into labeled parts. A retrospective study looks back at data that were collected for another purpose.

An idea from an old text

Silence is part of several meditation traditions, but its effect on the brain had barely been studied (pp. 665–666). The idea for this study came from Patanjali's Yoga Sutras (p. 671). That text lists five "modulations" of the mind, and describes imagination as knowing a nonexistent thing through words (p. 666). If inner chatter rides on language, the team reasoned, long silence might loosen the ties between the language network and the DAN and DMN (p. 666).

The data

The team reanalyzed resting scans from the 13 meditators in part 5, who averaged 291 days on silent retreats. They compared them with 34 local controls and 169 people from the Human Connectome Project (pp. 666–667). This time they used a finer atlas. They took only the core of the DMN, and kept the language network as its own network (p. 667).

Try it

Switch between the groups and compare the three connections.

What they found

In meditators, the core DMN and the language network were essentially uncoupled (r = −0.05), while the Connectome sample showed a positive link (0.14) (p. 668). The DAN and the language network were negatively correlated in meditators (−0.20) and near zero in both control groups (pp. 668–669). Meditators with more days in silence tended to have a more negative DAN–language link (r = −0.54). With so few people, that fell just short of the usual bar for significance (p = 0.06) (p. 669).

A correction to the earlier paper

Here is the surprise. With the finer atlas, the core DMN–DAN seesaw was not significantly stronger in meditators (−0.15) than in local controls (−0.08). The Connectome sample had the strongest seesaw of all (−0.37) (p. 668). That seems to contradict part 5. The authors explain it. The 2021 study used a coarse seven-network atlas whose "DMN" also contained pieces of the language network. The finer analysis suggests that the language network pulling away was a key driver of the earlier result (p. 671).

The authors list limits: no language tests, a small group, mostly male meditators because of word-of-mouth recruiting, and a retrospective design that can't show cause (p. 671).

Why? Why did the team drop the most experienced meditator before computing the silence correlation?

They set a rule to remove anyone more than two standard deviations from the average. One person, an outlier, had far more days of silence than anyone else. With only 13 people, one extreme point can swing a correlation (p. 669). That person's connection looked like the least experienced meditators'. The authors wonder whether the effect rises and then falls with experience, but they could not test it (p. 671).

Reading tipThe paper's summary at the top calls the silence correlation a "strong inverse relation." The results section reports it did not reach statistical significance (pp. 665, 669). When a summary and the results disagree, trust the results.

A common mix-up"p = 0.06 means a 94% chance the effect is real." A p-value doesn't measure that. It says how often data this extreme would turn up if there were no real effect. (This is standard statistics, from outside the paper.)

8. When experience stops (2023)

In this part: define cessation, follow the path meditators describe toward it, and sort each claim by how strong its evidence is.

Main source: Laukkonen, Sacchet, Barendregt, Devaney, Chowdhury and Slagter (2023), Progress in Brain Research. Devaney's role: fourth of six authors. All six took part in recording brain and body data during cessation events (p. 2), though the two early EEG studies below were run by other members of the team (pp. 10–11). Page numbers point to the journal's early online version.

Words to know

EEG records the brain's electrical rhythms through sensors on the scalp. The alpha band is the rhythm around 8 to 13 cycles per second. Jhanas are a ladder of deep meditative states described in Buddhist texts. Nirodha is Pali for "cessation."

Turning experience off from the inside

People lose consciousness through anesthesia, a concussion, or fainting. Some meditators report something stranger: they can switch off all experience on purpose, for up to seven days (p. 1). The paper describes two forms (p. 3):

  • Nirodha: a brief gap in experience, lasting milliseconds to seconds. It can happen without planning in deep insight practice, and is often followed by a sense of clarity.
  • Nirodha samapatti (NS, "cessation attainment"): entered on purpose, for a length of time set in advance. Traditional texts say it requires mastering all eight jhanas, and only very advanced meditators can do it (pp. 3, 7–8).

Classical texts say NS is unlike deep sleep. Afterward there is no sense that time passed, there are no dreams, and the person can't be woken by touch or pain, except, the authors note, by a cue the meditator intends to wake to (pp. 2, 19). The body is said to stay stiff rather than relaxed (p. 1). It is also different from "pure awareness" experiences, because in cessation there is no awareness at all (p. 4). The authors' working definition is (p. 5):

"The absence of all experience and consciousness—with no retrospective awareness of anything having taken place during the absence—accompanied by a subsequent profound sense of clarity, openness, and vitality."

The path, as the texts describe it

The first four jhanas are called "form" jhanas. Each drops a quality of mind, ending in equanimity. The next four are "formless": infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither perception nor non-perception (pp. 6–7). Each step leaves less content in experience. After the eighth may come a "signless" state, a step the paper marks as uncertain. Then, if conditions are right, NS (pp. 5–7).

The first measurements

The authors stress that these early findings "cannot yet be used to draw strong conclusions" (p. 10).

  • One meditator with more than 6,000 hours of retreat practice was recorded with EEG, and 37 clear nirodha events were selected for analysis (pp. 10–11). On average, about 20 seconds before each one, coordination between brain regions in the alpha band began a steady decline. It was lowest just after the cessation and recovered over the next 3 to 40 seconds. Only the alpha band changed (p. 11).
  • A second study compared one meditator's NS with ordinary rest and with a nap. NS also showed weaker alpha coordination (p. 12).
  • Similar alpha breakdowns happen when people lose consciousness under two anesthetics, propofol and ketamine (pp. 12, 15–16).
  • An earlier study by another team found something different in two meditators: more coordination in a faster rhythm, the gamma band (p. 11).

One observation stands out (p. 21). A meditator set an intention to stay in NS for 90 minutes. For 90 minutes there was no visible movement, and EEG, breathing, and heart rate looked steady. Near the 90-minute mark, heart rate rose by about 10 beats per minute and eye movements began. The meditator opened their eyes at 104 minutes. The authors say this needs careful replication. They also note that the cooling and slowed heart rate described in stories about NS were not seen (p. 14).

A proposed explanation

The authors use predictive processing, the idea that the brain constantly predicts what it will sense next (p. 12). They suggest meditation gradually shortens how far ahead the brain predicts. Cessation might be the final step: letting go of even the expectation to be awake (p. 13). They call this speculative. They also propose a testable prediction: right after cessation, visual illusions might weaken, because the brain leans less on its old expectations (p. 19).

Studying cessation is hard. Practitioners are rare, nirodha lasts an instant, and a person in NS can't press buttons or answer questions. Reports come afterward, and self-reports can be biased (pp. 9, 20). The authors propose methods that need no response during the state, careful interviews afterward, and full body measurements (p. 20). They also note that intensive meditation carries real risks, and that people on long retreats need support (p. 10).

Try it

Sort each statement by where its support comes from.

Why? Why would researchers care about a state that only a few people can reach?

Rare cases have taught neuroscience a lot, like patients with unusual brain injuries. Cessation is a switch-off of consciousness that is not caused by a drug or an injury. Meditators can also repeat it and describe the moments around it, which allows repeated measurements (pp. 17, 21).

A common mix-up"Cessation is just falling asleep." Practitioners report no sense of time passing, no dreams, and no waking to touch or pain (p. 2). In the early EEG data, NS also differed from a nap (p. 12). The authors do think it may share some bodily features with sleep (p. 14).

9. Recording jhana: an open dataset (2026)

In this part: see what is in the largest set of brain recordings from jhana meditation so far (by the authors' count), why it is shared openly, and how to read the authors' disclosures.

Main source: Fabus, Zerfas, Gruver, Fini, Gadaev and Devaney (2026), a bioRxiv preprint. Devaney's role: last author, the usual spot for the senior researcher.

Words to know

An open dataset is a set of recordings anyone can download and analyze. An artifact is a signal in a recording that doesn't come from the source you care about, like a muscle twitch in a brain recording. A preprint is a paper shared before peer review. A conflict of interest is a tie, often financial, that could sway an author's judgment.

What jhana feels like, as described

The paper describes the four form jhanas this way: the first brings intense bodily bliss, often with smiling and tense muscles. In the second, joy and happiness fill the body. The third is a deep contentment. The fourth is profound equanimity (p. 2). Until now, brain evidence came from single cases and small groups (pp. 2–3).

Put the four form jhanas in order, first to fourth.

What was recorded

Twenty-six expert meditators, defined as people who can reliably enter jhana at will, were recorded at three retreats. That makes 71 sessions and more than 100 hours (pp. 3–4, 9). The largest group averaged about 9,700 hours of lifetime practice, ranging from 300 to 67,000 (p. 7).

Each person wore a 64-sensor EEG cap plus sensors for the eyes, heart, breathing, and several muscles, including the main smiling muscle. Most also wore skin sensors (pp. 5, 10). Smiling is common in the early jhanas, and muscle signals can swamp brain signals. So each session began with deliberate artifacts: blinking, looking left and right, clenching the jaw, a big smile, a gentle smile, swallowing (p. 6). Analysts can use these to learn what each kind of noise looks like.

A first look, not a finding

As a demonstration, the team mapped alpha power across the scalp. The jhana stages "appeared to have distinct alpha topographies," but the authors add that this "was not formally tested" (p. 10). All the data are in a standard format, with code to load and analyze them (pp. 8, 11). That lets other teams run the real tests.

Reading the disclosures

Two notes belong next to any claim from this paper. First, it is a preprint, not yet peer reviewed (p. 1). Second, three authors work for Jhourney, a start-up building jhana neurofeedback devices. The other three, including Devaney, do occasional paid consulting for it, and Devaney is its Chief Scientific Advisor (p. 12). Disclosing ties like these is standard and honest. It tells readers where to look twice. Releasing the raw data openly is one good answer, because anyone can check the work.

Why? Why record "plan your day" and mental arithmetic before and after the meditation?

They give everyday thinking states to compare against, recorded on the same person with the same sensors on the same day (p. 6).

A common mix-up"This paper shows each jhana has its own brain signature." It does not claim that. It releases data and shows a first look that was not formally tested (p. 10). Testing it is the next step, and anyone can do it.

10. Reading the arc

In this part: put the papers in order, name the kind of evidence each one gives, and see which questions are still open.

The thread

Some concerns run through the whole arc. Careful mapping in single brains (parts 1 and 4) and telling thin neighboring regions apart (part 3) come up again and again. The seesaw between looking out and looking in (part 2) became the measure for studying trained minds (part 5) and walking (part 6). A finer atlas then revised the meditation result (part 7). The latest work moves to the far edge: states where experience fills with bliss, or stops entirely (parts 8 and 9).

Put these papers in the order they were published.

Kinds of evidence

Not every study can answer every question. One person, carefully measured, shows that something can happen. A comparison of groups at one moment shows a difference exists, but not what caused it. A dataset answers nothing by itself, but lets many people test ideas. Knowing the kind of study tells you what it can show.

Still open

  • Does meditation cause the network differences seen in meditators, such as the larger DAN–DMN gap during tasks and the language network pulling away? That needs scans of the same people before and after training (p. 16, Devaney 2021). Devaney's dissertation already listed a training study of new meditators as a next step (p. xiv, Devaney 2018).
  • Does silence itself change the language network, and does that matter for speech or thought? (p. 671, Tripathi 2024)
  • What happens in the body during longer cessations, and how does it compare with sleep? (pp. 19–21, Laukkonen 2023)
  • Do the jhana stages really differ in the brain? The open data now let anyone test it (p. 10, Fabus 2026).

What this guide simplifies or leaves out

  • Scanning methods and most statistics are left out. Brain regions are named in plain words, and the parietal and subcortical details in part 3 are condensed.
  • The dissertation is covered only through its abstract and opening pages. The 2021 paper states that it is part of that dissertation (p. 17, Devaney 2021).
  • The DAN–DMN "seesaw" is a simplification. The 2021 gait paper and the 2024 silence paper both stress that the relationship shifts with mental state and with how networks are defined.
  • The cessation paper's Buddhist background, its long discussion of predictive processing and integrated information theory, and its notes on hibernation are only sketched. Its brain findings come from single meditators and are described by the authors as preliminary.
  • The sources disagree with themselves in a few places, and this guide follows the methods and results sections. The silence paper's abstract and results describe the silence correlation differently, give different practice-hour figures, and give two different figures for the excluded meditator's days in silence. The 2021 meditation paper reports the meditators' brain–behavior correlations inconsistently. The 2019 TPJ paper swaps the two rescan intervals between its text and its figure. The jhana preprint says its retreats ran from March to June 2023 but dates one retreat to November 2022.
  • The averaging, seesaw, and stride tools on this page use simulated numbers. Reported values are labeled as reported.

Short quotations are marked and attributed. Figures are new drawings, not copies.
