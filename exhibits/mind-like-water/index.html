Mind Like Water

Learn David Allen's Getting Things Done, read as a spiritual practice in disguise: a way to be fully present with whatever is in front of you.

Start here

This page teaches David Allen's Getting Things Done (2001, revised 2015) and Ready for Anything (2003), together with five recorded conversations with Allen from 2020 to 2025. It is a study guide written in plain language, not a copy of the originals. About the source.

You already carry a list in your head. The essay. The text you have not answered. The friend you owe an apology. Whether to quit the team. That list never switches off, and it has a habit of speaking up at three in the morning. David Allen spent forty years teaching busy people how to work, and he says the pull of that list is optional. He also says, often, that the goal was never to get more done. The goal was to be fully here.

This page reads his method as a practice of presence that happens to use lists. That reading is ours. Allen's own words on what his method is and is not are in part 7, next to a comparison with sitting meditation (meditation done seated and still, usually with the eyes closed).

By the end you'll be able to

  • Explain what Allen means by mind like water and appropriate engagement.
  • Run any worry through his two questions: what does done look like, and what is the next action?
  • Name his five steps in order, and sort the things on your mind into the places his system keeps them.
  • Describe the weekly review and say why he calls it the hardest habit to build.
  • Compare his practice with sitting meditation, and say where he agrees with it and where he does not.
  • Predict what Allen says happens when you break an agreement with yourself.

Guess first

Answer these before you start. Guessing wrong is fine; trying first makes the answers stick.

1. The pebble and the pond

In this part: what Allen promises, and why the promise is a state of mind rather than an empty inbox.

Words to know

Mind like water is Allen's name for a mind that answers each thing with exactly the attention it deserves, then settles. Appropriate engagement is being OK enough with something that it stops pulling at you. The ready state is the martial artist's stance: relaxed, awake, able to move in any direction.

Early in his book, Allen borrows an image from karate. Throw a pebble into a still pond. The water answers with exactly the force of the pebble, no more and no less, and then it is still again. He calls this "mind like water." (GTD ch. 1, "The 'Mind Like Water' Simile")

Most of us do the opposite. We give an email the response a crisis deserves, or we ignore a problem until it grows teeth. Whatever makes you overreact or underreact, he says, ends up controlling you. (GTD ch. 1)

The karate lesson behind the image is about power. A punch gets its force from speed, not muscle, and a tense muscle is a slow one. Small people break boards because they are loose and fast. So advanced training in the martial arts teaches relaxation as much as anything else. (GTD ch. 1)

Then comes a surprising admission. Allen says the title of his own book misleads people. It is not about getting more done. It is about appropriate engagement: being OK enough with where something stands that it stops pulling on your attention. (GTD, introduction to the revised edition; glossary) In a 2021 conversation he put it bluntly: the method is far more about getting present than about getting things done. (Stoa 2021) He also says you cannot manage time at all. You cannot stretch five minutes into six. What you can manage is what you do, which is why the whole method is built around actions. (GTD ch. 1, "The Process: Managing Action")

In Ready for Anything he adds a condition. Water behaves this way by design. A person can only do it with a conscious system, built on principles that survive chaos and that line up with something deeper in our nature. The ready state of the martial artist is not passive, he writes. It is alive, creative, and expansive, and it is earned by training. (RFA, introduction)

An everyday picture

Think of a thermostat. It notices the room, responds by exactly the amount needed, and then rests. It does not panic at a draft or ignore a fire. The analogy breaks down in one place: a thermostat has a single input. You have hundreds, and you also choose which ones to let in.

Allen's own picture is blunter. If four people jump you in a dark alley, you do not want two thousand unanswered emails rattling around your head. You need to be clear. (Stoa 2022; Modern Wisdom 2020)

Why does Allen tie power to relaxing instead of trying harder?

Because speed needs looseness. A tense muscle is slow, so a tense fighter is weak. He makes the same claim about attention. A mind braced against fifty things cannot move quickly to the one in front of it. Clearing the mind, he says, is the key to being open and appropriately responsive. (GTD ch. 1)

A common mix-up"Mind like water means being calm and unbothered." Not quite. Allen says the image never assumes the water is undisturbed. Water engages fully with whatever hits it, and then returns. The goal is the right-sized response, not no response. (GTD ch. 15)

2. Open loops and the 3 a.m. voice

In this part: why unfinished business nags, what makes it stop, and the five steps that do the stopping.

Words to know

An open loop is anything pulling at your attention that is not where it belongs, the way it belongs. Allen treats every open loop as an agreement with yourself. The part of the mind that tries to hold them he calls psychic RAM (RAM is a computer's short-term working memory). The low hum they produce together he calls ambient anxiety. The containers you gather things into, from a paper tray to a notes app, he calls "in".

Allen's diagnosis is simple. Most stress comes from commitments you have made or accepted and not handled well. Every "I should" is an agreement with yourself, and a less-than-conscious part of you tracks every one, from "end world hunger" to "replace the porch lightbulb." (GTD ch. 1, "The Principle: Dealing Effectively with Internal Commitments")

The part of the mind that holds these agreements has no sense of past or future. So it pushes on all of them at once, all the time. (RFA 2, 6) "I need milk" and "I need to decide whether to buy this company" tie up the same kind of space. (RFA 9) That is why the smallest undone thing can wake you at three in the morning. (Team 2025)

Stress, he writes, comes from agreements with yourself that you have not kept. You can keep one, drop one, or renegotiate one, but not one you have forgotten. (RFA 2) In 2022 he traced this idea to personal-growth training he helped create early in his career. Breaking an agreement costs you self-trust. And a commitment opens a loop that holds energy until it closes. (Stoa 2022)

His test for an open loop is a single thought arriving twice. If "cat food" pops into your head a second time, you are not yet appropriately engaged with your cat. Your mind is for having ideas, not for holding them. It is brilliant at recognizing and terrible at recalling, which is why you can read today's calendar in a second but cannot recite the next two weeks. (Stoa 2021; GTD ch. 14, "Distributed Cognition")

Why do people live like this? Allen's more recent answer is not overwhelm. Real overwhelm, like a building on fire, gets handled. The problem is a low hum of ambient anxiety that people have grown comfortable with. "The last thing a fish notices is water." (Modern Wisdom 2020; Team 2025) The book's opening pages call the same thing an "ambient angst" that there is something you should be doing, a tension with no rest in it. (GTD, "Welcome") He calls it an addiction, meaning only that people are used to it and cannot picture another game. (Modern Wisdom 2020)

The release is specific. Capture the thought, decide what it means, and park the result where you trust you will see it again. Then the mind lets go. Nothing outside you has changed, but how you are engaged with your world has. Allen says this happens without exception: people feel better after making a complete list, even though nothing moved. (GTD ch. 1; RFA 29; Stoa 2021)

The first move, capture, has rules of its own. Allen asks you to gather a placeholder for every incomplete thing, physical or mental, into a small number of containers he calls "in": a paper tray, an email inbox, a notes app, a pocket of scraps. Get it all out of your head. Keep the number of containers small. Empty them regularly. Keep everything in your head or everything out of it, he says; in between, you trust neither. (GTD ch. 2, "Capture")

The five steps, in orderCapture: get it out of your head and into "in." Clarify: decide what it is and what the next action is. Organize: park the result in the right place. Reflect: look at your lists on a rhythm you trust. Engage: choose and do. Allen says an ordinary to-do list fails because it tries to do all five at once. (GTD ch. 2; RFA 2)

An everyday picture

A browser with forty tabs open. Each tab costs a little memory even when you are not looking at it, and the machine gets slow for no visible reason. The analogy breaks down because the mind does worse than slow down. It nags, and it nags loudest at night.

Allen's own example is a phone call. You decide to call your sister about Mom's birthday, but you park that decision back in your head, which has no sense of time. So it wakes you at 3 a.m. to remind you. (Modern Wisdom 2020)

Why does writing something down help when nothing has actually changed?

Because the loop closes at the level of attention before it closes in the world. Once the mind trusts that it will see the item again at the right time, it stops holding it. Allen calls this being "complete with your incompletions": a commitment that is captured, decided, and reviewed is, in a sense, already done. (RFA 23) The revised book points to research by Baumeister and Masicampo (2011) that found the same thing. Unfinished goals keep intruding on attention until a specific plan is made. It is the plan, not the finishing, that quiets them. (GTD ch. 14, "Relieving the Cognitive Load of Incompletions")

A common mix-up"So a to-do list is the fix." Allen says most to-do lists create as much stress as they relieve, because the items on them have not been thought through. "Mom" on a list is a reminder that you have decided nothing. The list is a monument to the anxiety, not a cure for it. (Modern Wisdom 2020; RFA 2)

3. Two questions: outcome and action

In this part: the small act of thinking that turns a worry into something your mind can release, and the places the result can go.

Words to know

A next action is the next physical, visible thing you would do. A project is any outcome that takes more than one action. The two-minute rule says do it now if it is that quick. Waiting for is the list of things other people owe you. Someday/maybe holds what you might do but have not committed to. A context is where you are and what you have with you, which decides what you can do.

Allen calls the heart of the method "the zeros and ones of productivity." For anything that has your attention, two questions: what outcome do you want, and what is the very next physical action that moves toward it? Most sophisticated people, he says, avoid both questions like the plague. (Modern Wisdom 2020)

Done comes first. Many worries are not tasks at all. They are decisions. "Should I quit the team?" is not finished by quitting. It is finished by deciding. So the outcome is "decide whether to stay on the team," and you can check that off either way. Allen says putting something to bed can itself be the outcome. (Stoa 2021; GTD ch. 15)

Then the next action. Not "fix things with Maya," which is an outcome. Not "figure out what I did wrong," which is thinking nobody can see. A next action is something you could watch yourself do: "talk to Maya at lunch." Allen's prompt is: if you had nothing else to do, what would you physically do first? (GTD ch. 1; Modern Wisdom 2020)

In the book, this is the clarifying step, a short set of questions you ask of each item in "in," one at a time. Is it actionable? If not, it is trash, or reference to keep, or something for someday/maybe. If it is actionable, will it take more than one step? Then it is a project, and it goes on a projects list with a next action. Can that action be done in two minutes? Do it now. Should someone else do it? Hand it off and track it on a waiting-for list. Otherwise defer it: to a calendar if it must happen at a specific time, or to a next-actions list if it should happen as soon as it can. (GTD ch. 2, "Clarifying"; ch. 6)

The calendar has strict rules. Allen puts only three kinds of thing on it: actions that must happen at a set time, actions that must happen on a set day, and information you need on a set day, like a test date. He calls this the hard landscape of the day. Everything else, including a daily to-do list, stays off it, because a calendar padded with wishes hides the one thing that truly must happen. (GTD ch. 2, "Calendar"; ch. 7)

The organizing step is simply putting each result where it belongs. The places are few: a projects list; next-actions lists sorted by context (calls, errands, at home, at school); the calendar; a waiting-for list; a someday/maybe list; and reference, which is anything you keep but never act on. Allen says being organized just means that where something is matches what it means to you. (GTD ch. 2, "Organize"; ch. 7)

Two things matter about this step. It asks you to think once, for about fifteen seconds, instead of re-thinking the item every time it floats past. (RFA 22) And it refuses vagueness. Allen's definition of a project is broad: any outcome that needs more than one step and that you want finished within a year. Most people have between thirty and a hundred. "Get a new lamp" and "decide whether to get divorced" both qualify, and either one can wake you at 3 a.m. (GTD ch. 2; RFA 34; Stoa 2021)

An everyday picture

A triage nurse. Each person who arrives gets one quick decision about where they go: home, a bed, surgery now. Nobody is left standing in the doorway undecided. The analogy breaks down because a nurse's categories are medical. Yours are about action: do, defer, delegate, or drop.

Allen's own example is a to-do list that says "Mom." Why is it there? It is her birthday. So what are you going to do about it? You captured it, he says, but you never clarified it. (Stoa 2021)

    Why two minutes, and not five or ten?

    Because somewhere around two minutes, the cost of writing an action down, filing it, and finding it again becomes higher than the cost of just doing it. Allen says the exact number is a judgment; the principle is that tracking has a price. He also notes that most people badly underestimate how long two minutes is. (GTD ch. 6, "The Two-Minute Rule")

    A common mix-up"A project is something big." Not for Allen. "Fix the printer" is a project. "Get new tires" is a project. The test is whether it takes more than one step, not whether it matters. The small ones are the ones most people never write down, which is why they keep nagging. (GTD ch. 15; RFA 7)

    4. The weekly review as ritual

    In this part: why a once-a-week look at everything is the hinge of the whole practice.

    Words to know

    The weekly review is an hour or two, once a week, spent getting the whole system current. A trusted system is any set of lists and places that your mind believes more than it believes itself. A mind sweep is writing down everything that has your attention until nothing is left in your head.

    Lists only relax you if you look at them. Allen says you can feel fine about the errands you are not running only if you review your errands list now and then. Without the review, a written-down thing is like a note in a bottle thrown into the sea. (Modern Wisdom 2020) The book puts the purpose of reviewing in one line: you need to be sure it is OK to be not doing what you are not doing, because that is what lets you be present. (GTD ch. 8, introduction)

    The weekly review is where that looking happens. Allen breaks it into three stages. Get clear: gather every loose paper and note into "in," get "in" to empty, and do a mind sweep for anything still in your head. Get current: walk your next-actions lists and mark off what is done. Review the calendar, the past weeks for loose ends and the coming weeks for what needs preparing. Check the waiting-for list. Then go through every project to be sure each has a next action. Get creative: read the someday/maybe list and add anything new, bold, or fun. (GTD ch. 8, "Get Clear," "Get Current," "Get Creative") You are done, he says, when you can honestly say you know everything you are not doing but could be doing if you decided to. (GTD ch. 8, "What Is the Weekly Review?")

    He calls it the most critical habit of all, one you keep for the rest of your life, and he explains why with a job metaphor. Emptying your head promotes your mind to higher work, "like a helium balloon." But the promotion is short-lived unless the lower levels stay handled. A system you stop reviewing is an assistant who quit. (RFA 29) In 2021 he called the weekly review the hardest habit to change, and said that writing things down plus a weekly look would change your life within a week. (Stoa 2021)

    There is a paradox here that Allen states plainly: you have to use your mind to empty your mind. To be present, you look at everything you have decided and say no to most of it. The external brain only works if you actually consult it. (Modern Wisdom 2020)

    Why weekly? He admits it is partly observation: a seven-day cycle fits how people regroup. He suggests two hours on the last afternoon of the week, so you go into the weekend clear, but says Sunday night or Monday morning work too. (Modern Wisdom 2020; GTD ch. 8, "The Right Time and Place for the Review") The week has a rhythm around it. Look at the calendar first, every day; Allen checks the next day's hard landscape the night before so he can relax and sleep. (GTD ch. 8; Modern Wisdom 2020) Look at actions several times a day and projects weekly. Look at the areas of your life every month or two, the year ahead once a year, your vision every few years. Purpose is "the Grand Think," and once may be enough. The weekly review, he says, lets you be "dumb and happy," just doing, for seven days. (RFA 12)

    He keeps a yearly version with his wife, Kathryn. They spend thirty minutes listing everything they did and everywhere they went that year, then thirty minutes on what would be cool to see on next year's list. He barely looks at the second list, he says. But until you acknowledge what you did, the year is not complete. (Stoa 2021; Stoa 2022)

    An everyday picture

    Many traditions set aside one day in seven to stop and look. Allen's version is an hour with your lists. The analogy breaks down because a sabbath is rest from work, and the review is work about work. Its reward is rest on the other six days.

    Allen's own examples are children. A teacher he worked with has nine- to eleven-year-olds doing weekly reviews in class, and Allen says they get it faster than nineteen-year-olds, who think they already have it. A nine-year-old emptying a backpack on Friday so Mom gets the note is doing the same thing as an executive emptying a briefcase after a board meeting. (Stoa 2022) For adults with a backlog, his advice is to wrap police tape around the garage, call the cleanup a project, and take one box at a time. Clutter attracts clutter; nobody drops a gum wrapper on a clean lawn. (Stoa 2022)

    Why does Allen say an unreviewed list is just "an externalized dump"?

    Because trust is the whole point. The mind lets go only when it believes it will see the item again at the right time. A list it does not expect to see is no better than memory, and looking at a stale list only reminds you how far behind you are. (Modern Wisdom 2020; RFA 29)

    A common mix-up"I did a big cleanup once, so I'm set." Allen says the clear feeling fades fast if the system does not stay current. Organization is not a state; things change their nature just by sitting there, and clutter generates itself. The review is what keeps the first cleanup alive. (RFA 29; Stoa 2022)

    5. Six horizons: from the ground to why you are here

    In this part: how one small action connects to purpose, and why Allen starts from the bottom.

    Words to know

    The horizons of focus are six altitudes at which you hold commitments, from today's actions up to your purpose. Areas of focus are the roles and standards you maintain rather than finish. The natural planning model is Allen's five-step picture of how a mind plans one thing.

    Allen pictures your commitments at six altitudes. On the ground are next actions. Horizon 1 is current projects. Horizon 2 is areas of focus and accountability: the roles you maintain, like health, family, school, and friendships. Horizon 3 is goals one to two years out. Horizon 4 is vision, three to five years out. Horizon 5 is purpose and principles: why you exist, and what matters to you no matter what. (GTD ch. 2, "The Six-Level Model for Reviewing Your Own Work")

    You cannot get more strategic than asking why you are on the planet, he said in 2022. But knowing your life purpose will not tell you which email to write first. Each level down is not less important. It is more operational. (Stoa 2022)

    Here Allen differs from most advice. Most goal-setting works top down: find your purpose, then derive your tasks. Allen says you can start at any level, but he recommends starting at the bottom, and he defends the choice. You cannot focus on the big picture while most of your energy is wrapped around open loops. (RFA 6; GTD ch. 9) If your boat is sinking, he says, you do not much care which way it is pointed. Handle what has your attention, and you discover what really has your attention. (GTD ch. 9, "Working from the Bottom Up") Clear the deck first, and the higher thinking rises on its own. His professional mission, he said in 2021, is a world where people perceive problems as projects: "There are no problems, only projects." (Stoa 2021)

    At the top sits his own answer, offered almost in passing in 2022. He thinks we are on the planet to complete what we have engaged with, and to be accountable for where we put our creative energy, because you cannot stop creating. Completion and creation. But, he added, he never has to say any of that. He just asks what that piece of paper on your desk is and what you are going to do about it. (Stoa 2022)

    The lists manage breadth, everything you are involved in at once. For depth on one thing, Allen describes a natural planning model in five phases: purpose and principles, a vision of the outcome, brainstorming, organizing, and next actions. He says your brain already does this whenever you decide where to go for dinner. (GTD ch. 1, "Horizontal and Vertical Action Management"; ch. 3, "The Natural Planning Model") Planning is enough, he adds, when the project is off your mind. (RFA, Part V summary, "The Natural Planning Model")

    He also warns that avoiding any one horizon shrinks you. If you never look up, or never look down, you live in contraction instead of expansion, "professionally, psychologically, or spiritually." (RFA 43)

    An everyday picture

    Zoom levels on a map. At street level you see your block; zoomed out you see the country. The analogy breaks down because on a map every level shows the same place. In Allen's horizons, every level asks a different kind of question. The bottom asks "what do I do now?" The top asks "why am I here?"

    Allen's own example is a question from a strategist who thought the method was too low-level for twenty-year plans. His reply: purpose, vision, goals, areas, projects, actions. Tell me which of those is not strategic. A strategic plan is fine. Just make sure it has next actions on its moving parts. (Stoa 2022)

    Why does Allen refuse to start with purpose?

    Because, he says, priorities only work at the conscious level, and nothing is conscious while your attention is tied up in open loops. People who skip the mundane and go straight to the sublime usually have no real inventory to prioritize. The mundane, he writes, is not a stand-in for the sublime but a hidden passage to it. (RFA 7, 8)

    A common mix-up"The horizons are a ladder you climb once." No. All six are live at the same time, and the weekly review mostly works at Horizon 1. The point is coherence between the levels, not ascent. Allen says that avoiding any one of them short-changes all of them. (RFA 43)

    6. The still, small voice

    In this part: how Allen chooses what to do, and why the last step is trust rather than a formula.

    Words to know

    The four-criteria model is Allen's order of filters for choosing an action. The threefold nature of work names the three kinds of thing you can be doing at any moment. "The still, small voice" is a phrase from the Hebrew Bible for a quiet inner prompt; Allen borrows it for intuition.

    After capturing, clarifying, organizing, and reflecting comes the fifth step, engaging: choosing what to do right now. Allen offers three models. The first is four criteria, in this order: context (what can you do where you are, with what you have?), time available, energy available, and only then priority. (GTD ch. 9, "The Four-Criteria Model for Choosing Actions in the Moment")

    The second model names three kinds of work. Doing work you already defined, from your lists. Doing work as it shows up, because something new just arrived. And defining your work: processing inputs and deciding what they mean. Most people, he says, over-indulge the second and neglect the third. There are no interruptions, he writes, only mismanaged inputs. (GTD ch. 9, "The Threefold Model for Evaluating Daily Work")

    The third model is the six horizons, used as a ladder for review when you need perspective. (GTD ch. 9, "The Six-Level Model for Reviewing Your Own Work")

    And then comes the honesty. No formula tells you what to do at 9:45 in the morning. Anyone who offers one is selling something, and anyone who offers a simple route to intuition is selling something too. (RFA 43) The answer is intuition. The system's job is to make intuitive leaps safe: to let you jump from a platform of trust instead of a slippery footing of hope. His formula for the jump is to prepare for the worst, imagine the best, and shoot down the middle. (RFA 42) The book says the same thing in fewer words: every decision to act is an intuitive one, and the challenge is to move from hoping you chose well to trusting that you did. (GTD ch. 2, "Engage"; ch. 9) In conversation he is looser about what to call it: trust your gut, or your spirit, or the still small voice inside you, or your liver. (Modern Wisdom 2020)

    Allen says this is why he built the method at all. His personal style is to follow his intuitive, spontaneous choices from moment to moment. The lists exist to remove what stops him from hearing the voice that says stop, go spend time with your dog. The whole purpose, he says, is to open you up to your intuitive wisdom. Call it the higher self, your spirit, or your soul; the name does not matter to him. (Stoa 2021) Some of his best projects were never on his lists, like a morning spent pruning his pine tree. He could do that without anxiety only because his lists were complete and recently reviewed. (RFA 13)

    He gives no rule about hard things first or easy things first. Sometimes he does what is most on his mind; sometimes what he feels like. A Hollywood producer who championed his work did only what was most fun and was wildly successful. "Eat the frog" works too. (Stoa 2022; Modern Wisdom 2020) The one thing Allen insists on is that you stay in the saddle of your life rather than become its victim. (Modern Wisdom 2020)

    An everyday picture

    A jazz musician who practiced scales for years and now improvises without thinking about them. The scales did not replace her ear; they freed it. The analogy breaks down because scales stay the same. Your lists change every week, which is why the review is part of the practice.

    Allen's own example is a grocery list. Because he has the list, he feels free to buy things that are not on it. Confidence, he says, is what you call that. (Stoa 2021)

      Why is context first and priority last?

      Because the first three filters remove most of your options before importance ever comes up. You cannot make a phone call in a silent library, or write an essay in five minutes, or draft a hard email when you are wiped out. What is left is a short list. Priority is where the horizons from part 5 come back: which of what is left serves the projects, roles, and goals you have committed to? That is a judgment, not a calculation. Allen also says that nothing on the list may fit, and that doing something small, or taking a nap, is a legitimate choice. (GTD ch. 9; Modern Wisdom 2020)

      A common mix-up"GTD tells you your priorities." It does not, and Allen is pointed about this. Systems are "crude placeholders." No tool outputs "call Fred." He also dislikes A-B-C priority codes, which he says become a license to ignore open loops: tires are either needed or not, and no code changes that. (RFA 41, 7)

      7. The spiritual roots

      In this part: what Allen says his method is and is not, where it came from, how this page reads it, and where it is questioned.

      Words to know

      A householder is someone who seeks a clear mind while living an ordinary life with family, work, and email, rather than in a monastery. Small-s spiritual is Allen's own phrase for anything that concerns the world you cannot see. Esoteric teachings are about an unseen inner world and are passed from teacher to student. Dzogchen is a Tibetan Buddhist tradition of resting in the mind's natural state; the comparison to it is this page's, not Allen's.

      So what does Allen say the method is? "Spiritual, with a small s." Spiritual means the world you cannot see, which is far bigger than the one you can. (Stoa 2022) But he insists he does not teach spirituality. He teaches how to clear and manage focus, so that your energy is available; what you do with the energy is up to you. (RFA 23; Stoa 2022) There is something mystical about people, he says, not about the process. (Stoa 2022) Being "at one with the universe" has little to do with lists. If you are good at it, you can be at one with the flu. (RFA 23)

      Where did it come from? Allen was a philosophy major who got bored with philosophers for proving their first assumptions with their first assumptions. In graduate school at Berkeley in 1968 he met a man who taught him karate and, in Allen's telling, opened him to powerful spiritual experiences he had no map for. He railed against a world that did not understand them, spent a painful stretch in a psychiatric institution, and decided he would rather cooperate with the world than fight it. A bookstore led him to Rudolf Steiner, an Austrian spiritual teacher, and then to John-Roger, who founded a church called the Movement of Spiritual Inner Awareness. Allen became a minister there in 1972, stayed with the teacher for fifty years, and still calls himself a beginning student. His own practice, he says, is active: chanting and mantras meant to help you relax and let go. (Stoa 2022) The book's acknowledgments thank "my spiritual coach, J-R." (GTD, acknowledgments)

      He is careful about the limits of experience without grounding. Psychedelics, he said, can open you up, but they leave a glass wall between you and reality, and he gave them up in 1971. (Stoa 2022) A note at the back of the book adds a warning from his own life. He once tried to trust the universe completely and check out of his practical connections, and it was, he says, a close call. The higher ground, he concluded, is surrendering to inner awareness while cooperating with the world you have chosen to play in. (GTD ch. 9, endnote)

      The productivity method grew out of personal-growth training that Allen helped create with friends from that community. Two of its ideas became the roots of his work: breaking an agreement costs self-trust, and a commitment opens a loop that holds energy until it closes. He says John-Roger's reasoning was practical. People who cannot handle their emotional and financial lives cannot free themselves for inner work. (Stoa 2022)

      And yet the most personal essay in Ready for Anything says the methods only work "to a point." Whether they keep working depends on why you clear your decks. Done from the head, as a way to disengage from life, the low buzz remains. Done "more from my heart," the same activity is "draped in elegance and ease." Then the paradox: the less attached he is to the material world, the more productively engaged with it he becomes. (RFA 24, "Stress Transcendence") Surrendering to and cooperating with something larger, he writes elsewhere, is the real game, and that kind of letting go is a directed energy, not caving in. (RFA 51)

      Allen rejects the choice between being and doing as a false dichotomy. Being is highly active, only inward. Meditation, as he defines it, lessens outer distraction so that subtler inner levels can be noticed. The better question is what kind of doing aligns you with your being. (RFA 26, "To Do or to Be?") In 2022 he said he never understood why anyone thought the practical had no spiritual basis. He called it "the magic of the mundane." The phrase is also a section title in the book. There he says that sitting with a client for hours while they empty their drawers is some of the best work he does. (Stoa 2022; GTD ch. 13, "The Magic of Mastering the Mundane")

      This page's reading

      This page claims that the method is a householder's practice of presence. A questioner put almost exactly that to Allen in 2022: a quasi-spiritual method disguised as productivity. Allen's answer was the small-s definition above, not a yes. The same questioner added that you can seek a clear mind in a cave, or as a householder with an inbox. GTD, he suggested, is one of the basic moves that lets you stay on the path while living an ordinary life. Allen agreed, joking that the cave was always an option but he liked good chardonnay. (Stoa 2022)

      The resemblance to contemplative traditions is ours to draw, not his. But it is hard to miss. A mind that returns to its natural state on its own. Thoughts allowed to arise once and dissolve. "No ripples left in the water." (RFA 25) Trust, not control, as the condition of letting go. (RFA 29) A form, like a karate kata (a fixed sequence of moves), practiced until it disappears into you. (RFA 37) These are the shapes of Zen and Dzogchen teaching, where the mind is compared to still water and thoughts to waves that settle by themselves. Allen heads a section of his first chapter with a line from the Zen teacher Shunryu Suzuki, that an empty mind is ready for anything. He uses Suzuki again on emptiness in the second book, and quotes other Zen, Buddhist, and Taoist teachers elsewhere. (GTD ch. 1, ch. 9; RFA 1, 13, 44) His own lineage, though, is Western and esoteric, not Buddhist. Treat the comparison as a lens, not a family tree.

      Where the method is questioned

      A critic, and Allen's replyIn the 2021 session a questioner named Joe said he had once loved these books for two reasons he now thought were wrong. One was that a smooth, productive life would make his life meaningful. The other was that integrity meant honoring and renegotiating agreements. Meaning, he said, lives in the space of not getting things done. Holding an outcome for a meeting makes him treat the people in it as means, and being a little flaky is part of who he wants to be. Allen replied that this was Joe's frame, not his. Satisfaction comes from being appropriately engaged with a relationship, not from finishing it. If you go to a party to have fun and do not have fun, that is inappropriate engagement with your party. Then he added that fulfilling your life purpose is itself an incompletion, so it is a project. The host stopped the exchange as "a battle of epistemologies." You can decide whether Allen answered the objection or absorbed it. (Stoa 2021)

      Why would being less attached make someone more engaged?

      Allen's answer is about where the energy goes. When he is focused on the material world and its slings and arrows, he spends energy trying to escape it. When he is focused "more spiritually," he no longer needs to escape, so he can attend to the smallest details without resentment. He calls the difference being the purpose instead of merely having one. (RFA 24)

      A common mix-up"Allen claims GTD is a spiritual path." He does not. He claims it clears space and that what fills the space is your business. He also says the methods work only to a point without an inner why. The "practice in disguise" reading is this page's, and Allen's own words are the evidence for and against it. (RFA 23, 24; Stoa 2022)

      Check yourself

      8. Falling off and coming back

      In this part: what mastery means to Allen, where people fall off, and how to return.

      Words to know

      The three tiers of mastery are Allen's stages of skill with the method. Latest and loudest is what runs your day when you have no system to consult.

      Allen is clear that mastery is not a Zen-like peace on a mountaintop, though he allows that could be a nice optional expression of it. Mastery is being able to regain clarity, stability, and focus when you need to, whatever is going on. The water is not always undisturbed. It engages the disturbance instead of fighting it. (GTD ch. 15, "The Path of GTD Mastery")

      He names three tiers and compares them to learning to drive. First, the basics: capturing everything, deciding next actions, keeping the lists, doing the review. Second, what he calls integrated life management, where your projects list becomes the heartbeat of the system and your areas of focus keep it complete. Third, focus, direction, and creativity: using the clear space for the most meaningful things, which only get your attention once the mundane is put to bed. (GTD ch. 15, "The Three Tiers of Mastery") Even the basics take a while, like learning a karate punch. And the sign of a master is that pressure makes them use the practices more, not less. When a crisis hits, they do a fresh mind sweep instead of taking everything back into their heads. (GTD ch. 15)

      Where do people fall off? In a 2020 interview Allen walked through four places, and each is a skipped step. They capture some things but not all, so they trust neither their head nor their list. They capture but never decide, so the to-do list becomes a sculpture of their anxiety. They decide but do not park the result, so it goes back into the head. Or they park everything and never look at it, so they are run by latest and loudest. (Modern Wisdom 2020; RFA 5) There is a fifth, subtler fall: your lists drift back into vague stuff, "Mom" and "tires" instead of next actions, and stop being trusted. (GTD ch. 12, ch. 15)

      And people fall off because clarity itself is uncomfortable. They are used to guilt and the 3 a.m. voice; getting clear takes them out of their comfort zone. Allen's answer is to keep raising your standard until unclear feels worse than the work of getting clear, the way unbrushed teeth feel worse than brushing. (Stoa 2022) When the system falls apart, come back to it. The book says falling off happens fast, because once a list is incomplete you stop trusting it and take everything back into your head; getting back on takes a pen, some paper, and another mind sweep. (GTD ch. 15, "It's Easy to Get Off Track... and Easy to Get Back On") Falling off is normal. Allen admits in the revised introduction that the full method can feel like too much to absorb, and that he is no expert in how people change habits. Measure progress by the percentage of better choices over time, not by perfection. (GTD, introduction to the revised edition; RFA 52)

      Then the trap of success: the better you get, the better you'd better get. Clear people take on too much because they can. At some point the incompletion is not a project but the quality of your experience: "I need to be more relaxed than I am right now." Allen's reply is the same as always. Great, you have a project. What's the next action? (Stoa 2021; RFA 42) The same question scales up. His first question to any team is what has the team's attention, because whatever has it is not yet appropriately engaged with. (Team 2025)

      He ends on satisfaction rather than happiness. As happy as you get, he says, that is how depressed you will get; he prefers enough. The whole method is about getting back to a peaceful, easy feeling, and then doing with it whatever you want: be more strategic, more creative, more loving, cook better spaghetti. (Stoa 2021)

      An everyday picture

      Learning to ride a bike. Nobody masters it by never falling; they master it by getting back on faster each time until falling is rare. The analogy breaks down because a bike stays learned. Allen's system drifts out of date every week, so coming back is not a one-time recovery but the practice itself.

      Allen's own example is a listener who set the whole system up, felt wonderful, and then watched it collapse. His reply: just keep coming back. The difference between you and me, he said, is that you were willing to tolerate the mess and I am not. (Stoa 2021)

      Try it: the four places people fall off

      Drag Allen's four failure points into the order of the steps they skip: capture, clarify, organize, reflect.

      Why does Allen talk about standards instead of discipline?

      Because he does not believe people lack discipline. They lack a disciplined approach: structures and habits that do the work willpower would otherwise have to do. If your standard for clarity is high enough, the review happens the way brushing your teeth happens, without a debate. (RFA, introduction; Stoa 2022)

      A common mix-up"If I fell off, the method failed." Allen says the opposite. People feel guilty when they fall off because the method looks so simple, but every return is a better choice, and better choices compound. The point is not a perfect record. It is how fast you get back to ready. (RFA 52, 20)
