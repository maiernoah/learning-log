The Dark Night of John of the Cross

Sort the four nights on John's map, test his three signs on sample cases, and see why he calls God's light a darkness.

Start here

This page teaches The Ascent of Mount Carmel and The Dark Night by John of the Cross (written about 1578 to 1585), in the English translation by Kieran Kavanaugh and Otilio Rodriguez (1991). It is a plain-language study guide. The original is linked in About the source.

People say "dark night of the soul" about any rough stretch of life. The phrase comes from a Spanish friar who escaped from a prison cell in 1578 and then wrote a love poem about slipping out at night. He spent years explaining what the poem meant. The result is a map of how a person grows from loving God for the good feelings to loving God for God. It is strange and demanding, and readers have used it for over four hundred years.

1. Why a night?

In this part: see where the dark night comes from, why John calls the road to God a night, and how he splits it into four.

Words to know

  • Union with God: John's goal. A person's will comes to want exactly what God wants, out of love.
  • Purgation: a cleaning out. John uses it as another name for each night.
  • Active and passive: what you do yourself, and what God does in you while you receive it.

A poem after a prison

In December 1577, John of the Cross, a Carmelite friar, was seized by opponents of the reform he was helping Teresa of Ávila lead. They jailed him in a monastery in Toledo, and after two months moved him to a room about six feet by ten. After nine months, on a summer night, he pushed open the lock and climbed down from a window on strips of old bedcovers. (Kavanaugh's introduction)

Soon after, he wrote an eight-stanza poem, The Dark Night. On its surface it is a love poem. A woman slips out of her sleeping house at night to meet her lover. John read it as the story of a soul going out to God. Here is the first stanza in Spanish, with this guide's own English.

En una noche oscura,
con ansias, en amores inflamada,
¡oh dichosa ventura!,
salí sin ser notada,
estando ya mi casa sosegada.
On a dark night,
burning with love's longings,
oh, lucky chance!
I slipped out unnoticed,
my house at last asleep.

He then wrote two prose books to explain the poem. The Ascent of Mount Carmel mostly covers what a person can do. The Dark Night covers what God does. He never finished either one. (Kavanaugh's introductions)

Three reasons for the word "night"

John gives three reasons to call the road to God a night. (Ascent 1.2.1)

  • The starting point. You give up your cravings for worldly things, and that feels like the senses going dark.
  • The road. The road is faith, and faith is dark to the mind.
  • The goal. God himself is beyond the soul's sight while it lives here.

He adds that these are really one night in three parts, like a real night. Giving up cravings is twilight, when things fade from view. Faith is midnight, the darkest part. God is the hour just before dawn. (Ascent 1.2.5)

Two nights, two ways

John also splits the night another way. The night of sense works on the body's senses and the pleasures they bring. The night of spirit works on the mind, memory, and will. (Ascent 1.1.2; 2.6.1)

Each night happens in two ways. In the active way, you do what you can. In the passive way, God does the work and you receive it. (Ascent 1.1.2; 1.13.1) That makes four nights. The Ascent mostly covers the two active ones, and the Dark Night covers the two passive ones. (Kavanaugh's introduction to the Night)

The night of sense comes to beginners, and John says it is common. The night of spirit comes later, to proficients, and he says only a few go through it. (Night 1.8.1)

This course follows a traveler through all four, in the rough order a person meets them: active sense, passive sense, then the active and passive nights of spirit. The nights overlap in practice, since God's work and the person's run side by side. (Kavanaugh's introduction to the Night) The order mixes the two books, so the references jump between them.

Example

An everyday analogy. Think of learning to swim. First you practice strokes on your own. That is the active part. Then your teacher takes you into deep water, and you have to let the water hold you. That is the passive part. The analogy breaks down because a teacher works from outside you, and John's God works from inside.

John's own example. Some people, he says, act like small children who kick and cry to walk by themselves when their mother wants to carry them. They make no headway, or if they do, it is at a child's pace. (Ascent prologue 3)

Why does John need passive nights at all? Isn't trying hard enough?

John says that however hard beginners work at giving things up, they can't finish the job. God has to complete it in them, passively, through the night. (Night 1.7.5) Effort still matters. People who resist God's leading, he says, go slower and suffer more. (Ascent prologue 3)

A common mix-upPeople often use "dark night" to mean any sad stretch of life. In the Dark Night, John defines the night as God's own light flowing into a person, which he calls contemplation. (Night 2.5.1) It feels dark because the person isn't ready for so much light. He says it darkens only in order to give light. (Night 2.9.1)

2. The night of sense: your part

In this part: learn what John asks you to give up in the active night of sense, and why he aims at the craving and leaves the thing alone.

Words to know

  • Appetite: John's word for a craving that isn't ordered toward God.
  • Attachment: a desire the will clings to, often out of habit.
  • Mortification: putting a craving to death by refusing to feed it.

The craving goes dark

The first night works on the senses. John calls it a deprivation, but of one thing only: the pleasure your appetites chase. (Ascent 1.3.1) The world you see and hear stays where it is. (Ascent 1.3.4)

He is clear about this. King David was rich, John says, yet called himself poor, because his will wasn't fixed on riches. Things can't get inside the soul and harm it. The will set on them does the damage. (Ascent 1.3.4)

Why does it matter? Love makes you like what you love, John says. If you cling to a creature, you shrink to its size. (Ascent 1.4.3) Cravings also do two kinds of harm. They crowd out God's Spirit, and they wear a person out, torment, darken, dirty, and weaken them. (Ascent 1.6.1) He compares cravings to restless children always whining for something new. (Ascent 1.6.6)

Not every desire counts. Natural desires, like hunger, are little or no obstacle if you don't consent to them beyond the first stirring. (Ascent 1.11.2) Small slips you don't notice don't block the way either. (Ascent 1.11.3) What blocks it is a voluntary habit: a small attachment you know about and never mean to give up. His examples include talking too much, and clinging to a certain book or to the way your food is cooked. (Ascent 1.11.4)

His advice is short. First, want to live as Christ lived, and study his life to learn how. Second, for love of Christ, let go of any sense pleasure that isn't for God's honor. If you can't avoid a pleasure, it is enough not to want it. (Ascent 1.13.3–4) Then come his famous counsels: lean toward the harder thing, the less pleasant thing, toward wanting nothing. He asks for good order and good sense in practicing them, and promises that people who do will find delight in them. (Ascent 1.13.6–7)

His verses at the foot of the mountain sum it up. In Spanish, "Para venir a gustarlo todo, / no quieras tener gusto en nada." In this guide's English, "To come to enjoy it all, / seek enjoyment in nothing." (Ascent 1.13.11)

One thing makes this possible. John says nobody beats the senses' cravings by denial alone. A stronger love, for Christ, has to burn hotter than the love of pleasure. That is the poem's "burning with love's longings." (Ascent 1.14.2)

Example

An everyday analogy. Someone breaking a phone habit doesn't have to throw the phone in a lake. The trouble is the pull, the reach for it every few minutes. The analogy breaks down because John's goal is larger than self-control. He wants room in the soul for God.

John's own example. A bird tied by a thin thread is held as surely as one tied by a cord. The thread is easier to break, but until it breaks, the bird can't fly. It takes only one flap of the wings. (Ascent 1.11.4)

Try it: thread or not?

Sort each case: is it the kind of tie John says keeps the bird on the ground?

Why must even a small attachment go? Couldn't you keep just one?

In union, John says, your will and God's will become one. Holding on, knowingly, to something God doesn't want keeps the two apart. (Ascent 1.11.2–3) He gives a picture: a log can't turn into fire if even one degree of heat is missing. (Ascent 1.11.6)

A common mix-upSome readers think John hates the world. He does say that creatures are nothing compared with God. (Ascent 1.4.3) The harm he describes, though, comes from the will's grip on them. A person can own things and stay free, he says, as long as the will isn't set on them. (Ascent 1.3.4)

3. The night of sense: God's part

In this part: see why John says God takes the sweetness out of a beginner's prayer, and what the dryness does.

Words to know

  • Dryness: the loss of pleasure and feeling in prayer and holy things. The translation also calls it aridity.
  • Capital vices: the seven classic roots of sin: pride, greed, lust, anger, gluttony, envy, and sloth.
  • Spiritual gluttony: craving good feelings in prayer the way a glutton craves food.

The sweet start

At first, John says, God treats someone newly and seriously devoted to him the way a mother treats a baby. She warms it, feeds it sweet milk, and carries it in her arms. Prayer feels wonderful. Long hours of prayer are a pleasure, and fasting feels like joy. (Night 1.1.2–3)

The trouble is the motive. Beginners pray largely for the good feeling, so their faults show up even in holy things. John sorts these faults by the seven capital vices. (Night 1.1.3)

The weaning

Then the mother weans the child. She rubs something bitter on the breast and sets the child down to walk. God does the same. Just when prayer feels brightest, he darkens it. Meditation stops working, and holy things taste dry and even bitter. (Night 1.1.2; 1.8.3) John says most beginners enter this night, and not long after they start. (Night 1.8.4)

The beginners' own efforts can't finish the cleaning. However hard they practice giving things up, John says, they won't succeed fully until God does it in them through this night. (Night 1.7.5)

What is God doing? John says God is moving his gifts from the senses to the spirit. The senses can't take in this new food, so they are left empty and dry. Meanwhile the spirit is quietly fed and grows stronger. (Night 1.9.4)

The night bears fruit. The first and chief fruit is self-knowledge. Without the sweet feelings, a person sees how weak they really are. (Night 1.12.2) From that comes humility, and then love of neighbor, because people stop comparing. (Night 1.12.7–8)

Example

An everyday analogy. Training wheels make a bike easy and fun. Take them off and riding suddenly feels hard and wobbly, just as you are about to ride for real. The analogy breaks down because a new rider can feel the progress, and John says that in this night you usually can't.

John's own example. In the desert, the Israelites had manna from heaven, which held every flavor. Still they cried for the meat and onions of Egypt, because their taste was used to them. Beginners miss the old sweetness the same way. (Night 1.9.5)

Why would a loving God take away the good feelings?

Because the feelings had become the motive. Beginners loved prayer partly for its sweetness, which is partly love of self. Taking the sweetness away lets them love God for God, and see themselves as they are. (Night 1.8.3; 1.12.2)

A common mix-upMany people in this night assume dry prayer means God has left or they have done something wrong. John says that fear is what hurts them most. (Night 1.10.1) He tells them to take comfort and keep going, because God is leading them by a new road. (Night 1.10.2–3) Dryness can have other causes too. That is why John gives signs to tell them apart, the subject of the next part.

4. The three signs

In this part: learn how John tells when it's time to stop meditating, and what each of his signs rules out.

Words to know

  • Meditation: prayer that works step by step with images and reasons, like picturing a Gospel scene and thinking about it.
  • Contemplation: God's quiet, loving gift of himself, received without images or step-by-step thought.
  • Loving attentiveness: staying quietly with God in love, without working at thoughts.

Stairs to a room

For John, meditation is good and right for beginners. You imagine a scene, such as Christ on the cross, and reason about it. It feeds love through the senses. (Ascent 2.12.3, 5)

Still, it is a means to an end. John compares it to a staircase. Each step helps you climb, but none of them is the room at the top. If you never leave the steps behind, you never arrive. (Ascent 2.12.5) Many acts of meditation build a habit of loving knowledge of God. Once you have it, prayer is like drinking water already stored up, with no need to haul it in. (Ascent 2.14.2)

John's three signs

So when should you stop? Too soon and you slide back. Too late and you get in the way. John gives three signs. (Ascent 2.13.1)

  1. You can't meditate or get anything out of it, the way you used to. (Ascent 2.13.2)
  2. You have no wish to fix your imagination on other things either. Your mind still wanders, but you don't want it to. (Ascent 2.13.3)
  3. You like to stay alone with God in a quiet, loving awareness, without particular thoughts. John calls this the surest sign. (Ascent 2.13.4)

All three must be there together, because each one guards against a fake. (Ascent 2.13.5) The first sign alone could come from carelessness. In that case you would want to think about other things, so the second sign would be missing. The first two together could come from melancholia, a bodily condition that dulls the mind. Then the peaceful, loving awareness of the third sign would be missing. (Ascent 2.13.6)

In the Dark Night, John gives a second list for the passive night of sense. First, you find no comfort in God, and none in anything else. Second, you keep turning to God with a painful worry that you aren't serving him. Third, you can't meditate however hard you try, and this lasts. (Night 1.9.2–3, 8) The worry rules out lukewarmness, since lukewarm people don't care. It also rules out a purely bodily cause, which brings no such desire to serve God. The lasting helps too, since bodily moods come and go. (Night 1.9.2–3, 9) John adds that melancholia often goes along with this dryness without spoiling its good work. (Night 1.9.3)

What should a person in this state do? Stop forcing meditation. Rest in a loving, peaceful attentiveness to God, even if it feels like doing nothing. (Night 1.10.4) John compares it to sitting for a portrait. If the model keeps moving to help, the painter can't finish. (Night 1.10.5) At first the new state comes and goes, he adds. On some days a person still needs to meditate gently to get there. (Ascent 2.15.1–2)

Example

An everyday analogy. When you learned to read, you sounded out every letter. Now that you can read, sounding out each letter would only slow you down. The skill you built is still there, working faster. The analogy breaks down because reading stays your own activity, while John says contemplation is received from God.

John's own example. Going back to meditation after the new state has begun is like peeling a fruit that is already peeled. You look for the rind, find none, and miss the fruit in your hand. (Ascent 2.14.3–4)

Try it: the director's desk

You are a spiritual director, someone people talk to about their prayer. For each of the four people, tick the signs you see, pick what John would advise, and press Check.

A note from outside the bookJohn's "melancholia" comes from sixteenth-century medicine, which blamed a whole range of emotional troubles on an excess of black bile. (Kavanaugh's note to Night 1.4.3) John says that when melancholia is the whole cause, the dryness isn't the dark night. (Night 1.9.3) Today, feeling low, numb, or hopeless for weeks can be depression, a real illness that doctors treat. If that sounds like you, tell a trusted adult or a doctor.

Why does John insist on all three signs at once?

Each sign rules out a different false alarm. Not being able to meditate could just be carelessness; the second sign rules that out. Not meditating and not wanting other things could be melancholia, which can feel like a pleasant daze; the third sign, a peaceful and loving awareness of God, rules that out. (Ascent 2.13.6)

A common mix-upSome people think contemplation is a technique for emptying the mind. John describes it as a gift: a hidden, peaceful, loving flow of God into the soul. The person only has to stop getting in its way. (Night 1.10.4–6) He warns that a pleasant, drowsy daze, with no loving awareness of God in it, can be mistaken for it. (Ascent 2.13.6)

5. Faith, the midnight

In this part: see why John calls faith dark even though he says it is certain, and what it asks of the mind.

Words to know

  • Faith: for John, firmly believing what God has revealed, which the mind can't see.
  • Faculties: the soul's three powers: intellect (understanding), memory, and will.
  • Theological virtues: faith, hope, and charity (love), which John says unite a person with God.

Too much light

The night of spirit has an active side too, and its tool is faith. John calls faith the "secret ladder" of the poem's second stanza, because its rungs are hidden from the senses and the mind. (Ascent 2.1.1) If the night of sense is twilight, faith is midnight. It is darker because it blinds reason itself. (Ascent 2.2.1–2)

Faith is certain and yet dark, John says. It is dark because its light is too strong. When the sun shines, every other light seems to vanish, and staring at the sun blinds you. Faith overwhelms the mind's own light in the same way. (Ascent 2.3.1)

Try telling someone born blind what white or yellow looks like. However much you explain, they grasp only the words. Faith tells us of things we have never seen, and nothing we know is like them. (Ascent 2.3.2–3) Yet this darkness guides. John points to the cloud in Exodus that was dark and still lit up the night for Israel. (Ascent 2.3.4–5)

So in this night you stop leaning on what you understand, feel, or imagine about God, and lean on faith. John's picture: a traveler who can still see a little won't let the guide lead, and so leads the guide astray. (Ascent 2.4.3) For the same reason he says to set aside even visions and other unusual experiences, and go by faith. (Ascent 2.11.2, 8) Nothing the mind can picture is like God, and faith alone is the fitting means. (Ascent 2.8.4; 2.9.1)

Faith has two partners. Each theological virtue empties one faculty. Faith darkens the intellect. Hope empties the memory, since you only hope for what you don't yet have. Charity strips the will of every love that isn't God. (Ascent 2.6.1–4)

Where does this lead? John's picture is a window in the sun. A smudged window shows only a little light. Clean it, and it shines so brightly it seems to be the sunbeam itself, though it is still glass. How much it shines depends on the window, since the sun is always shining. In the same way, a soul made pure by love shares in God's life while staying a creature. (Ascent 2.5.6–7)

Example

An everyday analogy. Point a phone camera at the sun and the photo comes out white. With too much light, you see nothing. Faith is dark for John in that way, from too much light. The analogy breaks down because a camera gets nothing from the glare, while John says faith gives the soul light in its darkness. (Ascent 2.3.4)

John's own example. The sunbeam in the try-it below is his, used in both books. (Ascent 2.14.9; Night 2.8.3)

John uses exactly this picture. The purer and simpler God's light is, the darker it seems, because there is nothing in it for the mind to catch on. (Ascent 2.14.8–10) When something comes up to be judged, such as a fault, the person suddenly sees it clearly, like the hand in the beam. (Night 2.8.4)

How can faith be certain if it is dark?

John says faith gives the mind certainty without clarity. (Ascent 2.6.2) Certainty is a firm yes. Clarity would mean seeing for yourself. If you could see it, he says, it wouldn't be faith.

A common mix-upSome read "dark faith" as "unsure faith." John means firm faith in something too bright for the mind, the way the sun is too bright for the eyes. (Ascent 2.3.1; 2.6.2)

6. The night of spirit: the log in the fire

In this part: follow John's account of the deepest night, why it hurts, and what he says it is for.

Words to know

  • Ray of darkness: an old phrase John borrows from the writer Dionysius for contemplation, a light so bright it blinds.
  • Proficient: someone past the beginner stage, already praying in quiet contemplation.

Pulling up roots

After the night of sense, John says, a person may spend years as a proficient. Prayer is freer and more peaceful. Yet the deepest roots of selfishness are still there. The first night cut off branches; this one pulls up roots. (Night 2.1.1; 2.2.1)

Then comes the night of spirit. John defines it as an inflow of God that cleans out a person's ignorance and faults, and he identifies it with contemplation. (Night 2.5.1) Why would God's light feel dark? He gives two reasons. It is too high for the soul, the way the sun blinds an owl. And the soul is impure, so the light hurts, like bright light on sore eyes. (Night 2.5.2–3, 5)

The pain is real. The mind goes dark, the will goes dry, and the memory feels empty. (Night 2.3.3) Worst of all, the person feels that God has rejected them. They know they love God and would give their life for him, yet they can't believe God loves them. (Night 2.6.2; 2.7.7) Comfort and advice don't help, John admits. (Night 2.7.3) It usually lasts some years, with breaks of light and peace. (Night 2.7.4)

John's main picture is a log in a fire. The fire first drives out the water. Then it blackens the wood, which smokes and smells. Finally it turns the wood into fire, glowing and giving heat as the fire does. (Night 2.10.1) The soul is the same. The light first shows up all its ugliness, so it feels worse than before. John says it is no worse; it only sees itself clearly. (Night 2.10.2)

The second stanza opens "a oscuras y segura": in darkness, and safe. John says the soul is safe because it usually goes wrong through its own cravings, tastes, and reasonings, and those are now quiet. God leads it by the hand like a blind person, to a place it doesn't know. (Night 2.16.2–3, 7) To reach a new land, he adds, you have to take roads you don't know. (Night 2.16.8)

Finally, John dresses the soul in the poem's "disguise" of three colors: a white tunic of faith, a green coat of hope, and a red robe of love. (Night 2.21.3–10) The darkness, he insists, is for the sake of light. It humbles in order to raise up, and empties in order to fill. (Night 2.9.1)

Example

An everyday analogy. Cleaning a deep cut stings more than the cut did, and the sting comes from the dirt in it. A good nurse cleans it anyway. The analogy breaks down because a cut heals back to how it was, while John says the soul ends up changed, burning like the fire.

John's own example. The log in the fire, which you can step through below. (Night 2.10.1–9)

Try it: the log in the fire

If this is God's love, why does it hurt?

John's answer: the pain comes from the soul's own weakness and impurity, the way wet wood hisses and smokes. The fire itself is good. Once the moisture is gone, the burning stops hurting, and joy remains. (Night 2.10.4–5) He even says God's hand only touches the soul. It feels heavy because the soul is so weak. (Night 2.5.7)

A common mix-upSome readers take the night of spirit as a punishment. John says God's aim in it is to grant favors. (Night 2.5.7) The person feels rejected because the light shows their faults so clearly, even though they are no worse than before. (Night 2.5.5; 2.10.2)

About the source

John of the Cross, The Ascent of Mount Carmel (written 1581 to 1585) and The Dark Night (poem 1578 or 1579; commentary 1584 to 1585). In The Collected Works of St. John of the Cross, revised edition, translated by Kieran Kavanaugh and Otilio Rodriguez, with introductions by Kieran Kavanaugh. Washington, DC: ICS Publications, 1991. Read the original.

References like (Ascent 2.13.4) mean book 2, chapter 13, paragraph 4 of The Ascent of Mount Carmel. (Night 1.9.2) means book 1, chapter 9, paragraph 2 of The Dark Night. These numbers are used in most modern editions, so you can find the passage in other translations too. "Kavanaugh's introduction" points to the editor's introductions and notes in the same volume.

What this guide simplifies or leaves out

  • It covers only these two works, and only their main line. It skips Book 3 of the Ascent, on purifying the memory and will, and gives one sentence to the long middle of Book 2, on visions, voices, and other unusual experiences.
  • It leaves out most of the second half of The Dark Night, Book 2 (chapters 11 to 25), including love's growing fire and the ten-step "ladder of love." It uses only the chapters on "in darkness, and safe" and the three-colored disguise.
  • John himself uses two outlines that don't fully match: the night in three parts, and the four nights of sense and spirit, active and passive. Kavanaugh's notes point this out. This guide shows the three parts first, then uses the four nights as the map for the rest of the course.
  • The order of parts follows a traveler through the four nights, so it moves back and forth between the two books.
  • John wrote as a Catholic friar, mainly for the friars and nuns of his reformed Carmelite order, and he reads the Bible freely to illustrate his points. This guide reports his view as his. It doesn't argue for or against it.
  • The Spanish lines and their English versions are this guide's own renderings of John's poem and verses. Everything else is paraphrase.
  • The life story in Part 1 comes from Kavanaugh's introduction. The drawing of the mountain is redrawn and simplified from John's sketch; its side-road labels, "goods of earth" and "goods of heaven," follow the sketch itself.
  • The note on depression in Part 4 comes from outside the book. John's own term is melancholia.
  • The video in Start here is a separate introduction. It isn't part of the book, and the parts of this guide follow John's text.
