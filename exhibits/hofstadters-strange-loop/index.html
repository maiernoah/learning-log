Start here

This page teaches I Am a Strange Loop by Douglas R. Hofstadter (2007). It also draws on three of his other works: The Mind's I (1981, edited with Daniel Dennett), the essay "Analogy as the Core of Cognition" (2001), and Surfaces and Essences (2013, with Emmanuel Sander). It is a study guide written in plain language, not a copy of the originals. About the source.

You say "I" all day long. Yet your brain is made of the same kinds of atoms as a rock or a puddle. So how does a lump of matter come to say "I" and mean it? Hofstadter, a cognitive scientist who has spent his life thinking about thinking, gives a bold and surprising answer.

He builds the answer from odd parts: a box of envelopes, a TV camera aimed at its own screen, a chain of dominoes, and a famous proof about numbers. This page walks you through each part, with something to try in every one.

1. How big is a soul?

In this part: see why Hofstadter treats having a soul as a matter of degree, and what he means by the word.

Words to know

  • Soul: in this book, the "someone in there" that makes a creature a who and not a what. Hofstadter strips the word of its religious meaning (SL ch. 1).
  • Huneker: a joke unit for measuring how much soul something has. A typical adult has about 100 (SL ch. 1).

The book opens at the dinner table. Hofstadter slices tomatoes without a flicker of guilt. He swats mosquitoes, tries not to step on ants, and carries other insects outside. After reading Roald Dahl's grim story "Pig" at 21, he gave up meat, slipped back, and finally became fully vegetarian while writing this very chapter (SL ch. 1).

Why tell you about his diet? Because everyone makes choices like these, meal after meal. And every such choice quietly ranks creatures by how much "someone is in there" (SL ch. 1). Staring at a pig's head in a European grocery store, the teenage Hofstadter wondered, "Who had lived there?" The same question about a tomato sounds silly (SL ch. 1).

So he proposes a scale. He names its unit the huneker, after the music critic James Huneker, who wrote that "small-souled men" should not attempt a certain fierce Chopin étude. A just-fertilized egg has essentially zero hunekers. A typical adult has about 100, and some may have more or less, the way height varies (SL ch. 1).

The key claim is that soul comes in shades. It is "a shaded, blurry numerical variable," not a light switch. It grows over years of development and can shrink, as in late Alzheimer's disease. What grows or decays is "a special kind of subtle pattern," and finding that pattern is the job of the whole book (SL ch. 1).

Hofstadter uses several words for the same thing: soul, self, "I," interiority, "a light on inside," and consciousness. To him these all name one phenomenon, and all of them come in degrees (SL ch. 1, ch. 19).

He admits the scale is risky. It can sound like some people are worth more than others, a thought that troubles him, so he declines to measure anyone. A two-year-old has a smaller soul than a twenty-year-old, he says, yet that gives no license to value the child's life less. The child's huge potential and its "cuteness" protect it (SL ch. 1).

An everyday comparison. Think of a dimmer switch instead of an on-off switch. The light can be faint, bright, or anywhere between. The comparison breaks down because a dimmer has one knob. Hofstadter's "soul" grows out of many patterns at once: memories, concepts, and a model of oneself.

The book's own example. A friend tells him she is going to see another friend's turkey. "Which, or whom?" he asks. "Which," she says: "A turkey's not a whom." Hattie, a chocolate Labrador, is "a whom, no doubt." Our pronouns already sort creatures by soul size (SL ch. 1).

Why? How does Hofstadter know people already believe in soul sizes?

He looks at what people do, not what they say. Someone may insist a mosquito could be "every bit as conscious" as a person, then swat one without a thought. He also asks a provocative question: why don't we protest at animal shelters where stray dogs are put to sleep, when we would never accept that for people? "Judge people's opinions not by their words, but by their deeds" (SL ch. 6).

A common mix-up"Soul" here means a ghostly religious substance that is either present or absent. Hofstadter uses the word with no religious meaning. For him it names a pattern in a brain, and patterns can be larger or smaller (SL ch. 1).

He returns to soul size in his last chapter. The word magnanimity (generosity) comes from Latin for "having a great soul." For Hofstadter, the more generous you are, the larger your soul, because you house so many other souls inside you (SL ch. 24).

2. Who shoves whom?

In this part: see how an abstract fact like "641 is prime" can be the best explanation of a physical event, and why Hofstadter says thinking lives at the level of patterns.

Words to know

  • Level of description: the scale at which you describe a system. A gas can be described molecule by molecule, or by its temperature and pressure.
  • Reductionism: the view that the lowest level (particles, neurons) is the only real explanation, and everything higher is shorthand.
  • Careenium: Hofstadter's imaginary pool table of tiny marbles, used as a model of a brain (SL ch. 3).

Ask brain scientists what structures a brain contains and they will list neurons, synapses, and regions of the cortex. Hofstadter wants to add abstract structures: the concept "dog," the link between "dog" and "bark," a sense of humor, and "I." Studying only the physical parts would be like reviewing a novel by analyzing its ink (SL ch. 2).

His comparison is the heart. A heart is made of billions of cells, but the big picture is that a heart is a pump. Heart surgeons think about its large structures, not the details of its cells. Likewise, the cell level is almost certainly the wrong level for explaining concepts, analogies, or consciousness (SL ch. 2).

Physics already works this way. Temperature and pressure summarize trillions of molecules, and engineers design engines without tracking a single one. Hofstadter coins matching terms for the brain. "Thinkodynamics" is the level psychologists study: choices, mistakes, and memories that pop up. "Statistical mentalics" is the averaged behavior of neurons below it (SL ch. 2).

Then comes his favorite example. Picture dominoes that spring back up after they fall, wired into a computer that tests whether a number is prime. You line up 641 dominoes as the input and tip the first one. Loops of dominoes test for divisors. If one is found, a "divisor stretch" topples. One domino in that stretch never falls (SL ch. 3).

Why not? One answer: "Because its predecessor never falls." True, but it just passes the question back one domino. The better answer: "Because 641 is prime." That answer mentions nothing physical at all, yet it explains the most. In Hofstadter's pun, 641 is "the prime mover" (SL ch. 3).

No extra force is involved. Every domino falls only because a neighbor hits it. But the arrangement of the chain makes it mirror arithmetic, so an arithmetic fact predicts what the dominoes do. Hofstadter calls this the "strange irrelevance of lower levels": the bottom level does all the work, yet its details don't matter to the outcome (SL ch. 3).

Try it: the domino computer

Run the computer on 641, then answer the question about the marked domino. Then try 642, 91, or a number of your own.

Hofstadter used this domino example long before this book, in his 1981 notes in The Mind's I. There he wrote that "an explanation that makes any sense demands higher-level concepts" (MI p. 195).

A brain on a pool table

Now imagine a frictionless pool table covered with myriad tiny magnetic marbles called "simms." They careen about forever. Some clump into big, slow clusters called "simmballs" (a pun on "symbols"). When something bumps the table's walls from outside, the simms carry the jolt inward, and the simmballs shift. Over time, the arrangement of simmballs comes to record what happened in the outside world (SL ch. 3).

Look closely and you see only simms pushing simmballs around. Now speed up time and zoom out. The simms blur into a gray soup, and you see simmballs trigger one another, following the logic of what they stand for. At that level, ideas give rise to ideas. "Who shoves whom" depends on the level you choose to look at (SL ch. 3).

The book's traffic jam. In a freeway traffic jam, honking at the car in front of you is silly. The cause is a ballgame letting out or a blizzard ahead: something at a higher level than cars. The comparison breaks down because cars have drivers with goals, while simms and dominoes have none (SL ch. 3).

From The Mind's I. In an earlier dialogue, Hofstadter imagined Aunt Hillary, an ant colony that holds witty conversations. Yet, as one character says, "all the ants in Aunt Hillary are as dumb as can be." Meaning shows up only at the level of the colony's symbols, never at the level of single ants (MI pp. 166, 180).

Why? Doesn't "641 is prime" smuggle in a force beyond physics?

No. The laws of physics move every domino. The arrangement makes the chain act out a calculation, so the result of the calculation tells you what the dominoes will do. Calling primality a cause is like calling a gas's temperature the cause of the push on a piston: a true summary that throws away the details you don't need (SL ch. 3).

A common mix-upA thought must live in one special neuron, the way a "grandmother cell" was once imagined to fire whenever you see your grandmother. Hofstadter rejects this idea. A neuron in a thought is like one voter in a national election: no single voter makes the decision. Thoughts live in large patterns (SL ch. 2, ch. 3).

3. Loops that lock in

In this part: see what feedback loops do, and why Hofstadter reserves the name "strange loop" for a special kind that crosses levels.

Words to know

  • Feedback loop: a system whose output is fed back in as its input, again and again.
  • Locking-in: what happens when a loop settles into a pattern that keeps refreshing itself (SL ch. 5).
  • Strange loop: "a paradoxical level-crossing feedback loop," in Hofstadter's words (SL ch. 8).

A toilet tank has a float on a rod. As water rises, the float shuts the valve. If the tank leaks, the float drops and the valve opens again. We say the toilet is "trying" to keep the water at a certain level. Nobody thinks it has desires, but once a system watches itself, goal words become a handy shorthand (SL ch. 4).

The more hidden the feedback, the harder that shorthand is to resist. At San Francisco's Exploratorium museum, a red spot of light darts away whenever someone reaches for it. Simple circuits drive it, yet it seems to tease people, even to have a sense of humor (SL ch. 4).

A microphone near its own loudspeaker gives the familiar screech. Each trip around the loop makes the sound louder, but the amplifier can't amplify forever. It saturates, and the loop settles at one steady, piercing pitch (SL ch. 4).

When Hofstadter first wanted to aim a store's video camera at the TV showing its picture, the salesman panicked and warned that he would "break the camera." Nothing breaks. Hofstadter thinks many people carry a deep fear of loops. The mathematician Bertrand Russell was one: after finding a paradox about "the set of all sets that don't contain themselves," he banned self-reference from his system of logic. Hofstadter calls the ban "timid" (SL ch. 4).

Pointing the camera at the screen opens a strange world. Screens nest inside screens like an endless corridor. Tilt the camera and the corridor twists into a spiral, sometimes a smooth "galaxy" (SL ch. 5). Once, his hand passed in front of the lens and left behind a pattern that kept pulsing like a heartbeat, a "self-sustaining visible memory trace" (SL ch. 5).

This is locking-in. From "the barest hint," after twenty or thirty trips around the loop, a stable pattern appears and stays, "forever refreshing itself" (SL ch. 5). To describe these shapes, Hofstadter needed new words like "corridor" and "galaxy" that nobody would have predicted from the wiring (SL ch. 5).

Try it: point the camera at the screen

Press Start, then change the zoom and twist. Wave a hand through the picture and watch what the loop does with it.

So what makes a loop strange?

A strange loop needs something more. As you go around it, each step feels like a climb to a higher level. Yet after several steps you land, to your surprise, right where you started (SL ch. 8).

M. C. Escher's famous drawing Drawing Hands shows the idea. A right hand draws a left hand, and the left hand draws the right. The step from "drawn" to "drawer" always feels like a step up: a drawer is alive, three-dimensional, and chooses what to draw. Here each hand is above the other (SL ch. 8).

But Escher's loop is only a picture, so Hofstadter calls it a fake. And video feedback, however dazzling, has nothing paradoxical in it. Camera and screen sit at the same level, passing pixels around. A real strange loop is harder to find. The classic example was discovered in 1930 by the mathematician Kurt Gödel, inside the very system Russell had built to keep loops out (SL ch. 8).

Why? If a video loop can lock in a pattern, why doesn't it get a self?

Because nothing in the loop perceives anything. The camera takes in pixels and the screen puts out pixels. No step sorts what it sees into categories like "corridor" or "galaxy"; only the human watching does that. Hofstadter puts it bluntly: the TV loop "is just a feedback loop" (SL ch. 13, ch. 14).

A common mix-upAny loop that feeds back on itself counts as a strange loop. Hofstadter's test is stricter. The loop must shift levels as it goes, like drawn to drawer, or numbers to statements about the system. Audio and video feedback never shift levels, so they fail the test (SL ch. 8).

4. Symbols and the self-symbol

In this part: see how perception turns millions of signals into a few active symbols, and how a brain ends up with a symbol for itself.

Words to know

  • Symbol (in Hofstadter's sense): a structure in your brain that becomes active whenever you think of a particular thing, like the Eiffel Tower or a penguin. He does not mean letters on paper or symbols in a dream (SL ch. 6).
  • Perception: turning a flood of tiny signals into the triggering of a few symbols. A camera only does reception: it takes in signals and triggers nothing (SL ch. 6).
  • Self-symbol: the brain's symbol for itself, its model of "me" (SL ch. 13).

Walk down an airport hallway and countless smell molecules tickle your nose. Depending on your hunger and your past, that flood might trigger just a few symbols: "sweet" and "smell," or "Cinnabon" and "nearby." That narrowing from millions of signals to a handful of symbols is what Hofstadter calls perception (SL ch. 6).

Signals also run the other way. Your expectations push outward from your symbols and meet the incoming signals halfway. The two negotiate until a pathway from raw input to meaning locks in (SL ch. 6).

Here is the key step. Any creature that can perceive its surroundings will, sooner or later, perceive itself. After all, "the one thing that is always in its environment is… itself." Self-perception is no miracle. It follows from perception the way video feedback follows from having a camera and a screen (SL ch. 6).

How rich that self-perception gets depends on the creature's stock of symbols. A mosquito may get by with categories like "potential source of food," "potential place to land," and "potential threat," or maybe none at all; Hofstadter compares its inner life to a thermostat's. A dog has a respectable set: "my dish," "leash," "the vet," "UPS truck." It also knows its paw is its own, so it has a rough model of itself (SL ch. 6).

Humans crossed a line. Our categories can be extended without limit, because concepts can nest inside other concepts. "Mother" and "father" give "parent"; "parent" gives "grandmother"; on up through "family" to "soap opera." Even "grocery store checkout stand" quietly bundles dozens of concepts, from bar codes to tabloid headlines (SL ch. 6).

We also store episodes: the time our dog got lost, the time we missed a plane by a hair (SL ch. 6). Hofstadter adds two cousins. Your "episodic projectory" holds what you expect and fear about the future. Your "episodic subjunctory" holds the what-ifs: "if only X had happened." Together they make "the endless hall of mirrors" that is an "I" (SL ch. 13).

So the self-symbol starts small and simple. Over a lifetime it grows into "the most important abstract structure residing in our brains," in Hofstadter's words. It sits in no single spot. It is spread out all over the brain, "because it has to include so much about so much," as he puts it. Chapter 13's section title says it plainly: "I Am My Brain's Most Complex Symbol" (SL ch. 13).

His library comparison. Think of a huge library. Most books sit on the shelves unread, but any one can be pulled down and opened when something calls for it. Symbols are like that: dormant most of the time, triggerable at any moment. The comparison breaks down because a library's books don't change each other, while active symbols trigger other symbols (SL ch. 6, ch. 19).

From The Mind's I. In the ant-colony dialogue, a conscious system has "symbols that represent the brain state, and they are themselves part of the very brain state which they symbolize," in one character's words. Hofstadter's notes call the result a self-symbol: "a complex subsystem that is a model of the full system" (MI pp. 182, 200).

Try it

Sort each thing by the self-model Hofstadter would say it has.

Why? Why does a richer set of symbols give a richer self?

A creature perceives itself with the same symbols it uses to perceive the world. If it has only a few crude categories, its picture of itself is crude too. A human, with symbols that nest without limit and memories stretching back decades, turns that machinery on itself and gets a self-model that is "extraordinarily deep and tangled" (SL ch. 6, ch. 15).

A common mix-upThe self-symbol is a little picture of you, stored in one spot. Hofstadter says it sits in no single place. It is spread through the brain and includes your memories, hopes, fears, and habits. He also warns against imagining "a movie screen inside the head" (SL ch. 13, ch. 17).

5. Meaning comes from mapping

In this part: see why Hofstadter says analogy runs nearly all of thinking, and how a mapping can give a sentence a second meaning its speaker never intended.

Words to know

  • Analogy: seeing one thing as "the same" as another at some level. Hofstadter counts the plain act of recognizing a cup as a cup.
  • Category (or concept): a mental grouping like "moon," "mother," or "sour grapes." Hofstadter treats categories and the symbols of part 4 as the same thing seen from two sides.
  • Mapping: a systematic match between the parts of one thing and the parts of another. Mathematicians call a perfect one an isomorphism.

Cognitive scientists often file analogy under "analogical reasoning and problem-solving," a phrase Hofstadter calls "dull and uninspiring" (Analogy essay). In his 2001 essay he says "analogy is everything, or very nearly so." Every concept you own is "a tightly packaged bundle of analogies" (Analogy essay).

His later book with the psychologist Emmanuel Sander makes the case at length. Their core claim: "without concepts there can be no thought, and without analogies there can be no concepts" (S&E Prologue). Analogies spring up in our minds "numerous times every second," almost always without our noticing (S&E Prologue).

Take Galileo. In 1610 his telescope showed dots of light circling Jupiter. He saw them as another Earth with its own Moons. Until then "the Moon" had one member. After Galileo's leap, "the concept of moon had been born" (S&E ch. 1).

Concepts grow this way all the time. A toddler's "Mommy" first means only his own mother. Then he sees another grown-up caring for a child and makes a leap: that person is a mommy too. Later still comes "mother," then "Mother Nature" and "motherboard." The authors see no sharp line between "true" mothers and metaphorical ones (S&E ch. 1).

So, they argue, categorizing and making analogies are one mechanism. Recognizing a cup's "cupness" is "no less a case of analogy-making" than recognizing a new case of sour grapes (S&E ch. 3). In the essay, perception itself is analogy: "vision takes an input of millions of retinal dots and gives an output of concepts" (Analogy essay). That is the funnel from part 4 seen from another angle.

Second meanings for free

I Am a Strange Loop uses analogy for a further job: explaining how one statement can carry two meanings. Bite a slice of cake and say "This tastes awful." Your friend knows you mean the whole cake, because the slices are all alike (SL ch. 11).

A father tells his five-year-old Andy to take off his muddy boots as Andy tramps in beside his equally muddy friend Bill. The words name only Andy, but they are aimed just as much at Bill. Here the double meaning is on purpose: the analogy lets the father speak to Bill without naming him (SL ch. 11).

The 1938 French film The Baker's Wife ends with a famous example. The baker's wife ran off with a shepherd and came home three days later. The baker greets her kindly. Then he turns on his cat, Pomponnette, who also strayed and came back the same day, and scolds her bitterly. Every word to the cat lands on the wife (SL ch. 11).

A second meaning can also arrive uninvited. Hofstadter invents a play about a strike, staged on a night when the theater's own ushers are picketing outside. When a character sneers at anyone who crosses a picket line, the insult lands on the audience, the opposite of what the actors want. Once a mapping is clear, the extra meaning is there "whether one chooses to read it or not" (SL ch. 11).

Hofstadter climbs a whole ladder of such examples. At each rung, the two sides of the mapping are more different from each other. At the top he puts a sentence about numbers that turns out, through a mapping, to talk about the very system it is written in. That is Gödel's discovery, and it is the subject of part 6. His conclusion: "all meaning is mapping-mediated" (SL ch. 11).

Note: the essay and Surfaces and Essences never mention strange loops. The bridge between Hofstadter's analogy books and his self books is drawn here and in chapter 11 of I Am a Strange Loop.

Try it: climb the ladder

Order these double meanings from the closest mapping (two nearly identical things) to the most far-flung (two wildly different things).

Why? Why would a second meaning exist even if no one intends it?

Because meaning comes from the mapping, not from the speaker's wishes. Once two situations line up reliably, a statement about one fits the other just as well. Hofstadter invents a playwright, "Y. Ted Enrustle," who furiously insists his play is only about numbers. Audiences who have seen the mapping hear it as a play about itself anyway (SL ch. 11).

A common mix-upAnalogy is a rare, special kind of thinking used by poets and geniuses. Hofstadter and Sander say the opposite. Analogies run "numerous times every second" in everyone, from a toddler calling a toy a truck to Einstein's greatest leaps. Small analogies and great ones sit on the same mountain, low slopes and high (S&E Prologue, ch. 1, ch. 2, ch. 8).

6. Gödel's strange loop

In this part: see how Kurt Gödel got a formula of pure arithmetic to say "I can't be proved," and why that makes the formula both true and unprovable.

Words to know

  • Formal system: starting statements (axioms) plus rules for shuffling symbols into new statements (theorems), with no thought about meaning.
  • PM: the short name, used by Gödel and by Hofstadter, for the formal system in Principia Mathematica (1910 to 1913), Russell and Whitehead's attempt to build all of mathematics from logic (SL ch. 9).
  • Gödel number: a whole number that encodes a string of symbols, so that statements can be treated as numbers.
  • Prim number: Hofstadter's playful name for the Gödel number of a statement that PM can prove (SL ch. 10).

Mathematicians live by what Hofstadter calls the "Mathematician's Credo." If a statement is true, there is a proof of it. If there is a proof, the statement is true. Russell and Whitehead hoped PM would make this airtight: every true fact about whole numbers would be a theorem of PM (completeness), and no false one would be (consistency) (SL ch. 9, ch. 10).

PM grows its theorems the way the Fibonacci sequence grows its numbers. Start with seeds (the axioms) and apply fixed rules over and over. Each rule moves symbols around by shape alone. The rules were designed so that true inputs always give true outputs, but whoever applies them never needs to think about meaning (SL ch. 10).

Gödel's first move was a code. Give each symbol a small code number. To encode a string, raise the first few prime numbers (2, 3, 5, 7, ...) to the codes of its symbols, in order, and multiply. If "0" has code 2 and "=" has code 6, then "0=0" becomes 22 × 36 × 52 = 72,900. Because every whole number factors into primes in only one way, you can always decode it again (SL ch. 10).

His deepest insight came next. Every rule for shuffling symbols in PM has a matching rule for calculating with their Gödel numbers. The two "move together in lock-step." So the numbers of PM's theorems form a club defined by arithmetic alone, just like the club of primes or of Fibonacci numbers. Hofstadter calls its members prim numbers. Asking "Is this number prim?" is secretly asking "Can PM prove this formula?" (SL ch. 10).

Try it: the Gödel number machine

Build a string from PM-style symbols and watch it become one number. Then decode a number back into symbols.

The formula that talks about itself

Gödel then built a formula, which Hofstadter calls KG. Read plainly, it says something about numbers: "The integer g is not prim." But g is KG's own Gödel number. Read through the code, KG says "KG is not provable in PM," or more briefly, "I am unprovable" (SL ch. 10, ch. 12).

How can a formula contain its own number? It can't hold the numeral itself, any more than an elephant fits in a matchbox. Instead it holds a short recipe for computing the number. As Hofstadter puts it, an elephant's DNA fits in a matchbox. He borrows a trick with quotation marks from the philosopher W. V. O. Quine to show how (SL ch. 10).

Why KG is true and unprovable

Suppose KG could be proved in PM. Then what KG says ("I can't be proved") would be false, so PM would prove a falsehood. Logicians take it as "an article of faith" that PM never does that. So KG can't be proved. But that is exactly what KG claims, so KG is true. KG is unprovable "not only although it is true, but worse yet, because it is true" (SL ch. 12).

So PM is incomplete: some truths about numbers slip through its net. Adding KG as a new axiom doesn't help, because Gödel's recipe at once produces a new formula of the same kind. The weakness comes from PM's strength. Any system rich enough to handle whole numbers can be made to talk about itself (SL ch. 12).

Hofstadter draws two lessons. First, a system built to talk only about numbers ended up talking about itself, without any word like "this" or "I." Second, KG's meaning determines its fate. We know KG is unprovable from what it says, without checking a single proof. He calls this downward causality, like explorers declaring a mountain unclimbable from one photo of its summit (SL ch. 11, ch. 12).

One more detail struck him. The shortest English versions of KG use the word "I": "I am not provable." He calls this "our first glimpse" of the link between Gödel's loop and a conscious self (SL ch. 10).

Why? Why couldn't Russell's ban on self-reference stop this?

Russell banned sentences that point at themselves directly. Gödel's formula never does that. On its face it is a plain statement about a number. Its second meaning comes from the mapping between numbers and formulas, which nobody can ban, because it is just arithmetic. Hofstadter tells of a mother who banned toy guns, only to have her son bite his sandwich into the shape of a pistol (SL ch. 8, ch. 11).

A common mix-upGödel proved that math is broken, or that nothing can be known for sure. Hofstadter's account says something narrower. Inside any fixed formal system rich enough for arithmetic, truth and provability come apart. Assuming PM is consistent, we can see that KG is true; PM just can't prove it (SL ch. 12).

7. I am a strange loop

In this part: put the pieces together. See how the self-symbol forms a loop that crosses levels, and why Hofstadter calls the "I" a hallucination yet treats it as real.

Words to know

  • Epiphenomenon: a large-scale effect made by many small events, like the feel of a marble that isn't there (SL ch. 7).
  • Downward causality: describing the low level as pushed around by the high level, as when "641 is prime" explains a domino (SL ch. 12).

Start with a strange box. Hofstadter once squeezed a box of about a hundred envelopes and felt a marble in the middle. He searched every envelope. No marble. At the point of each envelope's flap there are three layers of paper plus a strip of glue. Stack a hundred of them and that spot resists squeezing. It feels round, hard, and half an inch wide (SL ch. 7).

He kept the box for decades and named it "Epi," for epiphenomenon: "a large-scale illusion created by the collusion of many small and indisputably non-illusory events" (SL ch. 7). Yet the word "marble" describes the feeling better than any account of paper and glue (SL ch. 7).

Now the self. You act: you flash a smile in class, try a pun, shoot a basket. The world reacts. Millions of tiny signals bounce back, and your perception funnels them into a few symbols ("missed by a hair," "they laughed"). Those symbols update your self-symbol. The updated "I" acts again (SL ch. 13).

Round and round it goes, "moment after moment, day after day, year after year" (SL ch. 13). Like the screech of a microphone loop, the "I" "converges and stabilizes itself." By the time a child is a couple of years old, the "I" notion is "locked in" (SL ch. 13).

What makes this loop strange, and a video loop merely a feedback loop? Perception. At every pass, raw stimuli leap upward into symbols. "It is the upward leap from raw stimuli to symbols that imbues the loop with 'strangeness'" (SL ch. 13).

Try it: go around the loop

Step through one trip around the loop that makes an "I." Then show the level the loop can't see.

Two ingredients

Hofstadter names two ingredients for the strange loop of selfhood. The first is an ability: a huge, extensible set of symbols. Just as the richness of whole numbers let PM twist around and describe itself, our rich symbols let a brain model itself (SL ch. 14).

The second is an inability. We can't perceive our own neurons. We feel hopes and decisions, never the chemical squirts beneath them. This blindness "forces us to hallucinate" a split between a world of physical stuff and a world of hopes and fears. So the "I" seems to sit in the driver's seat: it is "where the causality buck stops" (SL ch. 7, ch. 13, ch. 14).

He tells an allegory to make the point. On the planet Austranius, creatures called Klüdgerot read huge PM strings only at the high, Gödelian level ("I am edible"). They can't imagine that the same strings make claims about numbers. We are in the same position. We read our brains only at the level of symbols and can barely imagine the neural level beneath them (SL ch. 14).

So is the "I" real?

Hofstadter's answers come in pairs. From the neuron level, the "I" is "a hallucination hallucinated by a hallucination" (SL ch. 22). Yet he also asks: are temperature and pressure real? Is a rainbow? If everyone had a box with a "marble" in it, and talking about marbles explained a great deal, you would believe in your own marble too (SL ch. 13).

The "I" earns its reality the way "641 is prime" did. The most efficient description of a brain, "and for most purposes, the truest way," is that ideas and desires push the microscopic stuff around (SL ch. 12). "Ultimately, the 'I' is a hallucination, and yet, paradoxically, it is the most precious thing we own" (SL ch. 21).

Why? Why can't a self-driving car with cameras pointed at itself have an "I"?

Seeing your own body isn't enough. Hofstadter discusses Stanley, a robot car that drove itself across the Nevada desert. To have an "I," Stanley would need a rich model of its own goals and its place among other cars, plus memories of its past, expectations for its future, and what-ifs about near misses. Its feedback loop guides it down a road, but it is "not strange enough" (SL ch. 13).

A common mix-upIf the "I" is a hallucination, then nothing you decide matters. Hofstadter disagrees. At the level where we live, the "I" really does cause things, just as temperature really pushes a piston. Critics might say his view turns us all into zombies. He playfully grants the label, then argues there is no real difference between such a "zombie" and us: "once you've got self, you've got consciousness" (SL ch. 13, ch. 22).

Check yourself

8. Living in each other

In this part: see why Hofstadter says one brain houses more than one "I," and what that meant to him after his wife died.

Words to know

  • Universal machine: a machine flexible enough to imitate any other machine, given the right description. Alan Turing defined the idea in 1936 (SL ch. 17).
  • Coarse-grained: low in detail, like a mosaic made of big tiles.
  • Caged-bird view: Hofstadter's name for the belief "One body, one person," or "One brain, one soul," with the skull as cage and the soul as bird (SL ch. 18).

A phone that can run any app is a universal machine. Hofstadter argues that human brains are universal in a similar way. We can copy almost any pattern we meet, including the patterns that make up other people (SL ch. 17).

Dogs can't. On a ski trip, Hofstadter and his children watched their dog Ollie on a kennel's webcam from far away. They knew there was no way to ever explain to Ollie that he had been watched (SL ch. 17).

So we build models of the people around us. At the core of each person-symbol, Hofstadter guesses, we install a stripped-down copy of our own strange loop. For the people we know best, that copy grows rich over the years and takes on a life of its own (SL ch. 15).

He pictures two video loops placed near each other. Camera A sees screen A, but also screen B, so B's loop shows up inside A's picture, smaller and grainier. Each loop is partly made out of the other. One loop still stays privileged in each head, because your own eyes and ears feed your brain directly. Other people reach it only through language, a much slower channel (SL ch. 15).

In December 1993, Hofstadter's wife Carol died suddenly of a brain tumor. She was not yet 43, and their children were five and two. One day, gazing at a photo of her taken shortly before her death, he found himself saying through tears, "That's me! That's me!" Their shared hopes for their children felt like one hope living in two brains (SL ch. 16).

His friend Daniel Dennett wrote to him, borrowing a phrase from Marvin Minsky, that Carol would "be thinking with your brain for quite some time to come." Hofstadter took this seriously. A coarse-grained copy of Carol's way of seeing the world lived on in him and in others who loved her (SL ch. 17).

He is frank about the limits. His copy of Carol is "hugely defective and incomplete." "Death's sting cannot be denied." But it is not quite total either. A dead person leaves an afterglow, like the corona around an eclipsed sun, that fades as the people who knew them die in turn (SL ch. 17).

Try it: mosaics of different grain

Hofstadter compares our pictures of each other to mosaics. Slide from a stranger to the person themselves and watch the portrait sharpen.

An everyday comparison. Think of a country and its people. Most of a nation lives inside its borders, but there are expats everywhere, and the nation's culture can survive even when its land is lost. Hofstadter uses this very image, citing Poland, whose land was swallowed up by its neighbors while the Polish people endured (SL ch. 18).

From The Mind's I. Dennett's story "Where Am I?" separates his brain, kept in a vat, from his body, which wanders far away. Where is Dennett? The story's narrator tries the slogan "Dennett is wherever he thinks he is" (MI p. 221). In Hofstadter's dialogue about a book that records Einstein's brain, a character insists that "it was the process" that mattered, not the paper (MI p. 446).

Why? If my soul lives partly in other brains, why don't I taste my sister's falafel when she eats it?

Hofstadter's own example. His sense organs feed his brain directly; hers feed his only through slow channels like a phone call. When she describes the falafel, his mouth waters, but at a delay and much weaker. He claims only blur, not full sharing: the closer two people are, "the more stuff gets copied back and forth," and the more faithful the copies (SL ch. 18).

A common mix-upHofstadter is promising an afterlife. He isn't. What survives is only a low-resolution pattern in other people's brains, and it fades. He also expected readers to suspect grief-driven wishful thinking, so he points to notes he wrote on these ideas years before Carol's death (SL ch. 16, ch. 17).

Check yourself

9. Zombies, teleporters, and free will

In this part: test Hofstadter's view against the hardest puzzles about minds, and see where he parts ways with other philosophers.

Words to know

  • Dualism: the view that consciousness is something extra, beyond physical law, that some things have and others lack (SL ch. 22).
  • Zombie (in philosophy): an imagined being that acts exactly like a conscious person but feels nothing inside.
  • Cartesian Ego: one indivisible, all-or-nothing unit of self. Hofstadter pictures it as a "precious yolk" in a "Cartesian eggshell." The philosopher Derek Parfit argued against it (SL ch. 21).

Hofstadter's short answer to "What is consciousness?" is "the dance of symbols inside the cranium." Shorter still: "consciousness is thinking" (SL ch. 19).

Skeptics ask who watches the dance. His reply: the watching is more of the same. A promising student turned down his research group, and old memories of similar letdowns bubbled up one after another. Finally one word settled out: "jilted." The brain perceives its own symbol patterns and boils them down, just as it perceives the outside world. No special inner viewer is needed, and positing one starts an endless regress of viewers inside viewers (SL ch. 19).

The philosopher David Chalmers, once Hofstadter's own doctoral student, imagines a universe identical to ours, atom for atom, but with no feelings in it. Its Chalmers gives the same lectures on consciousness yet feels nothing. Hofstadter replies that "consciousness is not a power moonroof." It is no optional extra. Just as Gödel's loop arises in any rich enough system of number theory, the loop of selfhood arises in any rich enough set of categories, "and once you've got self, you've got consciousness" (SL ch. 22).

To show what dualism looks like from outside, he invents "liphosophers," who believe every pile of leaves holds an undetectable essence called Leafpilishness. Which piles get it? How does it stick? Does a pile with an ant in it count? Dualism about consciousness, he says, raises the same endless, unanswerable questions (SL ch. 22).

The Mind's I has a story with the same point. Raymond Smullyan's "unfortunate dualist" plans to take a drug that would destroy his soul while leaving his behavior unchanged. A friend secretly injects him with it while he sleeps. The next morning he takes the drug again and complains that it didn't work: "I still obviously have a soul" (MI pp. 383–384).

Teleporters and copies

Parfit told a story about a "Teletransporter" that scans your body, destroys it, and builds an exact copy on Mars. In a second episode, a new scanner leaves the original alive on Earth with a damaged heart while the copy wakes on Mars. Hofstadter adds a third: copies built on Venus and Mars at the same instant, none left on Earth (SL ch. 21). The Mind's I opens with a similar teleporter, which might be a "murdering twinmaker" (MI pp. 3–4).

Hofstadter notices how easily the telling steers us. We happily accept the trip to Mars in the first episode, then call the Martian a fake in the second. Daniel Dennett calls such carefully crafted stories "intuition pumps" (SL ch. 21).

Try it: where are you after the teleporter?

Pick an episode and compare what two views say about where you are.

Free will

Hofstadter has no quarrel with "I did it because I wanted to." We do have wills, and our wants push us around inside a "hedge maze" of obstacles the world sets up. What he rejects is the word "free." Decisions come out of something like an inner vote among our desires, and the votes are physical events in our heads. "Our will, quite the opposite of being free, is steady and stable, like an inner gyroscope," and that stability is what keeps you you (SL ch. 23).

He ends the book with a dilemma. Either consciousness is magic outside physics, or it comes from physics, and then we must explain how a mere pattern can be someone. His answer is the strange loop: "You and I are mirages who perceive themselves." Near the very end, we are "unpredictable self-writing poems": "vague, metaphorical, ambiguous, and sometimes exceedingly beautiful" (SL Epilogue).

These debates are far from settled. Many philosophers, Chalmers among them, have defended the zombie argument. Even Parfit admitted that at some level he would always doubt his own conclusions about identity, a confession Hofstadter quotes with admiration (SL ch. 21).

Why? Why does Hofstadter compare consciousness to horsepower?

Nobody orders "racecar power" as an add-on to a two-cylinder engine. Horsepower comes automatically from how an engine is built, and it comes in amounts. Likewise, consciousness is "nothing but the upper end of a spectrum of self-perception levels that brains automatically possess as a result of their design." Mosquitoes have essentially none, dogs and two-year-olds a modest amount, adults a lot (SL ch. 22).

A common mix-upIf consciousness is physical, then a thermostat is conscious too. Hofstadter's view says it comes in degrees tied to how rich a system's symbols are. A thermostat, or a mosquito, sits at essentially zero (SL ch. 15, ch. 22).

How the references work

The copies of I Am a Strange Loop, Surfaces and Essences, and the essay used for this guide had no page numbers, so references to them give the chapter, as in (SL ch. 3). References to The Mind's I give its printed page numbers, as in (MI p. 166). Quotations are short and exact; everything else is paraphrase.

What this guide simplifies or leaves out

The Gödel number machine uses Hofstadter's sample codes for "0" (2) and "=" (6). The codes for "s" (4) and "+" (8) were chosen for this page. Gödel's real code and PM's real notation are more complicated.

The domino computer draws each divisor test as a square and stops at the square root of the number, a shortcut chosen for this page. The book only says that loops test for 2, 3, and so on. The video loop sketch only copies, shrinks, and turns the picture. A real camera adds brightness, contrast, and timing effects, and it produced patterns, like Hofstadter's pulsing "heartbeat," that the sketch can't. Hofstadter himself never fully explained the heartbeat.

The huneker numbers are Hofstadter's playful guesses, not measurements. He even gives the mosquito two different tiny values in different chapters. Several examples are his inventions. The picket-line play in part 5 (The Posh Shop Picketeers) never existed. The playwright "Y. Ted Enrustle" and the planet Austranius retell the story of Russell, Whitehead, and Gödel in disguise.

Neither Surfaces and Essences nor the analogy essay mentions strange loops. The link between Hofstadter's analogy work and his account of the self comes from chapter 11 of I Am a Strange Loop and from this guide.

Left out: most of the number theory in chapter 9 and the Twinwirld thought experiment in chapter 15. Also left out: most of the long dialogue in chapter 20, the "inverted spectrum" argument in chapter 23, and most of chapter 24 on generosity and Albert Schweitzer. From the other books, this guide leaves out nearly everything except the passages named here.

References in this guide, like (SL ch. 4) or (MI p. 195), point to the originals.
