The Practice of Asking

A training for executive directors on why people give, what you carry into every ask, and how to ask without needing the yes.

[ ◄ Noah's Weblog | About ]

Start here

This training draws on Noah Maier's working notes for a book in progress, The Fundraising Act, and on the writers those notes lean on. It is written for nonprofit executive directors. About the source.

Most fundraising training is about tactics: scripts, gift charts, databases. They help. But an executive director who dreads asking will find reasons not to use them. This training works on the dread itself. It asks what you believe giving is, what you carry about money, and who you are when you ask.

The notes state the goal in two short sentences: "That process will change you. The money is a side effect." (Welcome)

Each part ends with one practice to try during a normal week of work. The practices are where the change happens. Reading about them is the easy half.

By the end you'll be able to

  • Explain what it means to call generosity a "substrate," and what a fundraiser removes instead of adds.
  • Compare giving from overflow with giving from guilt, and say which one lasts.
  • Name the money story you carry and predict where it shows up in an ask.
  • Diagnose a hard ask by checking the four things that have to be true first.
  • Separate what you control in a donor relationship from what you don't, and decide what to release.

Guess first

Answer these before you start. Guessing wrong is fine; trying first makes the answers stick.

1. Two stories about asking

In this part: see how the story you tell about fundraising sets a ceiling on how well you can do it.

Words to knowPractice: something done again and again for what it builds in you, with no finish line.

Every profession tells a story about itself. That story decides who joins, how they act, and how high they aim. The notes call this the ceiling. (Generosity as Substrate)

The common story about fundraising is that it is a necessary evil. It is the awkward part of the job that pays for the real work. Leaders who believe this apologize for the development office. Boards treat the annual fund as an embarrassment. Talented people leave the field, and the ones who stay ask timidly. (Generosity as Substrate)

The training offers a different story. Fundraising is one of the oldest forms of human cooperation: gathering resources around a shared purpose. It is how cathedrals were built, universities endowed, hospitals founded and movements kept alive. In this story it is a necessary good. (Generosity as Substrate; What Fundraising Is and Isn't)

The notes define it plainly. Fundraising is the practice of sustained human attention directed at another person. The word practice matters. A practice is defined by repetition. You never complete it. It trains you, and the methods grow out of what it builds in you. (What Fundraising Is and Isn't)

The notes are just as hard on the opposite mistake. Fundraisers ask people for money; that is the job. Language so lofty that the ask disappears is its own kind of cowardice. What changes is orientation. In sales the transaction is the product. In fundraising the relationship is the product, and the gift is one expression of it. (What Fundraising Is and Isn't)

An example

An analogy: a friend tells you about a restaurant they love and says you should come along next time. Nobody calls that selling. It's an invitation into something they enjoy. The analogy has limits. A gift asks much more of the giver than a dinner does, and the fundraiser has a stake in the answer.

The notes' own example is a phrase they ban: "Sorry to bother you." It tells the donor you think the ask is an imposition. The notes treat the ask as an offer. Most people go through life wishing someone would ask them to be part of something that matters. You can be that someone. (What the Fundraiser Enables)

Try it

Sort these lines an executive director might say. Which story is each one telling?

Why? If the tactics are the same, why does the story matter?

Because the donor reads the story under the tactics. An ED who believes asking takes something will apologize, rush, or avoid the ask, and the donor feels it. The same words, said from the invitation story, land differently. The notes put it bluntly: you can say clumsy things in a donor meeting and recover, but you can't recover from the wrong posture entering the room. (Coaching Call: Love and Cynicism)

A common mix-up"If fundraising is a relationship, I shouldn't spoil it by asking." The notes call this a trap. The donor knows why you're talking, and is often waiting for you to ask. Holding back the ask to protect the relationship withholds intimacy from it. (Fundraising Meta-Philosophy)

Practice this weekListen for the necessary-evil story in your own words. Catch one apology before an ask ("sorry to bother you," "I hate to ask") and replace it with a sentence about what you're offering.

2. Generosity is already there

In this part: picture generosity as something already present, and the fundraiser's job as clearing what blocks it.

Words to knowSubstrate: the layer underneath that something else rests on or grows from.

The central claim of the training fits in one sentence. Generosity is the substrate: it is already present in every person, community and culture. Fundraising removes whatever stands between a person and that generosity. It doesn't manufacture it. (Generosity as Substrate)

The notes use an image. Generosity moves the way water flows downhill. Nobody pushes water. It moves when the path is clear. (Generosity as Substrate)

What blocks the path? The notes list fear, confusion and distrust. They list red tape, the sense that a gift won't matter, and the feeling that the ask is a transaction. They add the suspicion that the organization doesn't understand the donor, the memory of being asked badly before, and the drag of a busy life. Each one is specific. Each can be removed by someone paying close attention. (Generosity as Substrate)

So the notes compare the work to midwifery: the baby is coming, and your job is to help. (Generosity as Substrate) They borrow a line from John Gardner that the fundraising writer Jerold Panas embraced: the fundraiser's job is to unlock what is already there. (What the Fundraiser Enables)

There is a practical reason persuasion fails. Wealthy donors are approached by many organizations every year. Once a donor feels pushed instead of invited, you have probably lost them for good. People remember being pushed. (What Fundraising Is and Isn't)

Try it: clear the path

Each rock is one obstruction from the notes. Pick the move that removes it, and watch how far the water gets.

Rock 1 of 5

The water image has a limit, and the last rock shows it. Water has no say in where it goes, and donors do. Sometimes the only thing that clears the path is a clear ask: an amount, a date, and the reason now is the moment. Unlocking still includes asking. (Asking; Urgency)

Why? Pressure works on deadlines. Why doesn't it move gifts?

Pressure adds force in front of an obstruction without removing it. A donor who distrusts you trusts you less after a harder pitch. A donor who feels like a wallet feels it more after another ask. Urgency can help with one rock, inertia, which is why the notes call urgency a service. They mean real urgency, built into the situation: a campaign that closes in sixty days, a match that expires. Fake deadlines are manipulation, and donors see through them. The test for any move is whether it removes a specific obstruction or just pushes against it. (Urgency)

A common mix-up"Unlock, don't persuade" means waiting for donors to offer. The notes reject this. Your job is not to wait for donors to figure out the timing. More gifts are lost to politeness than to pushiness. (Urgency)

Practice this weekPick one donor who has gone quiet. Write down the single obstruction most likely in the way. Choose one move that removes it, instead of one that pushes past it.

3. What the traditions noticed

In this part: compare what religious traditions and anthropologists say about giving, including the uncomfortable parts.

Words to knowDāna, zakat and xenia: Buddhist giving, Islamic required giving, and Greek hospitality. Reciprocity: giving back for what you received. Hau: Mauss's word for the spirit of a gift.

The notes support the substrate claim with a pattern. Traditions that agree on little else all put generosity near the center. Christianity speaks of grace and agape love. Buddhism teaches dāna, giving that loosens the grip of attachment. Islam makes zakat, an annual giving of wealth, one of its Five Pillars, and the word carries the sense of purifying. (Generosity as Substrate; Universality of Generosity)

Hospitality shows up too. Ancient Greek religion made xenia, care for strangers, a sacred duty, with Zeus as the protector of travelers. Any stranger might be a god in disguise. The Norse poem Hávamál spends long passages on the duties of host and guest. (Universality of Generosity)

The Bhagavad Gita goes further and ranks kinds of giving (chapter 17). Sattvic giving is done because it is right, to a worthy person, at the right time, expecting nothing back. Rajasic giving is done for a return, or grudgingly. Tamasic giving is careless or contemptuous. In the notes' reading, the highest form is the one that most fully gets past the giver's ego. (Universality of Generosity)

From the pattern, the notes conclude that generosity is structural, not a local invention. That step is an interpretation. Shared teaching shows that people everywhere have prized giving. Whether everyone is generous underneath is a further claim, and it rests on your own experience as much as on the record.

The harder picture

Anthropology adds something less comfortable. In The Gift (1925), Marcel Mauss studied gift exchange across many societies. He found that gifts were never quite free. They carried three obligations: to give, to receive, and to give back. Gifts built alliances and status, and they could be coercive. The potlatch feasts of the Pacific Northwest, where hosts gained standing by giving wealth away, are a famous case. (Anthropology)

A review included in the notes calls this the biggest gap in the substrate argument, and suggests owning it. Gifts have always carried social weight, and that weight is how generosity has worked all along. A gala, in this reading, is a modern potlatch. (Anthropology) The practical upshot, as this page reads it: you can work with a donor's wish to be seen without resenting it.

Mauss also wrote about the hau, a Māori idea he read as the spirit of the gift. Something of the giver travels with the thing given, and that's why a gift binds people. Lewis Hyde took up the idea in his own book, also called The Gift (1983). For the notes, the lesson is practical. A donor making a real gift enters a relationship, and the fundraiser is the channel it passes through. If the fundraiser is detached from the work, the gift's spirit can't pass through, and a transaction replaces the gift. (The Easy Ask)

Try it

Sort these gifts by the Gita's three kinds. The Gita ranks them by what the giving does to the giver.

A caution, and this is the training's reading: the Gita's ranking is for your own giving and your own growth. It makes a poor tool for judging donors. Most real gifts mix motives. The notes say ego is a central motive in many large gifts and should be engaged with care. The fundraiser's work is to make the first kind easier to reach. (Book draft, ch. 3)

Why? Why would the notes welcome Mauss's darker picture?

Because it makes the argument honest and more useful. An ED who expects pure altruism will be disillusioned by the ego at every gala, and may start to resent donors. An ED who knows gifts have always carried status can work with it calmly, and still invite the donor toward something larger.

A common mix-up"If a donor wants recognition, the gift is less real." The notes disagree. Status has always traveled with gifts, and ego drives many large ones. What matters is whether the giving also moves the giver toward something beyond themselves. (Anthropology; Book draft, ch. 3)

Practice this weekRecall the most generous thing anyone has done for you. Notice what of that person came with it. That's the hau. Your donors are hoping to pass something like it through you.

4. Overflow, not obligation

In this part: compare giving from abundance with giving from guilt, and see why a gift's size is only one way to measure it.

Words to knowEudaimonia: a Greek word for a flourishing life in which nothing is lacking.

Why do people give? The notes answer that generosity is a natural overflow. A person who feels they have enough wants that surplus to mean something. Giving from that place feels like expression. Giving from guilt feels like a tax. (Abundance vs Obligation)

The Greek idea of eudaimonia helps here. It is usually translated as happiness, but the notes use it for something fuller: a fullness of being in which nothing is lacking. Happiness, in this sense, is a way of being, more than a prize collected after an achievement. From that place, the notes say, generosity is expression more than sacrifice. (Abundance vs Obligation; Happiness as Wholeness)

That's why the notes push against guilt. Their advice is direct: stop guilt-tripping donors and help them feel abundant. Generosity follows. (Philosophy Calls: Philosophy of Giving)

A second idea sharpens this. Whatever is useful is useful for the sake of something else. A hammer serves the nail, the nail serves the house, and the house serves shelter. Somewhere the chain has to end in something valued for itself. (The Usefulness Problem) The notes cite the Brazilian Indigenous writer Ailton Krenak, who put it bluntly in a book title: Life Is Not Useful. (Philosophy Calls: Philosophy of Giving) The argument is old. Aristotle opens his Nicomachean Ethics with a version of it; that link is added here, not drawn from the notes.

The donor who only asks "what's my ROI?" is stuck in that chain. The notes don't dismiss the question. Answer it well. Then help the donor notice that what they cherish most was never valuable for what it produced. Fulfillment should come through the act of giving, not later as a reward delivered by an impact report. A relationship held together only by reports lasts as long as the reports keep pleasing. (The Usefulness Problem)

The same trap catches fundraisers. When impact becomes an idol, every coffee becomes a moral calculation. The guilt never ends, because there is always a better way to spend the dollar. The notes credit effective altruism with real gains, like moving money to neglected causes. They also see this trap in it at full strength. Their answer is short: you're worthy now, and so is the donor. (The Achievement Trap; Fundraising Meta-Philosophy)

Then there's the widow. In the Gospels of Mark and Luke, Jesus watches rich people put large sums into the temple treasury. A poor widow puts in two small coins, all she had to live on. He says she gave more than all of them. Her gift did little for the temple budget. It did a great deal as an act of trust. (Fundraising Meta-Philosophy)

Try it: two givers

Set what each giver has and what share they give. Compare the two ways of measuring a gift.

Giver B

The notes take a side. A $300 gift from someone with $1,000 is the extraordinary act of generosity. A $20 million gift from someone who didn't notice it leave is transactional by comparison. A good fundraiser measures the honor of having unlocked generosity, not the dollar figure. The philosophy notes add an image: a small circle is as complete as a large one. (Unlocking Generosity; Philosophy Calls: Philosophy of Giving)

This is a claim about how to regard givers. It doesn't make the large gift unwelcome; your budget still needs it. It means the small donor is never "less than."

Why? Why would a fundraiser want a gift to cost the giver something?

Because the cost is where giving changes the giver. A gift nobody noticed leaving changed nothing in the person. The notes suggest you give money you can't quite afford yourself, enough to hold your breath before pressing send. You learn from the inside what a stretch gift feels like, and you stop protecting donors from the discomfort of generosity. (Giving Away Money)

A common mix-up"Talking about meaning means ignoring impact." The notes say answer the impact question, and answer it well. Their objection is to making impact the only measure, which leaves the donor with nothing valued for its own sake. (The Usefulness Problem; The Achievement Trap)

Practice this weekCall one small, long-time donor with no ask. The notes' version: "You've been a five-dollar donor for so long. Can you call me? This is my cell phone number. I just want to say thank you." (Book draft, ch. 7)

5. Your money story

In this part: name the story about money you carry, and see how it shows up when you ask.

Words to knowMoney story: the beliefs and feelings about money you formed early in life, usually unexamined, that shape how you ask and give.

Much of what blocks a gift sits on the fundraiser's side of the table. The notes put it as a working rule: assume the money is infinite, and treat the constraints as psychological, yours and the donor's. It's a stance to work from, not a measurement. (Fundraising Meta-Philosophy)

Start with the competition. Many EDs picture a fixed pot of philanthropy with every other nonprofit fighting over it. The notes see a different rival. The money you're asking for is mostly sitting in an index fund, or headed for a second home or an eighth vacation. Your real competitor is inertia, the default of doing nothing. A dollar that moves from passive wealth into giving usually wasn't headed to another charity. (What Fundraising Is and Isn't; Fundraising Meta-Philosophy)

Wealthy people often don't know how rich they are, either. The notes tell of a billionaire who held up two dresses in a store and realized, for the first time, that she could buy both. Wealth grows faster than identity adjusts. Part of your job is to show someone a use of their resources worthy of who they want to be. (Fundraising Meta-Philosophy)

So why does asking still feel hard? Because every fundraiser carries a money story. The notes call these stories the operating system that runs under every ask. (Your Money Story)

The stories vary. Someone who grew up with too little may flinch at a million-dollar number, because it feels unreal. Someone who grew up comfortable may treat a gift as unremarkable, because money felt easy. Someone taught that asking is rude may feel their throat close at the moment of the ask, whatever their training. The notes observe that the United States is a culture of giving but not a culture of asking. Don't ask for seconds. Don't ask for help. (Your Money Story)

None of this resolves quickly. The notes call it a lifelong project that deepens instead of ending. But a fundraiser who hasn't started it is driving with the parking brake on. (Your Money Story)

Try it: which story are you carrying?

Check any line that sounds like you. Nothing here is saved or sent anywhere.

Lines that sound like me

Why? Why would your childhood matter to a donor?

Because the donor reads your inner condition before your words. The notes borrow from the acting teacher Sanford Meisner, who taught that an audience can always tell when a feeling is manufactured. A donor can tell when confidence is covering a flinch. (The Easy Ask)

A common mix-up"My friends will resent me if I ask." The notes list this among the common myths. Your friends know your values, and many are looking for a way to be part of something meaningful. (Myths About Asking)

Practice this weekGet low-stakes reps. Ask for a discount at a place you go often. Tell a stranger what you do for a living. The notes call it training for a marathon you just learned to run, so be kind to yourself. (Book draft, ch. 10)

6. Ask from love

In this part: learn what it means to ask from love, and diagnose why a particular ask feels hard.

Words to knowWonder: staying open inside a question without steering toward an answer.

The notes give one instruction before any technique: ask from love. Before a meeting, take thirty seconds. Find something true about the person that moves you. Maybe they showed up. Maybe they're carrying something hard, or trying to do something meaningful with their life. Let yourself feel warmth for them. Then open the conversation. (Ask From Love)

This sounds soft. The notes argue it's practical. When you're on someone's wavelength, you see what you would otherwise miss. You hear what they're saying instead of what you were waiting to hear. You can tell a real no from a "not yet." You know what someone needs from you because you've stopped thinking about what you need from them. (Ask From Love)

The Catholic priest and writer Henri Nouwen gave this a spiritual footing in A Spirituality of Fundraising. As the notes summarize him, fundraising is announcing a vision that has gripped you and inviting another person to take part in it. Asked that way, the ask is a gift to the donor. Asked from fear or need, it turns into a transaction, and the donor feels it. Don't ask begrudgingly or apologetically. (The Easy Ask)

The notes add a distinction from the coach Joe Hudson: wonder versus curiosity. Curiosity wants to close the loop. It has an answer in mind and narrows toward it. Wonder keeps the loop open, the way a child looks at a turtle for the first time. Hudson warns that wonder bent even slightly toward strategy becomes creepy. The fix is to hold your agenda loosely. You're a fundraiser and you have an agenda; pretending otherwise is dishonest. (Wonder vs Curiosity)

The notes offer a quick test. When you ask a question, do you already have a rough sense of the answer you hope to hear? Then you're in curiosity. If you have no idea, and that feels exciting, you're in wonder. (Wonder vs Curiosity)

Love has a hard edge too. If you wouldn't put your own money into the work, in an amount that would cost you something, the notes say you have no business asking anyone else. Their test: if a close friend asked you, off the clock, whether to give to your organization, what would you say? (The Easy Ask)

Try it: why does this ask feel hard?

Think of one specific ask you've been putting off. Answer four questions about it.

One hard ask, four questions

These four come from the notes, which add a sharp line: when an ask is hard, the work is almost never to get better at asking. It's to find the missing piece upstream. (The Easy Ask)

Why? Three of the four are about the donor. Why is "you love the work" on the list?

Because it's the one fundraisers most often skip, since it points at them. A fundraiser without it cultivates poorly, reports dishonestly, and avoids the ask until the moment is wrong. It looks like a technique problem and gets treated as one. No script fixes it. (The Easy Ask)

A common mix-up"If the ask should feel easy, I'll wait until it does." The notes call this a permission slip for never asking. The discipline is to ask anyway, while working on whichever piece is missing. The easy ask is a direction you travel in. (The Easy Ask)

Practice this weekBefore each donor conversation, take the thirty seconds. Find one true thing about the person that moves you. Notice whether your questions change.

7. Release the outcome

In this part: separate what you control from what you don't, and practice wanting a gift without needing it.

Words to knowDetachment: doing the work fully while letting go of the result. Neediness: acting as if you need the other person's yes.

You prepare. You show up fully. You make the ask. Then you release the result. The notes call this the practice of detachment. (The Practice of Detachment)

Nearly every contemplative tradition teaches a version of it. The Bhagavad Gita says you have a right to your work, but not to its fruits (chapter 2). The Christian mystic prays and leaves the rest to God. The fundraiser's version: prepare, listen, ask, release. Your worth isn't set by whether the number moved this quarter. (The Practice of Detachment)

This is hard because institutions run on outcomes: goals, deadlines, thermometers, dashboards. The notes call the pull the grasping pattern. You notice it when you email someone because they're a peg toward your goal, not because it's right. Naming it is the practice. (Book draft, ch. 11)

Detachment has a tactical side. Drawing on Oren Klaff's book Pitch Anything, the notes call neediness the most destructive force in a donor meeting. When a donor glances at the clock, the needy fundraiser talks faster, adds data, laughs too hard, and asks "Does that make sense?" Each move says: I need you more than you need me. (Eradicating Neediness)

The notes' first rule is to want nothing. Before you walk in, decide that you want this gift, that the work deserves it, and that you don't need it. The organization will survive without it. So will you. Asking from love and asking without need turn out to be the same move. (Eradicating Neediness)

A no is information. It is rarely about you. "I don't give to buildings" and "I'm going through a divorce" are different situations behind the same word. Many early nos mean "not yet." Panas tells of a donor who cut a hospital from his will, was courted with birthday cards for three years, and left it $3 million. The notes also set a limit: after four clear, literal asks and four nos, move on. Their suggested reply to a no is wonder: "That's helpful to know. What kinds of things are feeling meaningful to you right now?" (Rejection as Information)

Holding both sides is the skill. The notes call it total commitment to the work and total surrender of the outcome. (The Practice of Detachment)

Try it

Sort each item: is it yours to hold, or yours to release?

Everything you hold gets your full effort. Everything else gets released. Notice that the ask itself is yours to hold. Only the answer is released.

Why? Why would offering to leave make a drifting donor lean back in?

The notes' rule is to say something like "I can see this might not be the right time. Why don't we pick this up another day?" The note gives two reasons. The offer triggers loss aversion: what was available is about to go away. And it shows you respect your own time and theirs. (Eradicating Neediness) This page's reading: rule one comes first for a reason. The offer only reads as respect if you really don't need the meeting.

A common mix-up"Detachment means caring less." The notes mean caring fully about the person and the work while letting go of the result. Commitment and surrender are held together. (The Practice of Detachment)

Practice this weekBefore your next ask, say to yourself: I want this, the work deserves it, and I don't need it. Afterward, send a handwritten note thanking the donor for the conversation, whatever they said. (Eradicating Neediness; Book draft, ch. 7)

8. The path that forms you

In this part: see why the training treats fundraising as a practice that changes the person doing it, and how to test whether that idea is helping you.

Words to knowFormation: change in the person doing the work. Optimization: better performance from the same person.

The notes make two spiritual claims and keep them apart. The first is about the donor: giving is itself a path. Real giving loosens attachment and connects a person to something larger. It brings a kind of joy that buying things doesn't. The donor who gives once has made a transaction. The donor who keeps giving, and wrestles with it, is on a path, and the fundraiser's job is to support it. (Fundraising as Spiritual Path)

The second claim is about you. Done seriously, the work keeps you in contact with the parts of yourself that most need attention. You must ask for money, which surfaces your stories about worth. You must take rejection without collapsing or hardening; the traditions call that equanimity. You must set your ego aside for someone else's experience. You must sit with wealthy people whose views you may dislike, and still see them clearly. And you must handle money, one of the most charged things in human life. (Fundraising as Spiritual Path)

That's why the notes separate formation from optimization. Optimization improves performance and leaves the person the same: a better script, a faster workflow. Formation changes the person. A year of daily practice paying real attention produces someone new, and that capacity reorganizes everything else. The notes' summary: train the instrument, not the technique. You are the instrument. (The Instrument; Formation)

Formation alone isn't enough. The notes warn that formation without steady activity produces beautiful, broke fundraisers, who understand donors deeply and don't talk to enough of them. Activity without formation produces burned-out grinders who hit their numbers and hate their lives. The love is the reason the activity matters, and the activity is how the love reaches anyone. (Formation and Activity)

Where this argument is weakest

The notes are candid about the risk in all this. Fundraising asks wealthy people for money. The power dynamics are real, and spiritual language can make extraction feel noble without changing it. Their test is about behavior. If the spiritual frame makes you a better listener, more honest with donors, more willing to sit with discomfort, and less attached to outcomes, it's doing its job. If it makes you feel elevated while you act from the same anxiety, it's decoration. (Fundraising as Spiritual Path)

The philosopher Byung-Chul Han names a related trap: people who exploit themselves while believing they are realizing themselves. The notes turn his warning on their own methods and ask: is this a ritual, or the performance of having a ritual? (Book draft, ch. 11)

The notes describe the result Noah hopes his coaching has in one sentence. Students don't say "he helped us raise money." They say "he changed how I think about asking." The money follows. (Book draft, ch. 10)

Why? Why would fundraising change a person more than many other jobs?

Because the work keeps touching worth, rejection, ego and money, the places most people avoid. The notes suggest asking anyone with five years in fundraising whether the job changed them personally, and they expect a yes. That's an observation from practice, not a study. You can test it on colleagues. (Fundraising as Spiritual Path)

A common mix-up"The spiritual frame makes the hard parts meaningful, so I don't need to change how I do them." The behavioral test exists for exactly this. The frame earns its place only if your behavior changes. (Fundraising as Spiritual Path)

Practice this weekWrite down why you started this work. The real reason, not the career one. Read it before your next ask. (Fundraising as Spiritual Path)

What this training simplifies or leaves out

It leaves out the craft: prospecting, qualifying, cultivation plans, events, campaigns and stewardship systems. The notes cover those at length elsewhere. This page keeps to the philosophy underneath them.

Several claims here are the notes' arguments, not research findings. That generosity is everyone's natural state, that the money is effectively unlimited, and that fundraising forms the fundraiser are positions to test against your own experience.

The notes flag their own weak points, and so does this page. Panas interviewed only successful fundraisers and generous donors, so the traits he found may be results of success instead of causes. His donors were mostly older, white and male. The material is almost entirely American. Later anthropologists dispute Mauss's reading of the hau. The notes call themselves rough and sometimes contradictory. On whether a small gift "matters more" than a large one or is "equally complete," this training gives both versions. On the donor who asks about ROI, one draft says answer it well and another says redirect; this training follows the first. The section on Mauss draws on a reviewer's suggestions included in the notes.
