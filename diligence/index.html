A client asks what you think of a nonprofit. You want more than a 990 and a rating before you answer, and you rarely have a free week to get it.

A dossier gives you the read: which entity the money reaches, what has actually changed because of the organization, how it is most likely to go wrong, and what a gift is good for. Every finding links to its source, so you can see what you can stand behind.

You get two things to work from. Advisor prep is for you alone: what to tell your client, how to raise the hard part, who else funds this and who to call. The client brief is two pages, ready to forward. The judgment stays yours.

The organization is not contacted, and you do not need to name your client.

    What is in a dossier

    1. Advisor prep. A private page for you: what to tell your client, how to raise the hard part, who else funds this, who to call. It stays out of the client PDFs.
    2. Client brief. Two pages to forward: bottom line, top risks, gift risk, questions to ask.
    3. Identity and structure. Which legal entity a gift reaches, and the affiliates around it.
    4. The money. Up to 25 years of Form 990 numbers and the named pay table, parsed from the filings.
    5. The ratios. Pay, fundraising cost, reserves, deficits and volatility, scaled to budget size and set against peers.
    6. The public record. Ratings, audits, governance answers, legal history, funders, and the organization's claims against the evidence.
    7. The results. What actually changed because of the organization, judged on significance, credit and durability, with failures beside successes.
    8. The field. Who else does this, what would be lost if the organization vanished, and whether it is the right unit to fund.
    9. The culture and the leader. How the leadership thinks about money, risk, loss and power, read from public professional material.
    10. Four futures. The two outside trends that matter most, and what each combination would do to this organization.
    11. The verdict. A health score, the likeliest ways this goes wrong, risk by type of gift, and the questions to ask before giving.

    Research agents gather and read; an editor pass makes every passage say its point. The arithmetic on filed numbers is done by code, not by a model, so every number traces to a filing.

    What it will not do

    • A character verdict on anyone. The leader read covers how a senior leader handles money, risk and criticism, from their own public professional words, with no diagnosis and nothing private.
    • Private or leaked material.
    • A recommendation to give or not give. The dossier sets out the risks, the strengths and the questions; the judgment stays yours.

    How to read one

    Findings rest on the cited public record. The few readings that are speculation appear in italics.

    Corrections

    Organizations and readers may request a correction to any factual claim. Corrections that check out are made and noted on the page.

    The fine print

    Dossiers are compiled from public records by research agents and arithmetic on filed numbers. Every claim carries its sources, and speculation is marked. Nothing in a dossier is legal, financial, tax or investment advice, and the organization assessed has not reviewed it. Verify anything you intend to act on.
