Before you give to a nonprofit you can read its 990, its annual report and a rating site. That tells you how it spends. It does not tell you what changed in the world because of it, how it is most likely to go wrong, what a gift is good for, or whether someone else already does the work better.

A dossier answers those questions in writing, with every claim tagged by how well it is supported and linked to its source. Name the organization by its EIN below. The dossier compiles on its own and arrives by email, usually within an hour, as a single password-protected page you can read anywhere, print, or save as a designed PDF.

    How a dossier is built

    1. Identity and structure. Which legal entity a gift reaches, and the affiliates around it.
    2. The money. Up to 25 years of Form 990 numbers and the named pay table, parsed from the filings.
    3. The ratios. Pay, fundraising cost, reserves, deficits and volatility, scaled to budget size and set against peers.
    4. The public record. Ratings, audits, governance answers, legal history, funders, and the organization's claims against the evidence.
    5. The results. What actually changed because of the organization, judged on significance, credit and durability, with failures beside successes.
    6. The field. Who else does this, what would be lost if the organization vanished, and whether it is the right unit to fund.
    7. The culture and the leader. How the leadership thinks about money, risk, loss and power, read from public professional material.
    8. Four futures. Two strong external trends as axes, and what each quadrant does to this organization.
    9. The verdict. Health score, pre-mortem, gift risk by gift type, and the questions to ask before giving.

    Research agents gather and read; an editor pass makes every passage say its point. The arithmetic on filed numbers is done by code, not by a model, so every number traces to a filing.

    What it will not do

    • Psychological profiles of individuals. Culture is read at the level of the organization, from public professional material only.
    • Private or leaked material.
    • A recommendation to give or not give. The dossier lays out the risks, the strengths and the questions; the decision stays yours.

    How to read one

    Every claim carries a tag. Evidence means an agent saw it in a source it fetched. Inference is a reading of evidence, and says what it rests on. Speculation is a hypothesis worth checking; those lines are blacked out until you click them. When something could not be found, the dossier says so and names what it looked for.

    Corrections

    Organizations and readers may request a correction to any factual claim. Corrections that check out are made and noted on the page.

    The fine print

    Dossiers are compiled from public records by research agents and arithmetic on filed numbers. Every claim is tagged evidence, inference or speculation and carries its sources. Nothing in a dossier is legal, financial, tax or investment advice, and the organization assessed has not reviewed it. Verify anything you intend to act on.
